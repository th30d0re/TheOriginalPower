## Claim A — RAND Gun Policy in America (Card 26)

**Claim as recorded.** Supportive evidence (RAND's strongest designation) that
child-access-prevention laws reduce firearm homicides and self-injuries among youth (2023
update); moderate evidence that background checks reduce firearm and total homicides. The
spoken line: "RAND's reviews find evidence for specific laws, like child-access prevention
and background checks."

**Status: VERIFIED.**

Child-access prevention:

- "There is now supportive evidence that child-access-prevention laws reduce firearm
  homicides and self-injuries among youth" — opening sentence, RAND press release dated
  "For Release Tuesday January 10, 2023."
- The current policy page carries the same rating with slightly different outcome labels:
  "There is supportive evidence that child-access prevention laws decrease firearm
  homicides or firearm assault injuries among young people." (Violent Crime row; page
  updated January 29, 2026.) The page also rates supportive evidence for firearm suicides
  among young people and for unintentional firearm injuries and deaths of children.

Background checks:

- "There is moderate evidence that background checks decrease total homicides and firearm
  homicides." (Violent Crime row, background-checks policy page, updated January 29,
  2026.) The analysis summary table phrases the same finding as "There is moderate
  evidence that background checks decrease firearm and total homicides."

The rating scale, in RAND's own words:

- "Evidence is categorized as inconclusive, limited, moderate, or supportive." (Each
  policy page, under "Evidence Strength.")
- "(Supportive is the project's highest level of evidence for a policy.)" (Press release,
  January 10, 2023.) The same release calls moderate "the second highest evidence rating
  used by the researchers."

Card 26's two further ratings also check out on the analysis page: "There is limited
evidence that licensing and permitting requirements decrease firearm and total homicides"
and "There is moderate evidence that waiting periods decrease total homicides."

## Claim B — NCRC gentrification report (Card 24)

**Claim as recorded.** NCRC ranked Boston third among "intensely gentrified" cities using
2013–2017 census data across more than 72,000 neighborhoods in 940 metro areas;
gentrifying tracts averaged 77% people of color; gentrification included Roxbury and
pockets of Dorchester along the I-93 corridor.

**Status: VERIFIED for the ranking, the 77% figure and its definition, the scope, and the
years. DIFFERS for the named Boston neighborhoods (attribution, see below).**

The primary document is NCRC's "Gentrification and Disinvestment 2020," which describes
itself as the follow-up to NCRC's 2019 report: "In this follow-up report, covering data
from 2012 through 2017."

Ranking:

- "San Francisco, California, took the title of most intensely gentrified city in America
  during 2013-2017, followed by Denver, Colorado, and Boston, Massachusetts." (Executive
  summary.)

Scope:

- "Nationally, out of the 72,668 neighborhoods that were evaluated, 9,743 neighborhoods
  were eligible for gentrification." (Results, national level.)
- "The current analysis confirms the pattern, with 654 of the 940 designated metro areas
  (CBSA) having no indications of gentrification" (Results). The 72,668 neighborhoods and
  the 940 metro areas confirm the card's "more than 72,000 neighborhoods in 940 metro
  areas."

The 77% figure and its definition:

- "The average minority population of the neighborhoods included in this study was 50%,
  but that figure rose to 77% in areas we determined to have gentrified." (Executive
  summary.) The 77% is a national average across all gentrifying neighborhoods in the
  study, measured as "minority population"; the same paragraph describes "gentrifying
  neighborhoods overwhelmingly populated by people of color." The card's wording matches
  both formulations.

Years:

- "In this report, we looked at newly gentrifying neighborhoods in the 2013-2017
  time-period." (Introduction.) Underlying data: five-year ACS 2008–2012 and 2013–2017.

Named neighborhoods — DIFFERS in attribution:

- The NCRC text opened here names no Boston neighborhoods; its Boston content is a
  metro-area map in appendix A-3. The list in Card 24 ("Mission Hill, Jamaica Plain,
  Fenway-Kenmore, Roxbury, East Boston, Hyde Park, and pockets of South Boston and
  Dorchester, most notably along the Interstate 93 corridor") comes from the Boston
  Globe's July 10, 2020 coverage of the NCRC data. The on-screen card
  (`Architecting_the_operation/video/cards_json/g18.json`) already attributes correctly
  with "Sources: NCRC via Boston Globe (Jul 10, 2020)"; the citations-deck paragraph
  presents the list as part of the study's own findings. The substance (Roxbury and
  pockets of Dorchester gentrified) stands on the Globe's reporting of the NCRC data.

URL: https://ncrc.org/gentrification20/ — accessed 2026-09-25.

## Claim C — G.L. c. 140, § 131M, as amended by St. 2024, c. 135 (Card 25)

**Claim as recorded.** A first offense for possessing a banned assault-style firearm
carries a fine of $1,000 to $10,000 or one to ten years in prison; a firearm lawfully
possessed on August 1, 2024 is exempt if the owner holds a valid license to carry or
license to sell and the firearm is registered and serialized.

**Status: VERIFIED.**

Penalty (subsection (d); the first-offense clause of the penalty sentence):

- "Whoever violates this section shall be punished, for a first offense, by a fine of not
  less than $1,000 nor more than $10,000 or by imprisonment for not less than 1 year nor
  more than 10 years, or by both such fine and imprisonment"
- The same sentence continues: "and for a second offense, by a fine of not less than
  $5,000 nor more than $15,000 or by imprisonment for not less than 5 years nor more than
  15 years, or by both such fine and imprisonment."

Exemption (subsection (b)):

- "Subsection (a) shall not apply to an assault-style firearm lawfully possessed within
  the commonwealth on August 1, 2024, by an owner in possession of a license to carry
  issued under section 131 or by a holder of a license to sell under section 122;
  provided, that the assault-style firearm shall be registered in accordance with section
  121B and serialized in accordance with section 121C."

State prison, house of correction, felony: the section names neither facility. It says
"imprisonment" with no place designation, and the word "felony" appears nowhere in the
section. The video's "one to ten years in prison" matches the imprisonment range; the
statute also authorizes the fine and the imprisonment together.

Current text versus the earlier version:

- Before St. 2024, c. 135 (2024 Wayback snapshot of the same malegislature.gov URL):
  "No person shall sell, offer for sale, transfer or possess an assault weapon or a large
  capacity feeding device that was not otherwise lawfully possessed on September 13,
  1994."
- The old penalty applied to "Whoever not being licensed under the provisions of section
  122 violates the provisions of this section"; the dollar and year ranges for first and
  second offenses are identical to the current text. The old section carried no
  registration/serialization proviso, no "own" or "import" verbs, and grandfathered
  September 13, 1994 where the current text grandfathers August 1, 2024.
- The live page also shows subsection (e) as amended by St. 2025, c. 14, § 45 effective
  August 5, 2025 (law-enforcement certification detail). Subsections (a) through (d) as
  quoted above are unaffected by that note.

URLs:

- https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXX/Chapter140/Section131M — accessed 2026-09-25
- https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXX/Chapter140/Section131M — pre-amendment text, accessed 2026-09-25

## Wording the primary sources do not support, with suggested corrections

1. **citations.md, Card 24 — the named Boston neighborhoods are presented as NCRC
   findings.** The NCRC report names no Boston neighborhoods; the list is the Boston
   Globe's. Suggested correction: "The Boston Globe's coverage of the NCRC data names the
   affected neighborhoods: Mission Hill, Jamaica Plain, Fenway-Kenmore, Roxbury, East
   Boston, Hyde Park, and pockets of South Boston and Dorchester along the Interstate 93
   corridor."

2. **cards_json/g18.json — the "77%" emphasis pairs with "Roxbury and Dorchester are
   among the neighborhoods affected."** The 77% is a national average across all
   gentrifying tracts in the study. Suggested detail line: "National average across
   gentrifying tracts; Roxbury and Dorchester are among the Boston neighborhoods affected
   (Globe)." Same figures, same point, correct scope.

3. **Script 07:49, Card 17, g12.json — "If you already owned and registered a rifle
   before August 1st, 2024, you keep it."** Section 131M(b) conditions the exemption on
   lawful possession *on* August 1, 2024 by an owner holding a license to carry or a
   license to sell, with registration and serialization. Suggested script line: "If you
   lawfully possessed a rifle on August 1st, 2024, and you held the license and later
   registered and serialized it, you keep it."

4. **Script 08:01 — "a first offense carries a fine of one to ten thousand dollars or one
   to ten years in prison."** The statute authorizes both penalties together. Suggested
   line: "a first offense carries a fine of one to ten thousand dollars, one to ten years
   in prison, or both." For the record: the statute designates neither state prison nor
   house of correction and never uses the word felony; the spoken "prison" is generic.

5. **citations.md, Card 25 caveat** can be upgraded: the section text was re-read
   directly on 2026-09-25 (live malegislature.gov page, plus the 2024 pre-amendment
   snapshot), so the "page fetch, not a printed copy" caveat can give way to "verified
   against the live section text, 2026-09-25."
