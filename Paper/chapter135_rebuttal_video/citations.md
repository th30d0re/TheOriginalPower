### Card 14 — Article 48, the 90-day stay, and the emergency-preamble exception
**On screen:** "MA Constitution, Article 48: a law is normally stayed 90 days if a referendum petition qualifies. An emergency preamble cancels that stay."
**Claim:** Under Article 48 of the Massachusetts Constitution, most new laws take
effect 90 days after passage, and a certified veto-referendum petition suspends
("stays") the law until voters decide. A law carrying an emergency preamble takes
effect immediately and **cannot** be stayed by a referendum petition — the referendum
can still happen later, it just doesn't pause the law in the meantime.
**Sources:**
- [Ballotpedia — Article XLVIII, Amendments to the Massachusetts Constitution](https://ballotpedia.org/Article_XLVIII,_Amendments_to_the_Massachusetts_Constitution)

### Card 16 — Grandfather clauses: the historical ones
**On screen:** "Louisiana, 1898: exempted from new voting tests anyone whose father or grandfather could vote before Jan. 1, 1867. Black registration: 130,344 → 5,320 in three years. Struck down 1915, Guinn v. United States."
**Claim:** Louisiana's 1898 constitution exempted a voter from new literacy/property
tests if he, his father, or his grandfather had been entitled to vote before
January 1, 1867 — a facially neutral cutoff that gutted Black registration (from
130,344 in 1897 to 5,320 in 1900, down 96%) while white registration fell far less
(164,088 to 125,437, down about 24%; it dipped to 74,133 in 1898 before partly
recovering). **Corrected 2026-09-24:** earlier drafts said white registration
"barely moved," which the numbers contradict. Figures come from secondary summaries
(BlackPast and other sources); the direct BlackPast fetch was blocked (403), and
Democracy Docket separately reports a ~40% white drop over two years, so the exact
white figure varies by endpoint. The script says "fell by about a quarter" (1897 to
1900). The U.S. Supreme Court struck down grandfather clauses (Maryland's and
Oklahoma's) in *Guinn v. United States* (1915), explicitly despite their being
"racially neutral on its face."
**Sources:**
- [BlackPast.org — "(1898) Louisiana Grandfather Clause"](https://blackpast.org/african-american-history/1898-louisiana-grandfather-clause/)
- [Wikipedia — Guinn v. United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guinn_v._United_States)

### Card 19 — Bruen (2022) and Massachusetts's licensing system
**On screen:** "NYSRPA v. Bruen (2022) struck down 'may issue' licensing. MA's 'good reason' standard was named directly. MA went from discretionary to shall-issue."
**Claim:** *New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen* (2022) held New

Massachusetts moved from a "may issue" system (real chief-of-police discretion) to a
"shall issue" one (issue unless the applicant is a prohibited person or found
"unsuitable").

### Card 22 — The national surge predates Bruen by two years, and MA's own bottleneck was handgun-specific
**On screen:** "Black gun buyers +58% in 2020. Bruen: June 2022 — two years later. In MA, rifles (FID) were already shall-issue; handguns (LTC) weren't, until Bruen."
**Claim:** The national surge in Black gun ownership began in 2020 (+58% among Black
buyers that year vs. 2019, per NSSF/CNN reporting), a full two years before *NYSRPA
v. Bruen* was decided (June 23, 2022). Bruen specifically invalidated Massachusetts's
discretionary "may issue" standard for the License to Carry (LTC) — required for
handguns — naming the state's "good reason" provision directly. It did **not** change
the Firearm Identification Card (FID) process, which covers rifles/shotguns and
already had no "good reason" requirement before Bruen. So the claim in the script is
specifically about handgun licensing lagging the national trend, not firearm
ownership in general.
