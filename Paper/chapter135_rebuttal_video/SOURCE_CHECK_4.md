## Item 1 — Boston Public Schools lead in drinking water
Verdict: VERIFIED (core events, April–May 2016). All sources opened are press; no primary BPS/city/MWRA document could be opened within budget.

What was found:
- April 2016: BPS tested fountains at 38 schools (the buildings with active fountains). Fountains at 4 schools — Thomas J. Kenny Elementary (Dorchester), Rafael Hernandez K-8 (Roxbury), Boston Latin Academy (Dorchester), Richard J. Murphy K-8 (Dorchester) — exceeded the Massachusetts DEP action level of 15 parts per billion (ppb) and were shut off; students got bottled water. By May 6, 2016, the district reported lead-related problems at 8 schools total; those fountains were shut off and replaced with bottled water.
- History: about 90 BPS schools had used bottled water since the late 1980s because of lead seeping from aging plumbing; bottled water cost the district $415,000 in the 2015–16 school year. Prior testing had covered only 2 schools per year. A plan launched in 2015 to restore fountains with new fixtures was halted in spring 2016 after newly installed fountains at 4 schools were turned on before testing was complete; two facilities staffers were placed on administrative leave. On June 8, 2016 the School Committee passed a Water Access Policy calling for annual testing of all water outlets (per a BPS fact sheet located but not renderable).
- Pipe/fixture replacement: press coverage records the new-fixture installation program and its 2016 pause, plus Mayor Walsh's statement about fixing old pipes. Completion numbers for pipe or fixture replacement were not found.

## Item 2 — Boston lead service lines and water
Verdict: PARTLY. Program existence, approximate count, and federal rule context are verified against opened sources. Exact remaining count, neighborhood-level clustering, and replacement completion totals were not confirmed within budget.

What was found:
- Count: BWSC officials have identified roughly 3,000 lead service lines supplying Boston homes and say there may be thousands more (as reported March 2024). MWRA's water is lead-free when it leaves the reservoirs, and BWSC's distribution mains do not add lead; lead enters through home service lines, lead solder, and brass fixtures (BWSC, primary).
- Replacement program and timeline: BWSC's free private lead service line replacement program was about one year old as of March 2024 (launch circa 2023); only about 400 property owners had used it in that first year. Federal grants finance the replacements. Completion totals and a Boston-specific end date were not found.
- Neighborhood clustering: not confirmed in any opened source. BWSC publishes a Water Service Map of reported lead services (based on visual inspections at meter installation), but no neighborhood breakdown was extracted within budget.
- EPA context: on October 8, 2024, EPA issued the final Lead and Copper Rule Improvements (LCRI), requiring drinking water systems nationwide to identify and replace lead pipes within 10 years, with more rigorous tap testing and a lower action threshold. Secondary reporting (state health-agency and industry pages in search results, unopened) puts the lowered action level at 10 ppb, down from 15 ppb, with compliance from November 1, 2027, and Federal Register publication on October 30, 2024 (89 FR 86418); these specifics were not confirmed on the opened EPA page.

## Item 3 — Lead paint, housing age, and childhood blood lead by Boston neighborhood
Verdict: PARTLY. The citywide pre-1978 housing share, the list of highest-risk Boston neighborhoods, citywide childhood blood-lead screening prevalence, Boston's status as a state-designated high-risk community, and a redlining statement from a primary state source are all verified against opened documents. Numeric pre-1978 shares per neighborhood and childhood blood-lead data by Boston neighborhood or zip code were not obtained within budget.

What was found:
- Housing age (citywide): the Boston Public Health Commission's Environmental Health Office states that 90% of Boston housing was built before 1978 (presentation dated 2014-10-20). It names the highest-risk neighborhoods for lead-paint poisoning as North Dorchester, South Dorchester, East Boston, Roxbury, Mattapan, Hyde Park, Roslindale, and Allston/Brighton, citing age of houses, housing condition, and inspection history. A slide titled "Pre 1950s Housing in Boston Neighborhoods" ranks those same neighborhoods, but its per-neighborhood values sit inside an image and were not extracted. No numeric per-neighborhood pre-1978 percentages were obtained.
- Childhood blood lead (Boston citywide, by sex): the 2019 Boston Community Health Needs Assessment (Boston CHNA-CHIP Collaborative, which includes the BPHC) reports that in 2011, 3.9% of boys under 6 screened had elevated blood lead levels, falling to 2.4% in 2015; for girls the figures are 3.0% (2011) and 2.2% (2015). Provenance caveat: see source 3 below.
- Statewide surveillance with Boston flagged: the MA DPH 2023 Annual Childhood Lead Poisoning Surveillance Report (published October 2024, data through 2023) ranks Boston 14th of 16 high-risk communities for childhood lead poisoning in 2023. Those 16 communities hold 55% of all lead-poisoned children while housing about one-third of Massachusetts children. Statewide 2023 figures: 416 children lead-poisoned (venous BLL ≥10 µg/dL), a prevalence of 2.5 per 1,000 children aged 9–47 months, down from 2.8 per 1,000 in 2022; estimated prevalence of BLL ≥5 µg/dL was 12.1 per 1,000 in 2023, down from 13.4 per 1,000 in 2022. Children in high-risk communities were 2.7 times more likely to have BLL ≥10 µg/dL; children in low-income communities were 3.3 times more likely to have elevated BLL than those in high-income communities; Black children were 1.9 times and multi-race children 4.4 times more likely than White children to have BLL ≥5 µg/dL. Massachusetts has the 4th-oldest housing stock in the country, with approximately 67% of housing units built before 1978 (2022 ACS 5-year estimates).
- Redlining statement: the MA DPH report states that historical housing policies that perpetuated segregation and limited home ownership, "such as redlining," increased lead-poisoning risk factors in Black communities, naming older housing stock, dilapidated housing, and fewer owner-occupied units. It cites Sampson & Winter 2016 (Chicago) and Moody et al. 2016 (Detroit). The statement is statewide; no Boston-specific redlining-and-lead statement was found within budget.

## Item 4 — Studies linking lead (air, water, pipes, paint) to violent crime and homicide
Verdict: PARTLY. Opened research supports associations between lead exposure and several crime outcomes. Quasi-experimental studies also report effects, conditional on their identification assumptions. The magnitude, homicide results, and contribution to historical crime declines remain contested.

What was found:
- Reyes uses policy-induced variation; Feigenbaum–Muller uses instrumental variables and water chemistry. These are quasi-experimental identification strategies with assumptions about confounding. Nevin, Mielke–Zahran, Boutwell, Stretesky–Lynch, and Wright use observational associations. Sampson–Winter 2018 makes a qualified causal argument from longitudinal observational analyses.
- Homicide, firearm crime, firearm homicide, arrests, and delinquency require separate interpretation. None of the listed studies supplies an independently identified effect on firearm homicide specifically.
- Disagreement concerns shared trends, ecological confounding, exposure proxies, cohort timing, publication selection, and effect magnitude. Time-series R² measures explained variation. Reyes's counterfactual percentage decline and Nevin's R² measure different quantities.

Sources and per-study findings (artifacts opened live on 2026-09-25):

1. Jessica Wolpaw Reyes 2007, “Environmental Policy as Social Policy? The Impact of Childhood Lead Exposure on Crime,” NBER WP 13097; journal version in The B.E. Journal of Economic Analysis & Policy 7(1). Primary scholarly research; NBER PDF opened with curl/pdftotext after browser fetch failed: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w13097/w13097.pdf — Quote: “No significant effects are found for property crime.” Design: quasi-experimental state/year panel, 50 states plus DC, crime 1985–2002, exploiting differing exposure reductions under the gasoline phase-out with state/year fixed effects. Shows: violent-crime elasticity about 0.8; implied 56% counterfactual violent-crime decline in 1992–2002. Murder estimates depend on excluding New York and DC. Does NOT show: a robust full-sample murder effect, a firearm-homicide estimate, or a randomized intervention. The 56% is a modeled percentage decline, not 56% of the observed decline. Exposure measurement and extrapolation to lower exposures limit interpretation.

Shows: Table 11 reports all-violent-crime R² 0.90 with a 23-year lag and teen unemployment; murder R² 0.60 for 1960–1998 with an 18-year lag, and 0.46 for 1900–1998 with a 21-year lag. Assault, rape, and robbery are also analyzed. Does NOT show: causal attribution of 90% of the crime decline, a separate firearm-homicide effect, or a property-crime effect. Lag selection and shared historical trends limit causal inference. PDF page-image retrieval failed; table values were read from the digital PDF text, with the violent-crime figure corroborated in Nevin 2007.

## Item 5 — Boston-specific link between lead exposure and violence or shootings
Verdict: PARTLY. A Boston census-tract study directly analyzes elevated childhood blood lead and combined violent crime. Official Boston materials also discuss redlining, lead exposure, and geographic overlap with violence. A Boston-specific causal estimate for later shootings or firearm homicide was not found.

What was found:
- Boston: Martin–Wolfe is a cross-sectional ecological analysis with a model-dependent association. The measured children's blood tests and crime records cover overlapping years. It does not follow those children into adult offending.
- Boston redlining: BPHC's 2021 report explicitly discusses segregation/redlining and lead; an EPA record of a 2018 BPHC presentation reports mapped overlap with violent crime. These support the existence of official local analyses and statements; causal mediation through lead remains untested.
- Massachusetts: Hoover and colleagues study firearm licensure and childhood lead, and separately study lead and suicide. The first examines ammunition-related exposure; the second concerns self-directed violence. Neither estimates lead's effect on interpersonal shootings.
- Searches covered the Globe, WBUR, Boston Indicators, Harvard Chan, BU, and MA DPH. Closest opened material and its limits follow.

Sources (opened live on 2026-09-25):
