So how did humans invent racism? The answer stretches across five continents,  
three thousand years of bad science, a few papal  documents, and one rebellion in Virginia that  
changed everything. And it starts in your DNA. If you are enjoying the video,  
hit that like button and let's get into it. Let's start with what your skin actually is,  
because the biology is the first thing racists  got wrong, and they got it wrong for centuries.  
Your skin color is determined by a pigment called  melanin. More melanin, darker skin. Less melanin,  
lighter skin. And the amount of melanin your  body produces is controlled by at least 150  
different genes. Not one gene. Not five. Over 150.  Sarah Tishkoff, a geneticist at the University of  
Pennsylvania, published a study in Science in  October 2017 analyzing pigmentation genetics in  
2,092 Africans from diverse populations and found  that pigmentation in Africa alone is far more  
genetically complex than anyone had previously  understood. Some of the gene variants associated  
with lighter skin in African populations are  hundreds of thousands of years old, predating  
the split between all modern human populations. And here is where this connects to the invention  
of racism. The people who built racial categories  in the 1700s and 1800s looked at skin color and  
treated it like a label, a simple switch that  sorted humans into four or five permanent  
types. The actual genetics say the opposite.  Skin color is not a label. It is a gradient.  
It changes gradually across geography, darker near  the equator, lighter near the poles, with no sharp  
boundaries anywhere. The scientific term for this  is a cline. Julian Huxley, the British biologist,  
coined the word in 1938. Human skin color  varies on a cline. It does not jump from  
one category to another at a border. It slides.  And different traits, skin color, nose shape,  
hair texture, lactose tolerance, slide along  different gradients, in different directions,  
driven by different environmental pressures.  There is no single line you can draw on a map  
that captures all human biological variation  at once. Any racial classification has to  
pick some traits and ignore others. Which  means every racial category ever created is,  
by definition, arbitrary. Someone chose which  differences to count and which ones to ignore. 
But that raises a question. If racial categories  are arbitrary, why do humans keep making them? 
The answer starts long before anyone  had a word for race. It starts with  
a glitch in the human brain. In 1970, a Polish-born British  
social psychologist named Henri Tajfel ran  an experiment at the University of Bristol  
that should disturb you. He took a group of 14  and 15 year old schoolboys, divided them into  
two groups based on nothing. Literally nothing.  In one version of the experiment, the boys were  
sorted by whether they preferred paintings by Paul  Klee or Wassily Kandinsky. In another version,  
it was a coin flip. The groups meant nothing.  The boys had no history together. No rivalry.  
No conflict. No reason to care. And then Tajfel  asked them to allocate money to anonymous members  
of their own group or the other group. They  favored their own group. Every time. They gave  
more money to boys wearing their own label. And  they did not just want their group to get more.  
They wanted the other group to get less. They  would sacrifice money for their own group just  
to widen the gap. Tajfel published the results in  the European Journal of Social Psychology in 1971  
and spent the rest of his career developing what  became Social Identity Theory. The finding was  
simple and unsettling. You do not need history,  you do not need culture, you do not need conflict.  
The mere act of being sorted into a category is  enough to trigger favoritism. The human brain  
is a sorting machine. Give it any label, any  division, and it will build an us and a them. 
Now, this is the part that connects directly to  how racism was invented. That tribal instinct,  
that us-versus-them wiring, is very old. It  predates our species. It exists in chimpanzees,  
in wolves, in almost every social animal. But here  is what it is not. It is not racial. For 290,000  
years, human in-group bias was based on kinship,  on language, on who you personally knew. Robin  
Dunbar, at the University of Oxford, published  his famous paper in 1992 in the Journal of  
Human Evolution showing that the human brain can  maintain roughly 150 stable social relationships.  
Below that number, you know everyone. You govern  by gossip and reputation. Your "us" is your band,  
your family, the people who share your fire. Your  "them" is the strangers over the hill. You might  
fear them. You might fight them. But you are not  sorting them by the color of their skin. You are  
sorting them by whether you recognize their face. The archaeological evidence confirms this. At  
Jebel Sahaba in Sudan, a cemetery excavated  by Fred Wendorf in the 1960s contained 59  
skeletons dating to roughly 13,000 years ago,  at least 24 showing signs of violent death. A  
re-analysis by Isabelle Crevecoeur, published in  Scientific Reports in 2021, confirmed these were  
victims of repeated inter-group violence over  Nile resources during drought. At Nataruk, near  
Lake Turkana in Kenya, a team led by Marta Mirazon  Lahr of Cambridge University published findings  
in Nature in 2016 describing 27 individuals,  roughly 10,000 years old, with skulls caved in  
by blunt force and obsidian arrow tips lodged  in their bones. Men, women, and at least one  
pregnant woman. This was not a duel. This was a  massacre. But nothing about the evidence suggests  
it was racial. These were territorial disputes.  Resource conflicts. Group against group. The  
violence was real. The concept of race was not. So when did people start noticing skin color? 
The honest answer is: they noticed it long  before they weaponized it. Ancient Egyptians  
painted people of different skin tones on their  tomb walls. The Book of Gates, found in the tomb  
of Pharaoh Seti I, who ruled from 1294 to 1279  BC, depicts four groups of people. Egyptians  
painted reddish-brown. Nubians painted dark  brown. Levantines painted yellowish. Libyans  
painted light-skinned. Egyptologist Frank Yurco,  writing in Biblical Archaeology Review in 1989,  
argued these were geographic categories, not a  racial hierarchy. And the proof is in Egypt's  
own history. The 25th Dynasty, roughly 747  to 656 BC, was ruled by kings from Kush,  
in modern-day Sudan. Pharaoh Taharqa, a Nubian  king, ruled all of Egypt. That does not happen in  
a society organized around skin-color hierarchy.  Egypt was ethnocentric. They thought Egyptian  
culture was superior to everyone else's. But  they did not think dark skin made you inferior.  
They put a dark-skinned man on the throne. The Greeks noticed physical differences too.  
Herodotus, writing around 440 BC, described the  Ethiopians as "the tallest and most beautiful of  
all men." That is not the language of someone  who thinks dark skin is a defect. Hippocrates,  
around 400 BC, proposed a climate theory in his  treatise Airs, Waters, and Places. He argued that  
climate shaped both appearance and temperament.  Hot climates produced dark-skinned people. Cold  
climates produced light-skinned people.  And here is the part that matters for the  
invention of racism. Hippocrates believed that  if you moved someone to a different climate,  
their descendants would change. The differences  were environmental. Temporary. Not permanent.  
Not biological. Not written into the soul. But Aristotle opened a door that would cause  
enormous damage later. In Politics, written  around 350 BC, he argued that some people  
are "natural slaves," suited by nature to be  ruled. He applied this primarily to non-Greeks,  
people he called barbaroi, a word that originally  just meant "non-Greek-speaking," imitating the  
babble of foreign languages. Historian  Benjamin Isaac, in his 2004 book The  
Invention of Racism in Classical Antiquity,  published by Princeton University Press,  
calls this "proto-racism." It linked geography  to character. It suggested some people were born  
to serve. But it was not yet modern racism,  because it was based on culture and climate,  
not on an unchangeable biological hierarchy.  That invention was still two thousand years away. 
Think about that for a second. From the time  Aristotle wrote Politics to the time someone  
actually built a biological racial hierarchy,  two thousand years passed. The idea was not  
sitting there waiting to be discovered. It had to  be constructed. And it was constructed for a very  
specific reason. Money. 
Let's skip forward. It is 1441 AD.  Portuguese explorer Antao Goncalves  
captures the first Africans and brings  them back to Portugal as slaves. In 1444,  
the first large-scale slave auction in Europe  takes place at Lagos, Portugal. The chronicler  
Gomes Eanes de Zurara describes the event in his  Chronicle of the Discovery and Conquest of Guinea,  
completed in 1453. He writes about the enslaved  Africans with a mix of pity and a justification  
you will hear repeated for four hundred years:  their enslavement serves their spiritual  
salvation by bringing them to Christianity. And the Church made it official. On June 18,  
1452, Pope Nicholas V issued a document called  Dum Diversas to King Afonso V of Portugal.  
It authorized the king to, and this is a direct  quote, "invade, search out, capture, vanquish,  
and subdue all Saracens and pagans whatsoever"  and "reduce their persons to perpetual slavery."  
Three years later, on January 8, 1455, the  same Pope issued Romanus Pontifex, extending  
and confirming those rights along the entire  West African coast. The head of the Christian  
Church signed two documents giving one country  blanket permission to enslave non-Christians.  
That is not ancient prejudice expressing itself.  That is institutional infrastructure being built. 
And the question of whether colonized peoples  were even fully human was debated openly. In 1550,  
at the Colegio de San Gregorio in Valladolid,  Spain, King Charles I convened a formal debate on  
the rights of Indigenous peoples in the Americas.  On one side, Juan Gines de Sepulveda, a humanist  
scholar, argued that Indigenous peoples were  "natural slaves," invoking Aristotle's concept  
from Politics, incapable of self-governance, and  that Spanish conquest was a moral duty. On the  
other side, Bartolome de las Casas, a Dominican  friar, argued that Indigenous peoples were fully  
rational human beings with souls and rights.  No official verdict was ever issued. But the  
fact that it happened at all tells you something  about the 1500s. The question of who counts as  
fully human was not settled. It was being actively  constructed. In real time. By powerful men in a  
room, arguing about whether entire populations  of people deserved to exist as free beings.  
And that debate was not about skin color. It  was about religion, civilization, and profit.  
The racial framework had not been built yet. But here is the thing most people miss about  
the early slave trade. The Portuguese  did not initially enslave Africans  
because they were Black. They enslaved them  because they were available, non-Christian,  
and far from anyone who could stop it. The racial  justification came later. It had to. Because as  
the Atlantic slave trade grew into one of the  largest forced migrations in human history,  
the people profiting from it needed an explanation  for why this was acceptable. You cannot build an  
economy on human trafficking and then look at  yourself in the mirror every morning without a  
story that makes it someone else's fault. Race was that story. 
In Spain, they were already practicing something  similar. In 1449, the city of Toledo enacted the  
Sentencia-Estatuto, the first limpieza de sangre  law. Purity of blood. It barred Jewish converts to  
Christianity from holding public office, not  because of their religion, they had already  
converted, but because of their ancestry. Their  blood was considered permanently tainted. That  
is a critical shift. Religious prejudice says  "change your beliefs and we will accept you."  
Blood purity says "nothing you do will ever be  enough, because the problem is in your body." That  
logic, the logic of the body, is the engine that  drives modern racism. And it was invented in the  
1400s to solve a political problem in Toledo. Now here is where science became the weapon. 
In April 1684, a French physician named Francois  Bernier published an article in the Journal des  
Scavans titled "New Division of the Earth by the  Different Species or Races of Men Who Inhabit It."  
It was the first published attempt to  classify all of humanity into distinct  
racial groups based on physical appearance.  Bernier proposed four or five categories:  
Europeans, sub-Saharan Africans, East Asians,  and the Sami people of northern Scandinavia.  
For the first time, a European intellectual  was arguing that humanity could be sorted into  
fundamental physical types. Not by language.  Not by religion. Not by culture. By bodies. 
Fifty years later, Carl Linnaeus, the Swedish  botanist who created the system we still use to  
classify all living things, published the tenth  edition of Systema Naturae in 1758 and divided  
Homo sapiens into four varieties. And this is  where it gets ugly. He did not just describe  
physical differences. He assigned personality  traits. Homo sapiens europaeus: "White,  
sanguine, muscular. Gentle, acute, inventive.  Governed by laws." Homo sapiens afer: "Black,  
phlegmatic, relaxed. Crafty, indolent, negligent.  Governed by caprice." He took a classification  
system designed for plants and animals, embedded  moral judgments inside it, and handed the entire  
package to the world as science. That is not  observation. That is ideology wearing a lab coat. 
Then came Johann Friedrich Blumenbach. In 1795,  in the third edition of his thesis On the Natural  
Variety of Mankind, Blumenbach proposed five  human races and invented a word you have heard  
a thousand times. Caucasian. He based the term  on a skull from a Georgian woman found in the  
Caucasus Mountains. He considered it the most  beautiful skull he had ever seen and concluded  
that the Caucasus must have been the original  homeland of humanity, from which all other races  
had "degenerated." The irony is that Blumenbach  was actually less racist than most of his peers.  
He collected writings by people of African descent  to demonstrate their intellectual abilities. But  
his framework, the idea that white Europeans were  the original and everyone else was a degraded  
version, was picked up by people far less careful  than he was. And it became the architecture of  
scientific racism for the next two centuries. Samuel George Morton, a Philadelphia physician,  
took that architecture and filled it with skulls.  By the 1830s and 1840s, Morton had collected over  
1,000 human crania. He filled them with lead  shot, measured their volume, and published his  
findings in Crania Americana in 1839 and Crania  Aegyptiaca in 1844. His conclusion: Caucasians  
had the largest brains, Africans had the smallest,  and brain size determined intelligence. In 1981,  
Stephen Jay Gould re-analyzed Morton's data in The  Mismeasure of Man and accused him of unconscious  
bias. In 2011, Jason Lewis and colleagues at  the University of Pennsylvania re-measured many  
of Morton's original skulls and published  their findings in PLoS Biology. They found  
Morton's measurements were largely accurate.  But they also emphasized the point that sinks  
Morton's entire project. Cranial capacity does  not correlate with intelligence. You can measure  
every skull on Earth with perfect accuracy  and the numbers still tell you nothing about  
who is smarter than whom. The measurements  were right. The conclusion was nonsense. 
And then Arthur de Gobineau, a French aristocrat,  published his Essay on the Inequality of the  
Human Races between 1853 and 1855 and dropped the  pretense of scientific caution entirely. Gobineau  
argued that the white "Aryan" race was superior to  all others, that racial mixing caused the fall of  
civilizations, and that every great civilization  in history had been founded by whites. His work  
was largely ignored in France. But in Germany, it  was championed by Richard Wagner and his circle.  
It influenced Houston Stewart Chamberlain's 1899  book The Foundations of the Nineteenth Century.  
And that book influenced the ideology  of Nazi Germany. A direct line from a  
French aristocrat's racial fantasy to the gas  chambers of Auschwitz. Ideas have consequences. 
But the most revealing moment in  the invention of racism did not  
happen in a laboratory or a library. It  happened in a tobacco field in Virginia. 
In 1676, a man named Nathaniel Bacon led a  rebellion. Poor white indentured servants  
and enslaved Black laborers fought side by side  against the colonial governor. Bacon's Rebellion  
terrified the planter class, because it showed  that poor whites and Black slaves could unite  
against the people exploiting both of them. And  the solution was racial law. After the rebellion,  
Virginia's legislature systematically drove  a wedge between poor whites and Black people.  
In 1691, Virginia banned interracial marriage.  In 1705, the Virginia Slave Codes consolidated  
racial laws, defining all non-Christian servants  brought into the colony as permanent slaves and  
granting poor whites legal superiority over  all Black people, regardless of wealth. The  
planter elite gave poor whites just enough  status to stop them from allying with the  
people they actually had the most in common with. Historian Barbara Fields, at Columbia University,  
put it plainly in a 1990 essay in New Left  Review. Racism was invented to justify slavery,  
not the other way around. The exploitation  came first. The ideology came second. The  
plantation owners needed cheap labor. They  got it from Africa. They needed a story that  
explained why this was acceptable. They  built one. And they built it so well that  
four hundred years later, most people  still think the categories are natural. 
Thomas Jefferson understood this contradiction  and lived inside it. In Notes on the State of  
Virginia, published in 1785, he wrote: "I  advance it therefore as a suspicion only,  
that the blacks, whether originally a distinct  race, or made distinct by time and circumstances,  
are inferior to the whites in the endowments both  of body and mind." He also wrote that slavery was  
morally wrong. He said he supported eventual  emancipation. And he enslaved over 600 people  
during his lifetime. DNA evidence, published in  Nature in 1998 by Eugene Foster and colleagues,  
confirmed that Jefferson almost certainly fathered  children with Sally Hemings, a woman he owned,  
who was also the half-sister of his deceased  wife. The man who wrote "all men are created  
equal" needed a racial ideology to explain why he  kept human beings as property. That is what racism  
does. It turns a contradiction into a system. And then, in the twentieth century,  
science finally caught up with what  should have been obvious all along. 
In 1912, Franz Boas, a German-born  anthropologist at Columbia University,  
published a study that pulled the first thread. He  had measured the head shapes of 17,821 immigrants  
and their American-born children in New York  City. He found that children born in America  
had different skull shapes than their parents  from Europe. Round-headed Eastern European  
Jewish immigrants had children with longer  heads. Long-headed Sicilian immigrants had  
children with rounder heads. The supposedly fixed,  permanent, racial marker of skull shape changed in  
a single generation based on environment.  The foundation of racial science cracked. 
And here is something that shows you exactly how  deep the constructed categories go. In the 1940s,  
psychologists Kenneth and Mamie Clark gave  Black children aged 3 to 7 a simple choice:  
a white doll or a Black doll. A majority  of the Black children preferred the white  
doll. They called it "nice" and "good." They  called the Black doll "bad." Children who had  
barely learned to speak had already absorbed the  racial hierarchy that adults had spent centuries  
building. The Clarks published their findings in  the Journal of Social Psychology in 1947, and the  
research was cited in the 1954 Brown v. Board  of Education Supreme Court decision as evidence  
that segregation psychologically harmed Black  children. Think about what that means for the  
invention of racism. A system invented by adults  for economic reasons had burrowed so deep into  
the culture that three-year-olds were reproducing  it without anyone having to teach them directly.  
That is not biology. That is engineering. Richard Lewontin, a geneticist at Harvard,  
drove a truck through the crack in 1972. In  a paper called "The Apportionment of Human  
Diversity," published in Evolutionary Biology,  Lewontin analyzed 17 genetic markers across  
populations worldwide. He found that 85.4 percent  of all human genetic variation exists within  
local populations, within a single ethnic group.  Only 6.3 percent of variation exists between the  
major racial categories. Pick two random people  from the same race and the genetic difference  
between them is nearly as large as the difference  between two random people from different races.  
The categories are not capturing real biological  boundaries. They are drawing lines on a gradient. 
The Human Genome Project confirmed it. When the  project was declared complete on April 14, 2003,  
the findings were unambiguous. All humans share  roughly 99.9 percent of their DNA. Craig Venter,  
who led the private genome effort, said  it directly: "The concept of race has no  
genetic or scientific basis." And in 2002,  Noah Rosenberg and colleagues published a  
study in Science analyzing 377 genetic markers in  1,056 individuals from 52 populations worldwide.  
Within-population variation accounted for 93  to 95 percent of total genetic variation. The  
boundaries between supposed racial groups were  not sharp lines. They were wide, blurry gradients  
that overlapped across entire continents. And the skin color question that started  
this whole mess was answered definitively. Nina  Jablonski and George Chaplin published a study  
in the Journal of Human Evolution in 2000 showing  that human skin color correlates almost perfectly  
with ultraviolet radiation levels at different  latitudes. Dark skin evolved near the equator to  
protect folate from UV damage. Light skin evolved  at higher latitudes to allow enough UV through  
for vitamin D synthesis. In 2005, Keith Cheng at  Penn State identified the SLC24A5 gene variant  
responsible for roughly 25 to 38 percent of the  pigmentation difference between Europeans and  
West Africans. And here is the fact that should  rewrite everything. DNA from a 7,000-year-old  
skeleton found at La Brana cave in Spain, analyzed  by Carles Lalueza-Fox and published in Nature in  
2014, showed that this ancient European had dark  skin and blue eyes. In 2018, analysis of Cheddar  
Man, a 10,000-year-old skeleton from England,  showed dark to dark-brown skin and blue eyes.  
Light skin in Europe is roughly 10,000 years  old. The people who built the racial categories  
of the 1700s thought skin color was ancient and  permanent. It is recent. And it is still changing. 
So here is the answer. How  did humans invent racism? 
They did it in stages. First, the human brain  evolved a tribal instinct, an us-versus-them  
sorting mechanism that helped small groups  of hunter-gatherers survive by favoring the  
familiar and fearing the stranger. That instinct  is real. It is old. And it is not racial. For  
290,000 years, it operated on kinship, language,  and personal recognition, not on skin color. 
Then, ancient civilizations noticed physical  differences between peoples and explained them  
through climate theory, the idea that geography  shaped the body. Greeks, Romans, Egyptians,  
and Islamic scholars all described human  variation, but they treated it as environmental,  
not as a permanent biological hierarchy. Aristotle  introduced the concept of "natural slavery," but  
even that was rooted in culture, not biology. Then, starting in the 1400s, the Atlantic slave  
trade created an economic system that required  millions of human beings to be treated as  
property. The people profiting from that system  needed a justification that went beyond religion,  
because enslaved people could convert, and beyond  culture, because culture can change. They needed  
something permanent. Something written into  the body. So they invented biological race.  
They hired scientists to measure skulls  and rank civilizations. They passed laws  
that turned skin color into legal status. They  built a theology that reinterpreted scripture  
to justify bondage. And they constructed a world  in which the categories felt so natural that most  
people forgot they were constructed at all. Was racism inevitable? The tribal instinct,  
the tendency to favor the familiar, that  appears to be hardwired. The probability  
that humans would develop some form of in-group  bias is essentially 100 percent. But the specific  
invention of biological racism, the idea that  skin color determines intelligence, character,  
and human worth, that was not inevitable.  It was a product of specific historical,  
economic, and political conditions, primarily the  Atlantic slave trade and European colonialism.  
The probability that this particular form  of racism would have emerged without those  
economic structures is low, roughly 10 to 15  percent. Other civilizations had prejudice,  
ethnocentrism, and caste systems. But none of them  independently produced the systematic biological  
racial hierarchy that Europe exported to the world  starting in the 1600s. Racism in its modern form  
was not discovered. It was manufactured.  By specific people. For specific reasons.  
And the receipts are in the historical record. The American Anthropological Association said  
it plainly in their 1998 Statement on  Race: "Present-day inequalities between  
so-called racial groups are not consequences  of their biological inheritance but products of  
historical and contemporary social, economic,  educational, and political circumstances." 
We built an entire global system of hierarchy  around a vitamin D problem. We measured skulls  
and wrote laws and fought wars and enslaved  millions of human beings over a trait controlled  
by 150 genes that changes on a gradient with the  angle of the sun. And every genome sequenced,  
every skeleton analyzed, every study published,  says the same thing. The categories were invented.  
The science was manufactured. And the system built  on top of them was designed, from the beginning,  
to benefit the people who built it. Tonight, wherever you are, your skin is  
doing exactly what it evolved to do. Regulating  vitamin D. Protecting folate. Responding to the  
latitude your ancestors lived at for thousands  of years. It is doing chemistry. It has never,  
not once, determined your intelligence, your  character, or your worth as a human being. The  
only reason anyone ever thought it did is because  five hundred years ago, some very powerful people  
needed a story to justify a very profitable crime.  And they told that story so well, and for so long,  
that it started to feel like the truth. It was never the truth. It was always an  
invention. And that might be the most important  thing about racism. If someone built it,
