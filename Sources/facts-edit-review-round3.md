## R1 — Line 12631

### Verdict: REJECT AS WRITTEN

### What Mitchell directly supports

Printed p. 191 places the disputed words inside a paragraph about the Haitian incident. The paragraph identifies Haitian rebels aboard *Crête-à-Pierrot*, their seizure of weapons from a German steamship, Wilhelm’s dispatch of *Panther*, and the sinking of the Haitian vessel. It then attributes “illegal and excessive” to John Bassett Moore. The antecedent of “the sinking” is therefore the sinking of *Crête-à-Pierrot*. It is not the later Venezuela blockade or the bombardment of Fort San Carlos.

The sentence continues beyond the previously visible search snippet. Mitchell says that Moore’s judgment bestirred neither the State Department nor the press. She immediately gives the *New York Times* “housecleaning” sentence. Note 21 cites:

- Moore to Alvey A. Adee, 15 September 1902, John Hay Papers, Library of Congress;
- *New York Times*, 11 September 1902;
- German archival material for the Kaiser’s reaction and the *Panther* report.

The restoration correctly reverses the prior second-opinion finding. The phrase concerns Haiti, and Mitchell attributes it to Moore rather than to German advisers.

### What exceeds Mitchell

The clause “no alarm spread through the American government or press” is broader than the Haitian evidence on p. 191. Mitchell names the **State Department** and the press. The phrase about no ripple of alarm through the **US government or press** begins the next paragraph and concerns Britain’s separate hoisting of the Union Jack on Patos Island. R1 appears to transfer that broader formulation from Patos to the Haitian sinking.

“The Department acted on none of it” is a defensible paraphrase of Mitchell’s statement that Moore’s judgment did not bestir the State Department. “No alarm spread through the American government” remains an inference because it expands one department to the federal government as a whole.

The next sentence calls the sinking “a formally illegal military action.” Mitchell establishes Moore’s legal judgment. The article does not identify a court, arbitral body, official departmental ruling, or other formal adjudication. An adviser’s opinion does not by itself establish formal illegality. The cited material therefore does not support “formally illegal.”

“The Western imperial consensus against Haitian autonomy” is an interpretive thesis. Mitchell’s paragraph documents State Department and press non-response and one supportive *New York Times* statement. It does not establish a general Western consensus.

### Moore’s institutional description

Mitchell calls Moore the State Department’s “legal adviser.” The official State Department chronology shows that Moore’s appointed Assistant Secretary service ended in 1898 and his Counselor appointment began in 1913. He held no listed appointed Department office in 1902. His 15 September 1902 advice to Adee may have been functional or ad hoc. The wording “the Department’s own legal adviser” can imply a formal office that the official chronology does not establish.

### Provenance

- Moore attribution and Department/press non-response: **scholarly secondary**, verified in Mitchell’s article reproduction at printed p. 191.
- Moore-to-Adee letter: **primary/contemporaneous, UNVERIFIED** because the cited archival item could not be opened remotely.
- *New York Times* sentence: **scholarly-secondary transmission of a contemporaneous source**. Mitchell cites the date; the original issue image was not opened.
- Entire-American-government non-alarm: **inference**.
- Formal illegality: **unsourced as a formal determination**.
- Western imperial consensus: **analytic inference**.

### Required disposition

R1 should not land in its present form. A supported formulation would stay within Mitchell’s named institutions: Moore judged the Haitian sinking illegal and excessive; his judgment bestirred neither the State Department nor the press; the *New York Times* supplied the quoted response. Any claim about the entire American government, a formal legal determination, or a wider Western consensus requires separate evidence or clear analytic labeling.

## R2 — Line 12717

### Verdict: ACCEPT THE RESTORED FACTUAL CORE, WITH A ROLE-DESCRIPTION CAUTION

Mitchell directly supports both operative points in the restored sentence:

1. Moore called the Haitian sinking “illegal and excessive.”
2. His judgment produced no action by the State Department.

The second point is a close paraphrase of Mitchell’s explicit statement that the judgment bestirred neither the State Department nor the press. R2 does not broaden the institution to the entire American government.

The description of Moore as the Department’s “own legal adviser” carries the institutional-status ambiguity described under R1. Mitchell uses “legal adviser,” but the official chronology does not show Moore holding an appointed Department office in 1902. The citation supports the description at the scholarly-secondary level; it has not been independently confirmed from the Moore-to-Adee letter or an appointment record.

The sentences following R2 call the legal violation “total and public.” That is an analytic conclusion. Mitchell documents Moore’s opinion and Departmental non-response. She does not document a formal adjudication or quantify the violation as “total.” The restored Moore sentence cannot serve as proof of those stronger propositions without explicit analytic framing.

### Provenance

- Restored Moore judgment and State Department non-response: **scholarly secondary, verified**.
- Underlying Moore letter and exact advisory capacity: **primary/contemporaneous, UNVERIFIED**.

## R3 — Line 12631

### Verdict: REJECT THE EXACT WORDING; ACCEPT THE FACTUAL CORE

Fluehr-Lobban directly supports the location, order, and death year:

- Printed p. 102 states that Firmin’s presidential bid ended in his exile in St Thomas, ordered by President Nord Alexis.
- Printed p. 107 states that Firmin died in 1911 at age 61 while in exile in St. Thomas.
- Printed p. 101 independently places him in exile in St Thomas around 1906.
- Printed p. 95 identifies 1911 as the year of his death.

The source does not say that Firmin **fled**. It describes an exile ordered by Nord Alexis. “Fled into exile … ordered by Nord Alexis” combines voluntary flight with an imposed order and creates an avoidable ambiguity about the event. The citation supports “went into exile,” “was sent into exile,” or “was exiled,” subject to the manuscript’s preferred syntax. It does not support “fled.”

### Provenance

- Exile in St Thomas ordered by Nord Alexis: **scholarly secondary, verified from the official page image**.
- Death in 1911 while in exile in St Thomas: **scholarly secondary, verified from the official page image**.
- “Fled”: **unsourced in the cited article**.
