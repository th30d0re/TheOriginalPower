# What drives the heterosexual orgasm gap?

Literature review completed 12 October 2026. This is a targeted narrative review using web search, publisher pages, abstracts, and fetched full papers. It is not an exhaustive systematic review. Publication years identify studies below; collection years are stated where verified. **For every study without an explicit collection date, the collection year was not confirmed in the inspected material.** Missing country information is identified separately. No numerical finding below relies on secondary media coverage.

Evidence strength refers to support for the specified mechanism: **strong** = convergent direct evidence; **moderate** = relevant observational or mixed-method evidence with important limitations; **weak** = indirect evidence or an incompletely tested causal pathway; **none** = no direct support found. Satisfaction, orgasm frequency, perceived entitlement, and sexual intentions are separate outcomes. A cross-sectional association cannot establish the direction of causation.

The evidence supports contributions from sexual practices, communication, and pleasure priorities. The proposed causal sequence—non-sexual compensation before sex → male self-centering → reduced female orgasm—remains untested in the sources located. The studies below also provide substantial qualifications to explanations based entirely on ignorance, entitlement, or stable personality categories.

## Q1. Do men overestimate their partners’ orgasms?

- **Direct couple comparison:** Wetzel, Cultice, and Sanchez (2022) studied **104 mixed-sex couples**, recruited online through Qualtrics; mean age **43.9**, **94.2% heterosexual**, **79.3% White**. Men significantly underestimated the within-couple orgasm gap. Women's orgasm frequency also predicted men's expectations for their partners' orgasms. The accessible abstract does not supply an individual misclassification rate; country was not confirmed. **Artifact:** opened publisher abstract. [DOI: 10.1007/s11199-022-01280-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11199-022-01280-7).
- **Direct comparison in a restricted population:** von Sydow (2002), **30 German couples**, assessed **seven months postpartum**, found that men overestimated their partners' sexual enjoyment, particularly orgasm during intercourse. Postpartum findings have limited generalizability. **Artifact:** indexed primary abstract, PubMed. [DOI: 10.3109/01674820209074667](https://doi.org/10.3109/01674820209074667).
- **Large unmatched comparison:** Frederick et al. (2018; sample in Q3) found **73%** of heterosexual men said their partners usually/always orgasmed; **65%** of heterosexual women reported that frequency themselves. Participants were not matched couples, so the difference cannot estimate individual men's accuracy. **Artifact:** full paper, results and Figure 2. [DOI: 10.1007/s10508-017-0939-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-017-0939-z).

**Verdict:** **Knowledge/perception: moderate.** Couple-report discrepancies support imperfect awareness; these studies do not establish that men generally lack stimulation knowledge or deliberately disregard known preferences.

## Q2. Faked orgasm: prevalence, reasons, and false feedback

- **Probability-sample prevalence:** Herbenick et al. (2019), **1,008 U.S. women aged 18–94**, GfK KnowledgePanel, **June 2015**, included women of different sexual orientations. Among **970** answering the faking item, **58.8%** had ever faked: **19.2%** still did and **39.6%** formerly did. Among **383 former fakers** answering reasons, **57.1%** wanted their partner to feel successful, **44.6%** were tired and wanted sex to end, **37.7%** wanted to protect the person's feelings, and **22.9%** hoped their partner would learn. Multiple responses were possible. These motive percentages describe former fakers. Current faking was associated with embarrassment and difficulty discussing specific sexual preferences. **Artifact:** full paper, Table 2 and results. [DOI: 10.1007/s10508-019-01493-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-019-01493-0).
- **Motives vary:** Cooper, Fenigstein, and Fauber (2014) developed a scale using **481 and 398 U.S. undergraduate women** selected for prior faking. Intercourse motives included protecting feelings, fear/insecurity, increasing one's own arousal, and ending sex. This selection cannot estimate population prevalence; an arousal motive does not establish that faking increases actual orgasm. **Artifact:** primary abstract. [DOI: 10.1007/s10508-013-0212-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-013-0212-z).
- **Association with orgasm difficulties:** Hevesi et al. (2021), **1,168 Hungarian primarily/exclusively heterosexual women**, recruited through universities and online channels, found orgasmic problems predicted faking occurrence and frequency across romantic, one-night, and continuing sexual relationships. Lifetime faking among women with each relevant relationship experience was **61.9%** in romantic relationships and **38.0%** in one-night relationships. Unequal exposure opportunities limit that comparison. Cross-sectional data leave causal direction unresolved. **Artifact:** full publisher paper, Table 1 and results. [DOI: 10.1016/j.esxm.2021.100419](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esxm.2021.100419).
- **Longitudinal counterevidence:** Jern et al. (2018) followed **1,421 Finnish women** across **seven years**. **34%** reported faking a few times or more in the preceding four weeks at at least one wave; **11%** did so at both waves. Bivariate associations linked faking and orgasm problems. The path model found **no support** for faking predicting later orgasm problems or pain, or for the reverse pathways. The long interval leaves shorter feedback processes unresolved. **Artifact:** opened publisher abstract. [DOI: 10.1007/s10508-017-1117-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-017-1117-z).

**Verdict:** **Knowledge/false feedback: weak causal support.** Faking and orgasm difficulties co-occur, and motives can conceal dissatisfaction; the proposed learning mechanism remains unproven.

## Q3. Which practices predict women's orgasm?

- **Frederick, St. John, Garcia, and Lloyd (2018):** U.S. NBC News website convenience sample, **N = 52,588**, ages **18–65**, including **26,032 heterosexual men** and **24,102 heterosexual women** in relationships. Usually/always orgasming during the past month was reported by **95%** of heterosexual men and **65%** of heterosexual women. Women's higher frequency was associated with receiving oral sex, manual genital stimulation, deep kissing, longer encounters, sexual variety, and communication. In Table 5, **35%** of heterosexual women reporting vaginal intercourse alone at their last encounter usually/always orgasmed over the month; corresponding values were **80%** for oral sex + genital stimulation + deep kissing, and **77%** when vaginal intercourse was also included. These are monthly-frequency groups, not event-specific success rates. Self-selection and correlated practices constrain causal interpretation. **Artifact:** full paper, methods and Table 5. [DOI: 10.1007/s10508-017-0939-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-017-0939-z).
- **Meta-analysis after 2018:** Mallory, Stanton, and Handy (2019) synthesized **48 studies, N = 12,145**, across countries and relationship populations; searches covered **1980–May 2017**. Communication correlated with orgasm function at **r = .23, 95% CI [.19, .27]**; the orgasm analysis included **25 studies** and **37 effects**. In the gender-moderation analysis, estimates were **r = .26 for women** and **r = .16 for men**. Outcomes encompass orgasm-function measures; the result is not a percentage-point improvement caused by communication. **Artifact:** full paper in PMC, tables and abstract. [DOI: 10.1080/00224499.2019.1568375](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2019.1568375).
- **Search gap:** A post-2018 meta-analysis jointly estimating the independent effects of oral sex, manual stimulation, duration, and variety on heterosexual women's orgasm was **not found**. The communication meta-analysis above addresses one component.

**Verdict:** **Knowledge and will: moderate mechanistic support; strong evidence of practice–orgasm associations.** These findings identify relevant behaviors. They do not determine whether absent behaviors reflect ignorance, preferences, opportunity, or unwillingness.

## Q4. Does entitlement to women's pleasure vary by relationship context?

- **Foundational mixed-method evidence:** Armstrong, England, and Fogarty (2012) used the **2005–2011 Online College Social Life Survey at 21 U.S. colleges**. Analyses included **6,881 women with complete hookup data** and **6,591 with relationship data**, alongside **85 qualitative interviews**. Women's orgasm was more common in relationships; sexual practices, familiarity, and commitment contributed. Interviews documented men's self-centered accounts of hookups and women's reluctance to claim pleasure. The entitlement interpretation is the authors' synthesis of interviews and behavior; a direct experimental entitlement measure was absent. **Artifact:** full author-hosted paper, methods and analyses. [DOI: 10.1177/0003122412445802](https://doi.org/10.1177/0003122412445802).
- **Later experimental qualification:** Klein, Kosman, and Kahalon (2024; online 2023) conducted German vignette experiments: Study 1 recruited **509 heterosexual men**; Study 2 analyzed **1,124 participants, 606 women and 518 men**, including heterosexual and bisexual participants. Study 2 found **no significant context effect on interest in a partner's orgasm**. On relative orgasm entitlement, women prioritized their male partner more in committed relationships than hookups; men's context difference was nonsignificant (**p = .162**). Stronger Madonna–whore beliefs were associated with devaluing women's pleasure. This is a conceptual extension with mixed contextual findings, not a direct replication of observed hookup behavior. **Artifact:** full paper, Study 2 results. [DOI: 10.1007/s11199-023-01424-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11199-023-01424-3).
- **Implication for the video:** Relationship commitment and greater familiarity coexist with better orgasm outcomes in Armstrong et al. Their study does not measure prior payment. Treating relationship context as a proxy for transactional compensation would be an unsupported substitution. [DOI: 10.1177/0003122412445802](https://doi.org/10.1177/0003122412445802).

A later direct replication of the full Armstrong design was **not found** in this search.

**Verdict:** **Will/entitlement: moderate.** Pleasure priorities vary across people and contexts; later experimental results qualify a uniform hookup-versus-relationship rule. **Transaction: none directly.**

## Q5. Does non-sexual payment produce sexual self-centering or lower orgasm?

**Direct test: not found.** Searches combining orgasm/pleasure/self-centeredness with paid dates, gifts, chores, investment, exchange, transactional framing, and sexual economics found no study testing the video's complete pathway. This statement describes the retrieved literature and its measurement limits.

The closest sources address different portions of the question:

- **Payment and entitlement to sexual access:** Lever, Frederick, and Hertz (2015), **17,607 unmarried heterosexual U.S. adults aged 18–65**, **2008** online survey: **16% of men** endorsed an item linking paying to entitlement to sexual activity. The reported outcome concerns sexual access; the paper supplies no payment-to-orgasm or payment-to-pleasure-neglect estimate. **Artifact:** full publisher paper, Table 1. [DOI: 10.1177/2158244015613107](https://doi.org/10.1177/2158244015613107).
- **Housework–sex negotiation:** Elliott and Umberson (2008) interviewed **31 U.S. married couples, 62 people**; **nine respondents (15%)** discussed exchanges involving housework and sex. The qualitative analysis concerns desire and marital negotiation. An orgasm consequence of those exchanges was not measured. **Artifact:** full paper in PMC. [DOI: 10.1111/j.1741-3737.2008.00489.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2008.00489.x).
- **Exchange orientation within sex:** Raposo, Impett, and Muise (2020), **three studies totaling 711 individuals**, measured tracking sexual favors and repayment. Country coverage was not fully confirmed; Study 1 used Prolific. Its **248 participants** showed a nonsignificant adjusted association between exchange orientation and own sexual satisfaction (**b = −.02, SE = .07**), alongside a positive communal-strength association (**b = .77, SE = .12**). Other analyses found some adverse partner associations, with contextual qualifications. The measure concerns sexual-for-sexual reciprocity and does not operationalize prior dates, gifts, or chores. **Artifact:** full author-hosted paper, measures and results. [DOI: 10.1007/s10508-020-01813-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-020-01813-9).
- **Sexual economics:** Baumeister and Vohs (2004) present a theoretical review of heterosexual sexual exchange. There is no single recruited sample or experimental payment-to-orgasm estimate. The framework offers hypotheses for testing. **Artifact:** publisher abstract. [DOI: 10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_2](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_2).
- **Counterexamples in commercial sex:** Komaromi (2026) analyzes **1987 Budapest interviews with ten women in sex work, three pimps, and one client**. Accounts include genuine pleasure and orgasm with paying clients, including attention to women's pleasure. This scholarly historical analysis provides counterexamples to a universal claim that payment eliminates mutual pleasure; it cannot estimate a causal average effect. **Artifact:** full publisher paper; provenance is scholarly secondary analysis of contemporaneous interviews. [DOI: 10.1177/13634607261479436](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634607261479436).

**Verdict:** **Transaction: none for the specified causal mechanism.** Evidence for payment-related sexual-access entitlement and exchange beliefs provides **weak, indirect plausibility**. The video's orgasm prediction requires a new test.

## Q6. Communal motivation: partner outcomes and effect sizes

- **General couple evidence:** Muise and Impett (2015) studied **118 U.S. heterosexual cohabiting couples** and **44 Greater Toronto mixed-sex couples** in a **21-day diary study**. Sexual communal strength predicted partner relationship satisfaction/quality: reported unstandardized coefficients included **b = .24** in the first sample and **b = .31** in the second. Gender did not moderate the relevant associations. These outcomes concern relationship evaluations; they do not provide an orgasm effect. Some associations weakened after accounting for own relationship quality. **Artifact:** full paper. [DOI: 10.1177/1948550614553641](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550614553641).
- **Women's sexual satisfaction:** Muise et al. (2017), **95 Canadian couples coping with vulvodynia**, including **93 male and two female partners**, recruited **May 2014–June 2016**, completed **eight-week diaries**. On days partners reported higher sexual communal strength, women's sexual satisfaction was higher: **b = .18, SE = .07, p < .01** (Table 3). This is an unstandardized within-person association. Women's unmitigated sexual communion—meeting the partner's needs while neglecting their own—predicted lower own satisfaction (**b = −.42, SE = .07**). Sexual-function results do not isolate orgasm. **Artifact:** full paper, Table 3. [DOI: 10.1037/hea0000470](https://doi.org/10.1037/hea0000470).
- **Relevant null result:** Hogue et al. (2019), **97 North American couples with a woman diagnosed with sexual interest/arousal disorder**, recruited **September 2016–May 2018**, found **no significant association between the partner's sexual communal strength and her sexual satisfaction**. Women's own communal strength was associated with both partners' satisfaction. **Artifact:** full PLOS paper. [DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0219768](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0219768).
- **A proximal orgasm measure:** Wolfer and Carmichael (2025), **127 partnered heterosexual adults, 66 men and 61 women**, U.S./Canada, ages **18–40**, recorded **506 sexual events over 21 days**. Personal and perceived partner orgasm pursuit were positively associated with orgasm occurrence. This study collected individual diaries rather than paired partner reports; it measured pursuit of orgasm, with distinct measurement from communal strength. **Artifact:** full publisher paper. [DOI: 10.1177/02654075251316579](https://doi.org/10.1177/02654075251316579).

**Verdict:** **Communal: moderate for partner satisfaction; weak for a specific male-communal-strength → female-orgasm effect.** The studies support measurable individual differences and daily variation. They do not establish an innate, discrete third category of men. Reported b values are scale-dependent, unstandardized coefficients, not standardized effects or percentage changes.

## Q7. Pornography: content, sequence, scripts, and partner outcomes

- **Content frequencies:** Fritz et al. (2022) coded **2,562 male–female videos from two mainstream sites in 2014**: fellatio appeared in **1,700 (66%)**, cunnilingus in **688 (27%)**, female orgasm in **317 (12%)**, and male orgasm in **910 (36%)**. These are video-level depictions; editing and clip selection affect apparent orgasm rates. **Artifact:** full paper, Table 1. [DOI: 10.1007/s10508-021-02175-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-021-02175-6).
- **Different coding denominator:** Séguin, Rodrigue, and Lavigne (2018) analyzed **50 all-time most-viewed PornHub videos**. **18.3% of female performers** and **78.0% of male performers** were depicted orgasming. The sample represents website content, with no national participant population; collection date was not confirmed. Performer-level percentages should be kept separate from Fritz's video-level estimates. **Artifact:** primary abstract, PubMed. [DOI: 10.1080/00224499.2017.1332152](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2017.1332152).
- **Sexual scripts:** Bridges et al. (2016), **1,880 heterosexual U.S. adults**, found pornography use associated with interest in and enactment of several porn-associated behaviors. The predicted gender interaction was nonsignificant. This cross-sectional study does not establish pornography-induced learning or a male-use effect on his partner's orgasm. **Artifact:** publisher abstract. [DOI: 10.1177/2374623816668275](https://doi.org/10.1177/2374623816668275).
- **Counterevidence to a uniformly negative association:** Fritz et al.'s comparison with the **2014 U.S. NSSHB**, adults **18–65**, found reported female orgasm in **53/61 (87%)** events involving shared pornography and **834/1,310 (64%)** without it; **φ = .10**. These reports include respondents' reports about themselves or partners. The unadjusted comparison measures shared event-level use and is vulnerable to selection. **Artifact:** full paper, Table 2. [DOI: 10.1007/s10508-021-02175-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-021-02175-6).
- **Sequence and male-use endpoint:** A dependable fellatio-before-cunnilingus sequence estimate was **not found** in the inspected studies. Frequencies alone do not establish order. A direct causal estimate linking men's pornography use to their female partners' orgasm was also **not found**.

**Verdict:** **Will/scripts: weak mechanistic support, with clear descriptive asymmetry in sampled content.** Observational script associations and shared-use findings leave causal effects on women's orgasms unresolved. **Transaction: none.**

## Gaps and feasible research

### What remains untested or unresolved

1. **The Q5 pathway:** No retrieved study jointly measures non-sexual investment before an encounter, the man's interpretation of it as payment, subsequent prioritization of pleasure, and the woman's independently reported orgasm. An effect conditional on explicitly framing investment as a debt also remains unresolved.
2. **Knowledge versus prioritization:** Existing studies rarely combine an objective knowledge test, accurate knowledge of this particular partner's preferences, event-level behavior, and both partners' accounts. Their separate contributions cannot be quantified from this review.
3. **False feedback over relevant timescales:** A convincing test would link a specific faked orgasm to the partner's updated belief and subsequent repetition of ineffective stimulation. The long-term null in [Jern et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-017-1117-z) leaves this shorter process unresolved.
4. **Communal motivation and orgasm:** A general-population dyadic estimate of men's communal strength predicting independently reported female orgasm, beyond preferences and practices, remains a gap in the located sources. Satisfaction findings do not supply that estimate. See [Muise et al.](https://doi.org/10.1037/hea0000470) and [Hogue et al.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0219768).
5. **Innateness and categories:** The reviewed communal studies do not establish biological origins, a distinct third class, or lifelong stability. A continuous person-level score and within-person variation are directly testable alternatives.

### Proposed independent-researcher study

All sample sizes below are **design proposals**, not reported study results.

**Primary design:** Recruit approximately **150 heterosexual couples, 300 adults**, for a baseline survey and **14-day private diary**. Recruit across relationship duration and socioeconomic circumstances. Describe the project neutrally as daily relationship experiences. Compensation should depend on survey completion, with identical eligibility on days without sex. Treat this as an estimation pilot; simulate power using realistic event counts and clustering before recruitment. Strong confirmatory mediation claims would require a larger study.

**Baseline items:** Obtain demographics, relationship duration, usual orgasm frequency during partnered sex and masturbation, relevant pain/medication information, objective sexual-knowledge questions, and each partner's own stimulation preferences. Ask each person to predict the other's preferences independently. Use published communal-strength and sexual-exchange measures from [Raposo et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10508-020-01813-9), subject to instrument permissions. Measure usual divisions of household work and expense payment.

**Daily and encounter items:** Use brief fixed-time reports of investment and an optional private report following sex. Record contribution times, intention-measurement times, and encounter times separately. Distinguish contemporaneous reports from retrospective recall; submission times alone cannot establish event ordering. Proposed items include:

- Who paid for dates or gifts, how much, and who performed which household tasks? Record each person's contribution and its usualness.
- Before any sexual activity, to what extent was the contribution intended to increase the chance of sex? Did either person explicitly connect it to sex?
- To what extent did each person feel that sex was owed because of that contribution? Separate the giver's expectation from the recipient's perceived obligation.
- Before sex, how important was each partner's pleasure to the respondent? Record desire, fatigue, stress, conflict, and expected orgasm separately.
- After sex, which oral/manual/penetrative practices occurred, in what sequence, for approximately how long, and in accordance with whose stated preferences? Who initiated or stopped each activity?
- Did the respondent orgasm? Did they communicate or fake an orgasm? Did they think their partner orgasmed? Permit uncertainty and keep responses inaccessible to partners.
- How satisfied was each person? Did either feel pressure, pain, or a wish to stop? Record consensual encounters without orgasm without automatically classifying them as failures.

**Analysis:** Preregister women's self-reported orgasm per encounter as the primary outcome. Fit a multilevel logistic model with encounters nested within couples. Separate each man's average investment from deviations around his own average; model exchange framing independently from amount spent or chores completed. Prespecify the investment × exchange-framing interaction. Adjust for baseline function, desire, familiarity, stress, and other temporally appropriate confounders. Report odds ratios, confidence intervals, and predicted probabilities.

Estimate the total association before adding proposed mediators. Then examine whether measured prioritization and practices account for part of the association, explicitly treating this mediation as observational. Analyze non-sex days as well: restricting all data collection to sexual encounters risks selecting on an outcome that payment expectations could themselves influence. Use independently reported orgasm disagreement to examine false feedback. Report missingness, discordant couple reports, and imprecision.

**Lower-cost experimental complement:** A preregistered **2 × 2 vignette study of approximately 400 adult heterosexual men** could randomize date payment (he pays / split) and framing (sex expected in return / no expectation). Hold relationship context and the partner's communicated preferences constant. Measure expected entitlement and intended attention to her pleasure using neutral items. This design tests effects on stated intentions; actual orgasm consequences would still require behavioral data. Report that limitation explicitly.

## Access and verification limits

All eight questions are addressed. Unresolved items include Q5's direct causal test, Q7's sequencing estimate and causal partner-orgasm effect, several collection dates, and Q1's country and detailed error distribution in the accessible 2022 abstract. The review's conclusions preserve these gaps. Bibliographic entries for all cited papers appear in `sources.bib`; dataset links are associated with their originating papers.
