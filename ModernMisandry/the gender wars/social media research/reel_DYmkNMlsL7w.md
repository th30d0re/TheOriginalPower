It's 5 in 10 of us who have badgered a woman who said no until she changed her answer. 8 in 10 of us who have lied to a woman in order to convince her to sleep with us. And more than 9 in 10 of us who have admitted to not accepting no as an answer. So we are back for Professor Neal's claims 3, 4, and 5. Today, I'm going to cover claim number 3. So let's look at the study. The paper he shows is Isolate, Inebriate, Intimidate, Repeat by O'Sullivan and Ronis, published in the Journal of Interpersonal Violence in 2026. Here's how the study worked. 2,689 men aged 18 to 34 were surveyed through an online panel called Qualtrics. To be eligible, you had to identify as a man, be in that age range, and have had a sexual encounter with a woman in the past 2 years. That is the sample. Then the men were asked one main question. In the past 4 years, how many times have you used any of the following strategies to get or try to get a woman to have some type of sex when she didn't want to have sex or acted like she didn't want to have sex? Read it again. To get or try to get. So an attempt counts, even if no sex happened. And then, when she didn't want to have sex or acted like she didn't want to have sex. So it includes cases where the woman actually didn't want it, but also cases where she just acted like she didn't want it. Now the men were given a list of 36 strategies and asked to mark how often they used each one. And here's the part that really matters. Those 36 strategies branch from things that are clearly aggressive and predatory, like use drugs to help or threaten to hurt her, all the way to things that on the surface are much more ambiguous, like told her whatever she wanted to hear. Those are very different categories of behavior. And the study lumps them all into one list of 36. Now here's what Professor Neal did. Out of those 36 strategies, he cherry-picked the ones that, when paraphrased a certain way, paint men in the worst possible light. His third claim, 5 in 10 of us badgered a woman who said no until she changed her answer. He most likely got this from the strategy, asked her repeatedly to have sex. 48.6% of men in the survey reported having done this at least once in the past four years. So roughly 5 in 10. But hold on. Merriam-Webster dictionary defines badger as to harass or annoy persistently. Now, is that what ask for repeatedly to have sex means? It can be, but it doesn't have to be. Let me give you a real world example. Imagine you met a woman at a bar. You hit it off, you're vibing, you're making out. You say, want to come back to my place? She smiles and says, oh, you playboy, you think I will jump into your bed just like that? We don't know each other that well yet. Okay, you keep having a good time. The vibe is still good. One hour later, you say, you sure you don't want to come over? She smiles and says, okay, you go to your place and spend the night together. By the survey's warning, that would count. You basically asked her repeatedly to have sex and she initially acted like she didn't want to. But is that badgering? Is that harassment? No, that is just how flirting works. Can the same item also capture genuine harassment where a guy keeps pressuring a woman until she gives in just to make him stop? Yes, absolutely. And if it does, that is a horrible behavior and a real problem. But then there are other, much more specific items on the survey for that, like gradually pushed verbally or physically until you got what you wanted. So when Professor Neil takes the broad ask her repeatedly item and paraphrases it as badgered until she changed her answer, he is not just reporting the data. He is misrepresenting it. He is picking the most aggressive possible interpretation of an ambiguous item, ignoring the fact that the item itself includes everything from rejected flirting to actual harassment and presenting it as if that is what the number means.
