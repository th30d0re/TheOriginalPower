# The Relationship Contract: What Nobody's Actually Talking About

There's a conversation happening right now about porn, monogamy, and relationship boundaries that frustrates me because nobody seems to be naming the actual structure underneath it.

On one side: "If you're in a committed relationship, you shouldn't watch porn."  
On the other: "You aren't owed sex, so porn is fine."

Both sides are dancing around the real problem. The issue isn't porn. The issue is that we've built a relationship model where one partner gets to keep all the autonomy while the other partner gets all the obligations — and we've convinced ourselves this is fair because we're using language that sounds progressive.

---

## The Historical Baseline Nobody Remembers

If you go back to what used to be called coverture — the legal system where a married woman's identity was suspended under her husband's — there was at least a structural coherence to it. It was brutal, unjust, and women fought hard to dismantle it. But it was *coherent*: the wife surrendered bodily, economic, and social autonomy. In exchange, the husband surrendered sexual autonomy (exclusivity) and took on provider obligations. Both people gave something up. The system was oppressive, but it was at least a *swap*.

What we've built now is not a swap. It's a selective dismantling where one side keeps the benefits of both the old system and the new system, while the other side keeps the costs of both.

Here's what that looks like in practice:

**The old system's benefits (now claimed selectively):**
- Male commitment and exclusivity
- Male provision and protection
- The security of a permanent partnership

**The new system's benefits (also claimed selectively):**
- Complete bodily autonomy — sex only when desired, no accountability for frequency or reciprocity
- Complete economic autonomy — "my money is mine"
- Unilateral right to exit the relationship
- No traditional domestic obligations

**What the other partner is left with:**
- Sexual exclusivity with no reciprocal guarantee of intimacy
- Provider obligations with no reciprocal guarantee of partnership
- The obligation to stay committed even when the contract is one-sided
- Social and legal frameworks that treat their unmet needs as entitlement, not injury

This isn't about hating women. This is about recognizing that a contract where one party keeps all the escape clauses and the other party keeps all the obligations is not a contract. It's an enclosure.

---

## What "Exclusivity" Actually Means

Let's be precise about what monogamy asks. Exclusivity is not a small thing. It means surrendering the right to seek intimacy anywhere else. That surrender only makes sense as part of a trade. If I'm giving up every other sexual option in the world, the reciprocity is that my partner is *available* — not on demand, not without consent, but as a partner who recognizes that exclusivity is a sacrifice that requires mutual care.

When exclusivity exists without availability, what you have is not monogamy. You have enclosure. The deprived partner is locked in a cage they agreed to enter on the assumption it was a shared space.

The standard response to this is: "You aren't owed sex." Which is true, in isolation. But nobody is arguing that anyone is owed sex. The argument is about what exclusivity *costs* and who bears that cost. If one partner can revoke sexual access indefinitely while the other partner is forbidden from seeking alternatives, the enclosure is total. The only remaining options are: leave, cheat, suffer, or force — and three of those are socially condemned while the fourth is criminal.

This is why the porn conversation matters. When someone says "you can't watch porn either," they are not enforcing intimacy. They are completing the enclosure. The partner is now sexually enclosed on every front: no external partners, no paid alternatives, no solo outlets. Their sexual autonomy has been reduced to zero while their partner's sexual autonomy remains absolute.

That is not a relationship. That is a system of control dressed in the language of boundaries.

---

## The Structural Parallel

I'm going to make a comparison here, and I need to be careful about it because people will misunderstand it on purpose if I don't spell it out.

Under chattel slavery, slave masters fed enslaved people, housed them, and provided medical care to a minimal standard. If you pointed only at those provisions, you could make slavery sound like a welfare system. But nobody does that, because we understand that the resources extracted from enslaved people far outweighed what they received in return. The *structure* was extraction, and the provisions were maintenance costs.

I'm not comparing the *magnitude* of harm. I'm comparing the *structure*. Under coverture, men provided for women. But women also surrendered their legal identity, their economic autonomy, their bodily autonomy in marriage. The provision was the maintenance cost of a system that extracted far more.

What some people want now is the provision without the extraction. They want men to continue the old obligations — provision, protection, commitment, exclusivity — while women keep the full autonomy that feminism won. That's not a trade. That's not even a theft. It's a structural impossibility, because the old obligations only existed in the context of the old surrender. If one partner keeps full autonomy, the other partner's obligations become unmotivated. There's no contract left.

---

## The "Getting Their Lick Back" Question

Here's where I want to push deeper, because I think the frameworks sometimes stop too early.

Some people will read the above and say: "This is just the pendulum overshooting. Women were oppressed for centuries; now the system overcorrected. It will settle back to equilibrium." That might be structurally true. But it doesn't fully account for something I see in some feminist discourse: a genuine *revenge* mentality.

There is a strain of contemporary feminism that doesn't just want equality. It wants payback. It wants men to experience what women experienced. It treats male suffering under the new asymmetry not as a bug to fix, but as a feature to celebrate. You can see this in the tone of certain conversations — the barely-concealed satisfaction when men struggle in dating, the framing of male loneliness as "male entitlement" rather than a structural outcome, the social media rhetoric that treats male emotional needs as punchlines.

The feeling of superiority that the advantaged partner gets in an asymmetric system is real. And it is *enjoyable*. That's why it's so durable. The advantaged group in any extraction system doesn't just tolerate its position; it defends it, because the status compensation feels valuable even when the material compensation is thin.

This matters because it means the problem isn't just ignorance. Some people genuinely *like* the asymmetry. They've been sold a narrative where any male grievance is either patriarchal fragility or just desserts, and that narrative lets them enjoy the advantages without guilt. Telling them "the pendulum will settle" assumes they want equilibrium. Some don't. They want the high ground.

That doesn't mean all women, or even most women, are operating this way. It means the structure *rewards* those who do, and that reward creates a selection pressure. The people most likely to defend the asymmetry are the ones benefiting from it most directly — which is true of every extraction system in history.

## The Intimacy Pathway Deadlock

There's another layer that gets missed. People often talk about sex in relationships as if the conflict is just about frequency — one person wants more, one wants less. But the deeper conflict is about *pathways*.

For some people, emotional safety opens the door to physical intimacy. For others, physical intimacy opens the door to emotional safety. Neither pathway is wrong. Both are valid. But in the current framework, only one pathway gets validated as legitimate.

The "equal" standard we apply is this: both partners need emotional connection before physical intimacy. That sounds fair — same rule for everyone. But it's not fair, because it's *one partner's pathway imposed as universal*. The partner who needs physical touch to feel emotionally close is structurally blocked: they can't reach the prerequisite (emotional connection) without the thing they're told must come after it (physical connection). They're trapped in a loop.

When they express frustration, they're told they're "making it about themselves" or "only caring about sex." Their legitimate need for a different pathway is pathologized. Meanwhile, the partner whose pathway happens to match the socially endorsed sequence experiences the arrangement as natural and fair.

This is how asymmetry hides inside language that sounds neutral.

---

## What Would Actually Fix This

I'm not advocating for a return to coverture. That system was unjust. What it had was *coherence* — a full swap of autonomy for security. We broke the swap on one side and expected the other side to keep holding up their end. That's not sustainable.

The actual fix requires one of three things:

1. **The full swap returns.** Both partners surrender significant autonomy in exchange for deep reciprocal obligation. This is the traditional model, and while it had severe injustices in practice, its *structural logic* was symmetrical.

2. **Full autonomy for both.** Both partners keep their autonomy, including the right to seek alternatives if needs are chronically unmet. This is the honest liberal model, but it requires renegotiating what "monogamy" means — and most people aren't willing to have that conversation explicitly.

3. **Explicit negotiated equity.** Both partners acknowledge their different pathways, needs, and capacities, and build a contract that accounts for asymmetry rather than pretending it doesn't exist. This is the hardest option because it requires ongoing negotiation, mutual good faith, and the capacity to see the other person's needs as legitimate even when they differ from your own.

What we have now is a society that pretends option 2 exists while actually enforcing a one-sided version of option 1's obligations. We're told we're in a world of equal autonomy, but the implicit contract still loads all the old duties onto one side. That's the dishonesty. That's the fracture.

---

## A Note on the Science

I want to address the testosterone question briefly because people keep bringing it up. Yes, there are documented differences in average testosterone levels and their relationship to sexual desire. But that's not the argument I'm making, and it's not necessary to the argument. The structural analysis holds regardless of biology, because the harm mechanism operates identically across genders. Women in the deprived position experience the same depression, anxiety, and relational deterioration that men do. The structure is the variable; biology is noise.

Bringing testosterone into this risks two things: it lets people dismiss the argument as biological essentialism, and it lets the other side derail the conversation into hormone debates that miss the architecture. The architecture doesn't need biology to be real.

---

## The Bottom Line

The conversation about porn in relationships is a distraction from the actual conversation we need to have: what do we mean by monogamy, what are we actually trading, and does the current arrangement make sense as a contract?

If exclusivity is going to be enforced, availability has to be part of the deal. If autonomy is going to be absolute, exclusivity can't be. And if we're going to claim we're past the old systems of obligation, we can't keep loading the old obligations onto one partner while calling it equality.

What we have right now is not equality. It's not even a honest asymmetry. It's an asymmetry that uses the language of equality to prevent the disadvantaged partner from naming what's happening to them. And the first step toward fixing it is being allowed to name it.
