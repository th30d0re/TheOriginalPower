# The left-handed mortality curve and height as a gendered axis: findings

**Research date:** 2026-10-01  
**Model:** Codex (GPT-5)  
**Scope:** Every supported claim below rests on an artifact opened during this review. Quotes contain fewer than 25 words. Provenance is classified as primary/government, scholarly secondary, tertiary, or unsourced. “Supported” means that the opened evidence supports the stated prediction at the stated level of precision.

## L1. What Halpern and Coren reported

**Verdict: SUPPORTED as a description of their reports; NOT SUPPORTED as a mortality inference.** Halpern and Coren published two different results. The 1988 *Nature* correspondence used deceased Major League Baseball players and found an eight-month mean difference. The widely repeated nine-year claim came from a 1991 one-page *New England Journal of Medicine* letter based on recent California deaths and next-of-kin reports. Neither design estimated life expectancy from a birth cohort.

### Best evidence

1. **Halpern and Coren (1988), *Nature* — scholarly secondary; archival comparison.** The study used *The Baseball Encyclopedia*. The opened 2019 review reproduces the reported group statistics: 1,472 right-handers died at mean age 64.64 years (SD 15.5), and 236 left-handers at 63.97 years (SD 15.4), a difference of 0.67 years. Exact quote: “mean age at death of right-handers … was clearly not different.” The reviewers identify an inappropriate Kolmogorov–Smirnov analysis as the basis of the original inference. [Opened artifacts: *Nature* article record and the complete open-access review text returned by PubMed Central](https://www.nature.com/articles/333213b0); [review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7058267/).

2. **Halpern and Coren (1991), *New England Journal of Medicine* — scholarly secondary; decedent survey letter.** The authors drew 2,875 recent death certificates from two Southern California counties, sent handedness questionnaires to next of kin, and obtained 987 usable cases. Mean age at death was 75 for right-handers and 66 for left-handers. Accident deaths were reported for 7.9% of left-handers and 1.5% of right-handers, relative risk 5.7. Exact quote: “the proportion of left-handers in the population decreases substantially with age.” [Opened artifacts: NEJM letter page and Altman’s statistical reconstruction of the sampling design](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM199104043241418); [Altman (2005)](https://rss.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1740-9713.2005.00130.x).

3. **McManus (2019), *Brain and Neuroscience Advances* — scholarly secondary; historical review.** This review distinguishes the reports and states that the 1991 letter examined 987 Californians, producing 66 versus 75 years. Exact quote: “the left-handers died 9 years earlier.” It also recalculates the baseball means and rejects a significant difference under an ordinary comparison of means. [Opened artifact: full open-access review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7058267/).

### Evidence against the prediction

Both original samples condition on death. A cross-section of recent deaths cannot convert mean age at death into life expectancy when handedness prevalence differs sharply across birth cohorts. The baseball sample also spans many birth years, has large imbalance between handedness groups, and shows a raw mean difference of eight months. These features preclude the claimed nine-year population effect.

**Licensed on-air sentence:** The famous nine-year figure came from 987 recent California deaths reported by relatives; the earlier baseball study found about eight months, and neither design followed comparable left- and right-handed birth cohorts through life.

## L2. Cohort effects, rebuttals, and current consensus

**Verdict: SUPPORTED.** The age curve is largely a birth-cohort and reporting phenomenon shaped by forced conversion and changing norms. Several prospective studies find no mortality excess. The available evidence permits, at most, a small unresolved effect in particular subgroups; it rejects a nine-year general loss of life.

### Best evidence

1. **Salive, Guralnik, and Glynn (1993), *American Journal of Public Health* — scholarly secondary; simulation and prospective cohort.** A national-data simulation reproduced a younger mean age at death from the age distribution of handedness alone. A six-year East Boston cohort of 3,774 older adults found relative odds of death 1.04 for left-handers, 95% CI 0.79–1.36. Exact quote: “left-handedness is not associated with mortality.” [Opened artifact: full PubMed Central article and abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1694599/).

2. **Gilbert and Wysocki (1992), *Neuropsychologia* — scholarly secondary; 1,177,507-person cross-sectional survey.** Respondents aged 10–86 reported writing and throwing hand on the *National Geographic* smell survey. Left preference declined with age, while right-writing/left-throwing became relatively more common in older groups. A later reconstruction reports 2.96% left-handed among 7,870 people born in 1900–1905 and 11.5% among 614,438 born from 1945 onward. Exact quote: “age-specific rates of mixed- and left-handedness are distinct.” [Opened artifacts: PubMed abstract and McManus’s historical-rate analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1528408/); [historical analysis](https://jhanley.biostat.mcgill.ca/bios601/CandHchapter06/HistoryGeographyHumanHandedness.pdf).

3. **Hugdahl et al. (1993), *Neuropsychologia* — scholarly secondary; cross-sectional study with switching history.** Among 2,787 people aged 21–101, current left-handedness declined from 15.22% at ages 21–30 to 1.67% above age 80. Reported switching rose from 2.69% to 6.75%. Exact quote: “This supports the modification hypothesis and questions the elimination hypothesis.” Adjustment did not erase the full age gradient, so the authors left residual uncertainty. [Opened artifact: PubMed abstract and bibliographic record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8502368/).

4. **Basso et al. (2000), *Epidemiology* — scholarly secondary; Danish twin follow-up.** In 118 opposite-handed twin pairs born 1900–1910, the right-handed twin died first in 60% of dizygotic pairs (95% CI 49.0–71.5%) and 50% of monozygotic pairs. Exact quote: “We did not find evidence of excess mortality.” [Opened artifact: complete journal PDF surfaced through the Max Planck demographic repository](https://user.demogr.mpg.de/jwv/pdf/PUB-2000-040.pdf).

### Evidence against the prediction

Some studies retained a residual age gradient after accounting for self-reported switching. Rogerson’s survival analysis of 4,448 men found small significant survival declines for left-handers between ages 65 and 85. A 1993 cricketer archive found a 25-month difference, 65.62 versus 63.52 years (p=.006), concentrated in war and accidental deaths. These findings allow a narrow subgroup effect. They do not reproduce the nine-year claim. Prospective Canadian data also found no significant excess: OR 1.18, 95% CI 0.72–1.93, among 2,786 adults over 65. [Opened artifacts: Rogerson PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8178195/); [cricketer PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8350033/); [Canadian cohort PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15513160/).

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Later cohort studies show that older generations reported less left-handedness and more forced switching, while prospective cohorts in the United States, Canada, and Denmark found no significant mortality penalty.

## L3. Does the curve match the end of forced conversion?

**Verdict: SUPPORTED for Germany and broadly consistent for the United States and United Kingdom.** German cohort data show a direct temporal match between declining conversion and rising reported left-handed writing. United States and United Kingdom sources document advocacy and practice during the twentieth century, with weaker national timing data.

### Best evidence

1. **Guber (2019), *Labour Economics* — scholarly secondary; German Socio-Economic Panel.** The study covers birth cohorts 1920–1997. About 90% of left-handers born before the end of World War II wrote with the right hand; switching fell to about 60% for the 1960 cohort and approached zero by the late 1990s. Across cohorts, 57% of innate left-handers reported switching. Exact quote: “switching rates decline monotonically.” [Opened artifact: complete Max Planck discussion-paper PDF underlying the published article](https://www.mpisoc.mpg.de/fileadmin/user_upload/datapool/publications/1732_DP%2007-2016_2.pdf).

2. **German education guidance documented by Guber — primary policy quoted within scholarly secondary research.** The 1960 guidance called writing a “particular right-hand activity.” In 1987 teachers received instructions to support writing with the preferred hand. Exact quote: “It was not until 1987” that supportive instruction arrived. [Opened artifact: pages 4–5 of Guber’s complete PDF](https://www.mpisoc.mpg.de/fileadmin/user_upload/datapool/publications/1732_DP%2007-2016_2.pdf).

3. **Kushner (2012), *Laterality* — scholarly secondary; historical review of British and American practice.** The review documents educators, psychologists, and psychiatrists advocating conversion in both countries. It includes the 1946 New York City Board of Education chief psychiatrist’s recommendation that children adopt right-handedness and reports routine conversion attempts in some American schools into the 1970s. Exact quote: “advocated forcing left-handed children to write with their right hands.” [Opened artifact: full Taylor & Francis article page](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1357650X.2011.615127).

4. **Ellis et al. (1998), *Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health* — scholarly secondary; northern England survey.** In 6,097 Edinburgh handedness inventories, prevalence by a negative laterality quotient fell from 11.2% at age 15 to 4.4% at age 70. The study explicitly tested forced dextrality as a cohort explanation. Exact quote: “Evidence for forced dextrality was sought.” [Opened artifact: PubMed abstract and linked full article record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9604040/).

### Evidence against the prediction

The timing match is incomplete outside Germany. United States and United Kingdom evidence establishes that conversion occurred, while national cohort-specific conversion rates remain unavailable in the opened record. Hugdahl et al. found that reported switching explained part of the decline and left a residual age effect. Self-reports can also undercount attempted conversion and classify people differently depending on whether writing, throwing, or a multi-item inventory defines handedness.

**Licensed on-air sentence:** In Germany, forced right-hand writing fell from about 90% in prewar left-handed cohorts to nearly zero by the late 1990s, closely matching the rise in reported left-handedness across birth cohorts.

## T1. Height wage premium and mechanism

**Verdict: SUPPORTED for a wage association; MIXED on mechanism.** The premium appears for men and women and varies substantially by country and specification. Childhood cognition, education, occupational sorting, adolescent social experience, health, and discrimination each receive some support. No single mechanism explains every sample.

### Best evidence

1. **Thompson, Portrait, and Schoonmade (2023), *Economics & Human Biology* — scholarly secondary; systematic review and meta-analysis.** The review included 42 studies in narrative synthesis and 17 in meta-analysis. Premiums were smaller in the United States and Australia and larger in Latin America and Asia; within regions, men generally had larger premiums. Exact quote: “the height premium varied by context.” [Opened artifact: complete IDEAS/RePEc article record with abstract and study counts](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ehbiol/v50y2023ics1570677x23000540.html).

2. **Case and Paxson (2008), *Journal of Political Economy* — scholarly secondary; US and UK longitudinal datasets.** Each additional inch was associated with 1.0–2.3% higher hourly earnings and 1.4–2.9% higher weekly earnings across male and female samples. In the 1970 British cohort, childhood test scores cut coefficients by more than half and rendered them insignificant. In the 1958 cohort, test scores and background controls reduced the premium 48% for men and 63% for women. Exact quote: “test scores explain a large portion of the height premium.” [Opened artifact: complete Princeton-hosted published PDF](https://www.princeton.edu/~accase/downloads/downloads/Stature_and_Status.pdf).

3. **Persico, Postlewaite, and Silverman (2004), *Journal of Political Economy* — scholarly secondary; NLSY and NCDS longitudinal analysis.** For white US men, one adult inch was associated with 1.8% higher wages. Holding adult height constant, an extra inch at age 16 predicted 2.6% higher adult wages. Club and sports participation partly mediated the teen-height coefficient. Adult height became negligible after teen height entered the model. Women showed an adult premium in some specifications, without the same teen-height pattern. Exact quote: “Controlling for teen height essentially eliminates the effect of adult height.” [Opened artifacts: NBER paper page and complete working-paper PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10522); [PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10522/w10522.pdf).

4. **Judge and Cable (2004), *Journal of Applied Psychology* — scholarly secondary; meta-analysis plus four earnings studies.** Across 45 independent studies, corrected correlation with workplace success was .26; estimates were .29 for men and .21 for women, with the sex difference nonsignificant (Z=1.86). Subjective criteria correlated .31 with height and objective criteria .21 (difference Z=2.17, p<.01). Their four earnings samples yielded a weighted correlation of .31 after controls for sex, age, and weight. Exact quote: “height clearly matters in the context of workplace success.” [Opened artifact: complete 14-page article PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Daniel-Cable-2/publication/8545837_The_Effect_of_Physical_Height_on_Workplace_Success_and_Income_Preliminary_Test_of_a_Theoretical_Model/links/00b495390a8394aea7000000/The-Effect-of-Physical-Height-on-Workplace-Success-and-Income-Preliminary-Test-of-a-Theoretical-Model.pdf).

### Evidence against the prediction

The premium shrinks sharply after controls for childhood test scores, education, occupation, industry, family background, and health. In nine waves of the British Household Panel Survey, height initially predicted 1.5% higher wages per inch for both sexes; education explained half, and higher-status occupations and industries explained half of the remainder. Finnish identical-twin estimates found a female premium and no male premium, reversing the usual sex pattern. Hübler’s German analysis found nonlinear results: shorter-than-average women and moderately tall men had the highest wages, with less robust female estimates. These studies weaken a universal discrimination account and a universal linear premium. [Opened artifacts: NBER BHPS record](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14007); [Finnish twin article record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927537113000778); [German article record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1570677X09000483).

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Taller workers generally earn more, often about one to two percent per inch in US and UK samples, and much of that association travels through childhood cognition, education, and occupational sorting while a residual premium varies by setting and sex.

## T2. Leadership and elections

**Verdict: MIXED.** Height predicts perceived leadership, leader emergence, popular-vote share, and reelection in several studies. It does not significantly predict which candidate wins a US presidential election across the full historical series. CEO statistics come mainly from a journalist’s nonrandom survey and do not isolate selection or causation.

### Best evidence

1. **Stulp et al. (2013), *The Leadership Quarterly* — scholarly secondary; analysis of all available US presidential elections.** Taller candidates won 26 of 45 elections, 58%, statistically indistinguishable from chance. Relative candidate height correlated with relative popular support at r=.39 across 46 elections and explained 15% of its variation. Reelected presidents were 5.47 cm taller than non-reelected presidents, 181.87 versus 176.40 cm (p=.048). Exact quote: “taller candidates were not more likely to win.” [Opened artifact: complete author-hosted published PDF](https://www.gertstulp.com/pdf/Stulp%20et%20al%202013_TLQ_Presidential%20height.pdf).

2. **Stulp et al. (2013), presidential selection and ratings — scholarly secondary.** Presidents averaged 7.23 cm above their male birth cohort (d=1.02); losing candidates averaged 6.95 cm above their cohort (d=1.08). Height correlated with historians’ average greatness rank at partial Spearman r=.328 (p=.034), controlling election year. Leadership-rating correlations ranged from .289 to .378. Exact quote: “both winning and losing presidential candidates were much taller.” [Opened artifact: complete article PDF](https://www.gertstulp.com/pdf/Stulp%20et%20al%202013_TLQ_Presidential%20height.pdf).

3. **Judge and Cable (2004), *Journal of Applied Psychology* — scholarly secondary; meta-analysis.** Height had a positive average relation to leader emergence, while the credibility interval included zero, indicating substantial heterogeneity across studies. Subjective evaluations showed a stronger relationship with height than objective outcomes, .31 versus .21. Exact quote: “high variability in the height–leadership correlations.” [Opened artifact: complete article PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Daniel-Cable-2/publication/8545837_The_Effect_of_Physical_Height_on_Workplace_Success_and_Income_Preliminary_Test_of_a_Theoretical_Model/links/00b495390a8394aea7000000/The-Effect-of-Physical-Height-on-Workplace-Success-and-Income-Preliminary-Test-of-a-Theoretical-Model.pdf).

4. **Gladwell CEO survey, summarized by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis — tertiary; convenience survey.** Gladwell surveyed about half of Fortune 500 CEOs. Roughly 58% were at least six feet tall and 30% at least 6 feet 2 inches; the comparison figures cited for American men were 14.5% and 3.9%. Exact quote: “the average CEO is approximately 3 inches taller.” The sampling method, nonresponse, sex composition, and confounding factors prevent a causal estimate. [Opened artifact: Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis article reproducing the survey and caveats](https://www.stlouisfed.org/publications/regional-economist/april-2005/so-much-for-that-merit-raise-the-link-between-wages-and-appearance).

### Evidence against the prediction

Presidential height does not significantly predict the binary election outcome over the full series. Candidate-height data become less reliable in older elections, and all serious candidates are already strongly selected for height, education, income, and status. Historical greatness ratings invite halo effects and retrospective confounding. The CEO figures lack a peer-reviewed sampling frame and cannot show that boards chose executives because of height.

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Taller presidential candidates received more popular support and taller presidents were more likely to be reelected, yet the taller candidate won only 26 of 45 elections, a rate indistinguishable from chance.

## T3. Gendered asymmetry in wages, leadership, and mate choice

**Verdict: MIXED.** Height advantages men more consistently in wages, status perception, and heterosexual mate preferences. The prediction that height becomes neutral or penalized for women after a threshold receives isolated support and substantial contrary evidence. The proposed mechanism that tall women are punished for challenging a masculine role remains unverified by height-specific evidence.

### Best evidence

1. **Thompson, Portrait, and Schoonmade (2023), *Economics & Human Biology* — scholarly secondary; systematic review and meta-analysis.** Within geographic regions, the height wage premium appeared larger for men than women. Exact quote: “a larger height premium for men than for women.” The review establishes average asymmetry and contextual variation; it does not establish a female penalty. [Opened artifact: complete IDEAS/RePEc article record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ehbiol/v50y2023ics1570677x23000540.html).

2. **Blaker et al. (2013), *Group Processes & Intergroup Relations* — scholarly secondary; experiments on perceived leadership.** Tall male and female targets were seen as more leader-like. The male effect was stronger and was mediated by dominance, health, and intelligence; the female effect was mediated by perceived intelligence, with no significant height effect on female dominance or health. Exact quote: “height did not have a significant effect on female health or female dominance.” [Opened artifact: complete university-repository chapter containing the published study](https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/1528680/chapter%202%20-%20The%20Height%20Leadership%20advantage%20in%20men%20and%20women.pdf).

3. **Stulp et al. (2013), *PLOS ONE* — scholarly secondary; UK household couples and simulations.** Actual partners showed modest height assortment, r=.18. Male-shorter couples and couples with men more than 25 cm taller occurred less often than chance. Exact quote: “preferences for partner height generally translate into actual pairing … only modestly.” [Opened artifact: complete PLOS ONE article](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0054186).

4. **Hübler (2009), *Economics & Human Biology* — scholarly secondary; German panel, 1985–2004.** Wages were nonlinear by sex: the estimated maximum was below average height for women and above average for men. Results for women were less robust. Exact quote: “women who are shorter than average … enjoy significant wage advantages.” This is direct evidence for a female optimum below the mean in one national dataset. [Opened artifact: ScienceDirect article record and abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1570677X09000483).

### Evidence against the prediction

Case and Paxson found positive associations of similar size for men and women. Judge and Cable estimated corrected workplace-success correlations of .29 for men and .21 for women, with no significant sex difference. Finnish identical-twin analysis found a significant female height premium and no male premium. Indonesian estimates associated 10 cm with 7.5% higher earnings for men and 13.0% for women, including 18.0% among self-employed women. A Finnish natural-setting study of 44 raters and 78 female subordinates judged tall women better managers. These results directly contradict a general tall-woman penalty. [Opened artifacts: Case and Paxson full PDF](https://www.princeton.edu/~accase/downloads/downloads/Stature_and_Status.pdf); [Finnish twins](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927537113000778); [Indonesia](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1570677X14000045); [female managers PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8201814/).

General gender-backlash research finds a small penalty for women’s explicit dominance, including meta-analytic d=-0.28 for explicit displays and d=0.03 for implicit displays. Height-specific verification of a masculine-role mechanism requires research that manipulates height; this study manipulated dominance displays. [Opened artifact: Academy of Management meta-analysis abstract](https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/ambpp.2015.17293abstract).

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Height advantages men more consistently, especially in mate preference and leadership perception, while tall women usually show smaller positive effects; evidence for a general tall-woman penalty or a masculine-role mechanism remains insufficient.

## T4. Design default in vehicles, PPE, and workstations

**Verdict: SUPPORTED.** Size and sex defaults shape crash testing, PPE, and workstation design. Small-bodied people and women bear measurable fit and safety costs. Contemporary standards increasingly specify adjustable ranges, which reduces the reach of the claim.

### Best evidence

1. **US Government Accountability Office (2023) — primary/government review.** GAO found that federal crash-test dummies cover a limited body-size range, omit some female physiology, and lack lower-leg sensors. Women face higher death and lower-leg injury risk in comparable crashes. Exact quote: “currently used dummies represent a limited range of body sizes.” GAO’s recommendation prompted NHTSA’s 2024 comprehensive plan. [Opened artifact: complete GAO report page with findings, methods, and recommendation status](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105595).

2. **NHTSA (2026), report to Congress — primary/government analysis.** Early devices centered on a midsize male, with a small 5th-percentile female created by scaling. For 2010–2020 model-year vehicles, female relative fatality risk was 6.3%, down from 18.3% in 1960–2009 vehicles. The current HIII-05F remains the benchmark that the female-specific THOR-05F is intended to improve. Exact quote: “Early ATDs centered on developing a dummy representative of a midsize male.” [Opened artifact: complete NHTSA report-to-Congress PDF](https://www.nhtsa.gov/document/report-congress-progress-thor-05f-worldsid-05f-and-female-crash-safety-research).

3. **Ramstrand et al. (2022), systematic review of PPE fit — scholarly secondary.** Sixteen studies met inclusion criteria; 88% found a statistically significant effect of fit on occupational performance. Poor fit reduced mobility, endurance, pulmonary function, or reaction performance. Female soldiers were especially poorly served by unisex torso sizing. Exact quote: “88% … found that the fit of PPE had a statistically significant effect.” The review also judged the literature small and methodologically biased. [Opened artifact: complete PubMed Central systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9710848/).

4. **NIOSH (2023) and OSHA workstation guidance — primary/government guidance.** NIOSH identifies smaller-framed construction workers as vulnerable to standard-size PPE and requires representative anthropometric databases. OSHA desk guidance specifies adjustability from the 5th-percentile female to the 95th-percentile male. Exact quote: “poorly fitting PPE is particularly relevant for smaller-framed construction workers.” [Opened artifacts: complete NIOSH bulletin](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2023/ppe-fit-construction.html); [OSHA workstation guidance](https://www.osha.gov/etools/computer-workstations/components/work-space).

### Evidence against the prediction

The phrase “designed around male height” is too broad for current workstation standards, which explicitly span small female to large male anthropometry. Crash injury gaps also reflect vehicle type, crash configuration, seating position, age, stature, and restraint use. IIHS found equal head and chest injury odds after narrowing crashes by vehicle size and configuration, while women retained roughly double the odds of moderate injury, mainly in legs and feet. Newer vehicle design reduced the fatality gap substantially. These facts support a design-cost claim and constrain a single-cause account. [Opened artifact: complete IIHS technical explanation](https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/improving-safety-for-women-requires-more-than-a-female-crash-test-dummy).

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Crash testing began from a midsize male reference, PPE studies find performance losses from poor fit, and the resulting costs fall disproportionately on women and smaller-bodied workers even as modern adjustable standards reduce some of the mismatch.

## T5. Collector

**Verdict: NOT SUPPORTED as an active extraction axis.** Employers and markets allocate wages, authority, and opportunities partly along height. The evidence supports selection, sorting, and default-based friction. It identifies no stable actor that systematically captures the value lost by shorter people or tall women.

### Best evidence

1. **Case, Paxson, and Islam (2009), *Economics Letters* — scholarly secondary; British Household Panel Survey.** The raw premium was 1.5% per inch for men and women. Education explained half; sorting into higher-status occupations and industries explained half of the remainder. Exact quote: “Most of the premium is explained by higher average educational attainment and sorting.” [Opened artifact: full PubMed Central article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2678677/).

2. **Rietveld, Hessels, and van der Zwan (2015), *Economics & Human Biology* — scholarly secondary; German panel.** Each centimeter predicted 0.39% higher hourly earnings for employees and 0.52% for the self-employed; education explained 28–42%. A premium among self-employed workers weakens a model in which employers alone collect from short employees. Exact quote: “A height premium in terms of work and life satisfaction exists.” [Opened artifact: ScienceDirect article record and abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1570677X15000143).

3. **Sohn (2015), *Economics & Human Biology* — scholarly secondary; Indonesian panel.** Ten centimeters predicted 7.5% higher earnings for men and 13.0% for women after extensive productivity controls. Sector estimates included 12.3% for self-employed men and 18.0% for self-employed women. Exact quote: “Workers’ productivity does not fully explain the height premium.” The author interprets the pattern as suggestive of customer discrimination, which identifies a demand mechanism without demonstrating a collector. [Opened artifact: ScienceDirect article record and abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1570677X14000045).

4. **Judge and Cable (2004), *Journal of Applied Psychology* — scholarly secondary; meta-analysis.** Height related more strongly to subjective evaluation than objective performance, .31 versus .21. The authors identify possible perceptual bias and customer response while stating that their analysis does not resolve the mediating process. Exact quote: “This study does not open the black box.” [Opened artifact: complete article PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Daniel-Cable-2/publication/8545837_The_Effect_of_Physical_Height_on_Workplace_Success_and_Income_Preliminary_Test_of_a_Theoretical_Model/links/00b495390a8394aea7000000/The-Effect-of-Physical-Height-on-Workplace-Success-and-Income-Preliminary-Test-of-a-Theoretical-Model.pdf).

### Evidence against the prediction

Employers can pay a shorter worker less. The studies leave the wage difference distributed among education, occupation, productivity, customer preferences, bargaining, selection, and any employer rent. Positive height premiums among self-employed people rule out a necessary employer collector. Leadership and mating outcomes allocate status and partners without a measurable transfer. Product and safety mismatches impose costs on users, while the opened record contains no systematic matched-price audit or profit decomposition showing capture by manufacturers. A beneficiary cannot be inferred from a disadvantage alone.

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Height sorts wages, occupations, authority, and mating opportunities, but the evidence does not show a stable collector receiving a systematic transfer from shorter people or tall women.

## Classification

The left-handed mortality curve is a clear case of **historically enforced partition** producing a misleading population pattern. Schools and families imposed right-hand writing, and the practice declined by birth cohort. The nine-year mortality interpretation fails against prospective follow-up, twin comparisons, simulation, and cohort history.

Height qualifies as **friction from a design default** where a reference body shapes vehicles, PPE, and workstations. The cost is measurable for smaller-bodied workers and women in specific settings. Contemporary adjustable standards show that this friction is design-contingent and reducible.

Height also functions as a **selection axis** in wages, leadership, and mating. The evidence supports average advantages for taller people, often stronger for men, with substantial mediation by childhood conditions, cognition, education, occupation, and social evaluation. Limited occupational thresholds can enforce a height partition in specific settings. A general institutional rule assigning people to height classes is absent from the opened record. Employers, consumers, and markets participate in allocation. The active-extraction classification remains unsupported because the evidence demonstrates no stable collector or systematic transfer.

## Unverified remainder

This review could not verify a nationally representative US or UK year-by-year forced-conversion series, a contemporary all-age mortality meta-analysis, a causal CEO-selection effect, a general threshold beyond which women’s height becomes a labor-market penalty, a height-specific experiment showing masculine-role backlash against tall women, or a profit decomposition identifying a collector. Those claims should remain off air until an underlying dataset, experimental study, archival policy record, or market accounting artifact is opened.
