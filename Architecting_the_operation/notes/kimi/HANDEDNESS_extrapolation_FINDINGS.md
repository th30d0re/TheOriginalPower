# Handedness extrapolation: findings

**Research date:** 2026-10-01  
**Model:** Codex (GPT-5)  
**Scope:** Evidence was classified from artifacts opened during this review. Quotes are limited to fewer than 25 words. “Supported” means the cited record supports the stated prediction. It does not establish that handedness has the same historical scale, political organization, or extraction mechanism as the manuscript’s six identity axes.

## H1. Collisions

**Verdict: MIXED.** Specific tools and interfaces embody a right-handed default, and some studies measure associated performance or posture differences. The general claim that left-handed people sustain more injuries is inconsistent across stronger and later studies.

### Best evidence

1. **Coren (1989), American Journal of Public Health — scholarly secondary; retrospective survey.** The opened PubMed record reports 1,896 Canadian college students. Left-handers had higher odds of a medically attended injury during the prior two years: OR 1.89, 95% CI 1.39–2.58. Left-handed men had OR 2.35, 95% CI 1.25–4.43, for motor-vehicle injuries. Exact quote: “Left-handers were more likely to report having an injury requiring medical attention.” [Opened artifact: PubMed abstract and bibliographic record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2751023/).

2. **Tomizawa et al. (2026), Journal of Occupational Health — scholarly secondary; prospective cohort.** Among 4,016 Japanese workers, the adjusted occupational-accident IRR was 1.14 for left-handed workers versus right-handed workers (95% CI 0.63–2.07; p=.660). Mixed-handed workers had IRR 1.75 (95% CI 1.14–2.70; p=.011). Exact quote: “left-handed workers exhibited no elevated risk.” This is the strongest direct evidence against a general left-handed occupational-injury excess. [Opened artifact: full Oxford Academic article](https://academic.oup.com/joh/article/68/1/uiag019/8650443).

3. **Porac (1993), Neuropsychologia — scholarly secondary; case-control study.** The study compared 486 people with unilateral hand injuries against 402 age- and sex-matched controls. Exact quote: “The risk of hand injury was similar for right- and left-handers.” Injury followed frequency of hand use more closely than left-handedness. [Opened artifact: PubMed abstract and bibliographic record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8502371/).

4. **Mouloua et al. (2017), Human Factors and Ergonomics Society proceedings — scholarly secondary; motor-task experiment.** Right-handed participants were significantly faster in a mouse-clicking task. The authors identify mouse design and differential training as competing explanations. Exact quote: “right-handed participants were significantly faster than the left-handed participants.” The abstract supplies no effect magnitude. [Opened artifact: SAGE article abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1541931213601787).

### Evidence against the prediction

The prospective 2026 cohort found no excess among left-handed workers. The 1993 case-control study also found equal hand-injury risk. These results directly limit the 1989 retrospective finding. They also separate mixed-handedness from left-handedness, a distinction that older claims often collapse.

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Right-handed design creates documented mismatches for some tasks, while the best recent workplace study found no significant accident excess among left-handed workers and did find one among mixed-handed workers.

## H2. I²R loss

**Verdict: MIXED.** Time, error, posture, injury, and workload can be measured in handedness-sensitive tasks. The available results do not establish a uniform cumulative burden for left-handed people, and adaptation sometimes removes or reverses the measured disadvantage.

### Best evidence

1. **Park and Park (2015), Journal of Physical Therapy Science — scholarly secondary; within-subject motion study.** Eighteen left-handers used both scissor types. Left-handed scissors reduced wrist flexion, yet task time did not differ significantly and recorded errors were higher with left-handed scissors: 1.39±1.29 versus 0.72±0.67. Exact quote: “The time taken … was not significantly different.” The article’s abstract and conclusion claim improved accuracy with left-handed scissors, which conflicts with its results table and discussion. [Opened artifact: complete two-page journal PDF](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jpts/27/3/27_jpts-2014-649/_pdf/-char/en).

2. **Hoffmann, Chang, and Yim (1997), Applied Ergonomics — scholarly secondary; randomized clinical trial.** Every surveyed student computer had its mouse on the right. Cursor-movement times showed no significant penalty for left-handers. Exact quote: “left-handed users were not significantly disadvantaged.” [Opened artifact: PubMed abstract and bibliographic record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9414363/).

3. **Tomizawa et al. (2026), Journal of Occupational Health — scholarly secondary; prospective cohort.** Injury requiring medical treatment occurred in 6.2% of left-handed, 5.0% of right-handed, and 9.8% of mixed-handed workers. Adjusted excess risk was significant only for mixed-handed workers. Exact quote: “the precise causes of the accidents remain to be established.” [Opened artifact: full Oxford Academic article](https://academic.oup.com/joh/article/68/1/uiag019/8650443).

4. **Liang et al. (2019), Experimental Brain Research — scholarly secondary; reaching experiment.** Under the highest cognitive-perceptual load, left-handers crossed the midline with their dominant hand more often than right-handers, F(1,19)=4.817, p=.042. The authors interpret those reaches as mechanically costly hand-choice errors. Exact quote: “greater high-cost movements that reached to extremes of the contralateral workspace.” This measures task-specific planning behavior; it does not quantify everyday cognitive loss caused by right-handed design. [Opened artifact: PubMed abstract, figures, and bibliographic record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415290/).

### Evidence against the prediction

The mouse trial found effective adaptation to a universal right-side setup. The scissor experiment found improved wrist position alongside more recorded errors with the nominally proper tool, and its prose contradicts its table. The prospective injury study found no significant excess for left-handers. The record therefore supports localized costs and adaptation costs, with no defensible aggregate estimate.

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Handedness friction can appear as awkward posture, slower control, planning effort, or injury, though experiments also show substantial adaptation and no consistent population-wide loss.

## H3. Enforced partition

**Verdict: SUPPORTED.** Germany and Taiwan provide direct documentation of forced conversion. Measured neurological differences persist after conversion; evidence for adverse functional or economic outcomes remains mixed.

### Best evidence

1. **Guber (2019), Labour Economics — scholarly secondary; German Socio-Economic Panel.** The sample covers birth cohorts from 1920 through 1997. Switching among left-handers was about 90% for cohorts born around 1950, about 60% by 1960, and nearly zero by 1990; 57% of left-handers across cohorts reported switching. Germany’s 1960 school guidance treated writing as a right-hand activity, and teachers received instructions supporting left-handed writing in 1987. Exact quote: “Starting among cohorts born in 1950, switching rates decline monotonically.” [Opened artifact: full ScienceDirect article text](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927537118301246).

2. **Meng (2007), Laterality — scholarly secondary; Taiwanese child sample.** Of 211 innately left-handed children, 121 had been forced to convert. The article prints 59.3%, while 121/211 equals 57.3%; the count and printed percentage are internally inconsistent. Current handedness was reported for 114 of the 121 children, including 20 classified as mixed-handed (17.5%). Exact quote: “121/211 … had been forced to convert to right-handedness.” [Opened artifacts: PubMed indexed abstract and Chang Gung University publication record](https://pure.lib.cgu.edu.tw/zh/publications/the-rate-of-handedness-conversion-and-related-factors-in-left-han-3/).

3. **Siebner et al. (2002), Journal of Neuroscience — scholarly secondary; PET study.** Eleven German converted left-handers and eleven matched right-handers performed handwriting tasks. Kinematics did not differ, while converted left-handers retained a more bilateral activation pattern after decades of right-hand writing. Exact quote: “persisting differences in the functional neuroanatomy of handwriting.” [Opened artifact: PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11923446/).

4. **Guber (2019), Labour Economics — scholarly secondary; long-run outcomes.** Switched German left-handers performed as well as or better than right-handers on observed labor-market outcomes. The author states that selection and reverse causality prevent a clean causal interpretation; instrumental-variable estimates were mostly statistically insignificant. Exact quote: “switched left-handers perform equally well or even better in the labor market.” [Opened artifact: full ScienceDirect article text](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0927537118301246).

### Countries and periods licensed by the opened record

- **Germany:** coercive school and family switching is measured across cohorts born 1920–1997; policy and cohort evidence place its steep decline between the 1950 and 1990 birth cohorts.
- **Taiwan:** forced conversion was directly measured in 121 of 211 left-handed children in a 2007 study; the abstract does not identify the children’s birth years and contains the percentage discrepancy reported above.
- **United States and United Kingdom:** Guber’s literature review documents advocacy and practice in the early twentieth century, including a 1946 New York education official. This is a scholarly summary of earlier historical work.
- **China and Japan:** this review did not open an artifact that establishes a national forced-conversion rate or a reliable timeline. Claims for these countries remain unverified.

### Evidence against the prediction

Conversion did not erase underlying motor lateralization. The imaging studies establish altered or persistent activation patterns without demonstrating clinical harm. Guber found no labor-market penalty for the converted group and warns that family selection may explain the association.

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Schools and families enforced right-hand writing in documented settings, with German switching falling from roughly 90% in older cohorts to nearly zero by 1990 and a Taiwanese study recording forced conversion in 121 of 211 left-handed children.

## H4. Extraction

**Verdict: NOT SUPPORTED.** The record supports niche-product scarcity and inconsistent earnings correlations. It does not identify a stable transfer, a consistent beneficiary, or a documented system that extracts value from left-handed people as a class.

### Best evidence

1. **Goodman (2014), Journal of Economic Perspectives — scholarly secondary; multiple longitudinal datasets.** Across roughly 40,000 young adults in the United States and United Kingdom, left-handers earned 10–12% less annually. Observable cognitive skills and behavioral problems explained much of the association. Exact quote: “lefties have 10-12 percent lower annual earnings than righties.” The paper attributes the gap primarily to measured human-capital differences, not tool-design rents. [Opened artifact: American Economic Association article record and abstract](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.28.4.193).

2. **Ruebeck, Harrington, and Moffitt (2007), Laterality — scholarly secondary; US longitudinal survey.** College-educated left-handed men received a positive wage effect, strongest in the lower half of the conditional wage distribution; women showed no effect. A later review reports an approximately 15% premium for college-educated left-handed men. Exact quote: “This positive wage effect is strongest among those who have lower than average earnings.” [Opened artifact: NBER article record for the published study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12387).

3. **Denny and O’Sullivan (2007), Journal of Human Resources — scholarly secondary; UK cohort.** Left-handed men earned more, with manual workers receiving a slightly larger premium; left-handed women earned significantly less. A later peer-reviewed synthesis reports approximately +8% for men and −8% for women. Exact quote: “there is a positive effect on male earnings.” [Opened artifact: University of Warwick repository record and peer-reviewed abstract](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/32251/).

4. **Sartarelli (2016), PLOS ONE — scholarly secondary; laboratory labor-market experiment.** Among 432 students, including 35 left-handers, experimental payoffs did not differ significantly by handedness. Exact quote: “payoffs … are not significantly different for L.” The study has high internal validity and low population generalizability. [Opened artifact: full PLOS ONE article](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0164412).

### Price premium and manufacturing-cost findings

No peer-reviewed price audit comparing matched left- and right-handed consumer products was located. The scattered retail listings returned by the search were unsuitable for matched price comparisons and cannot establish a general “left-handed tax.” No opened study documents that choosing a single right-handed human-interface design produces a manufacturer cost saving, or quantifies the saving. General manufacturing logic makes extra tooling and low-volume variants plausible cost drivers; that inference does not demonstrate extraction.

### Evidence against the prediction

Earnings results point in opposite directions by dataset, sex, and education. Goodman’s negative association is largely mediated by observed skills and behavior. Other studies find male premiums or null experimental results. None identifies an actor collecting the gap. Specialized product premiums, where present, may reflect production scale, tooling, retail markup, or market power; the opened record does not decompose them.

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Researchers have found negative, positive, and null earnings differences by handedness, and the available record does not establish a systematic left-handed price tax or an extracting beneficiary.

## H5. Ability linkage

**Verdict: MIXED.** Universal-design and accessibility guidance explicitly treats handedness and forced use of the nondominant hand as design concerns. The opened disability scholarship supplies the environmental-barrier logic. The standards reviewed do not classify ordinary left-handedness itself as a disability or create a handedness-specific conformance category.

### Best evidence

1. **ISO/IEC Guide 71:2014 — primary standard.** The guide identifies “reduced ability caused by use of non-dominant hand (left or right)” and recommends products that support either or both hands. Exact quote: “products shaped to facilitate easy grasping, lifting and carrying with either or both hands.” [Opened artifact: complete ISO/IEC Guide 71 PDF hosted by the Japanese Industrial Standards Committee](https://www.jisc.go.jp/international/sr/ISO_IEC_Guide_71_2014.pdf).

2. **Center for Universal Design, Principles of Universal Design, version 2.0 — primary design framework.** Principle 2, Flexibility in Use, directly states: “Accommodate right- or left-handed access and use.” It also calls for choice in methods of use and support for accuracy and precision. [Opened artifact: RL Mace Universal Design Institute principles page](https://www.udinstitute.org/principles).

3. **World Health Organization, International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health — primary intergovernmental framework.** ICF codes environmental factors as barriers or facilitators from the affected person’s perspective. Exact quote: “functioning and disability of an individual occurs in a context.” ICF supplies a framework for describing hostile design as an environmental barrier; the opened material does not mention handedness specifically. [Opened artifact: WHO ICF overview and official browser](https://www.who.int/classifications/international-classification-of-functioning-disability-and-health).

4. **W3C, WCAG 2 and Input Modalities — primary web standard and official explanatory guidance.** WCAG addresses pointer gestures, target size, and concurrent inputs for motor accessibility. Exact quote: “Make it easier for users to operate functionality through various inputs beyond keyboard.” The opened WCAG guidance contains no handedness-specific conformance criterion. [Opened artifact: W3C Understanding Guideline 2.5](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/input-modalities.html).

### Evidence against the prediction

The standards support hand-neutral design through broad operability and flexibility rules. ISO Guide 71 frames difficulty around nondominant-hand use, which can affect people of either handedness. WHO and WCAG do not name left-handedness as an impairment or protected class. The social-model analogy is conceptually licensed at the level of environmental barriers; a formal disability classification is not.

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Universal-design guidance explicitly requires right- and left-handed use, and accessibility frameworks recognize environmental barriers, though ordinary left-handedness is not classified as a disability in the standards reviewed.

## H6. Current example: Apple iPhone Duo

**Verdict: SUPPORTED.** Apple announced the iPhone Duo on 9 September 2026, with preorders scheduled for 16 October and availability for 23 October. Official design guidance explicitly optimizes the closed interface for the right thumb. A 9to5Mac report describes the hardware and UI as primarily right-hand biased. Real-world left-handed testing remains preliminary because retail availability had not begun by the research date.

### Best evidence

1. **Apple Newsroom (2026) — primary company announcement.** Apple describes a 5.4-inch outer display, 7.6-inch inner display, central hinge, side-button Touch ID, and October 2026 availability. Exact quote: “Pre-orders begin Friday, October 16, with availability beginning Friday, October 23.” [Opened artifact: Apple’s announcement](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/09/apple-unveils-iphone-duo/).

2. **Apple Developer Tech Talk (2026) — primary design documentation.** Apple’s designers say that controls on the closed outer display move to the right side. Exact quote: “make them easier to reach with your right thumb.” The talk also says left-side split-view controls move left, showing partial adaptation on the inner display. [Opened artifact: transcript of Apple’s “Design for iPhone Duo” talk](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/tech-talks/111466/).

3. **9to5Mac (25 September 2026) — tertiary technology reporting with linked hands-on video.** The report identifies the flat hinge edge on the left, opening edge on the right, and right-side dock. Exact quote: “the new foldable is clearly designed for right-handed use primarily.” It also reports that Apple had not announced a setting to move the dock left. [Opened artifact: full 9to5Mac report](https://9to5mac.com/2026/09/25/iphone-duo-video-shows-left-handed-use-for-new-model/).

### Evidence against the prediction

Apple’s inner-display split view moves controls to the outer edge of whichever pane is active, including the left edge for a left-side app. The independent hands-on video found that a user could reach the right-side dock from the left, though the demonstrator was right-handed. No study has measured left-handed error, time, grip stability, strain, or injury on production hardware.

**Licensed on-air sentence:** Apple’s own iPhone Duo guidance says the closed interface moves controls right for the right thumb, while its inner split view can place controls on the left; measured left-handed usability data are not yet available.

## Classification

Handedness is best classified as **friction from a design default**, supported at moderate strength by ergonomic studies, interface research, and explicit universal-design rules. It also qualifies as **a historically enforced partition** in documented national settings, supported strongly for Germany and Taiwan and less precisely elsewhere. The evidence does **not** support handedness as **an active extraction axis**: product-price evidence lacks a matched systematic audit, wage findings conflict across populations, and no stable collecting actor or transfer mechanism appears in the opened record. Within the framework, handedness offers a credible small-scale test of default-driven collision and adaptation. Its historical enforcement was real. Its present economic structure lacks the evidence required for an extraction claim.

## Unverified remainder

This review could not confirm a cross-national forced-conversion timeline, a representative price premium for matched left-handed products, a quantified manufacturer saving from a single right-handed design, or production-device usability measurements for the iPhone Duo. Claims in those categories should remain off air until an underlying dataset, standard, archival record, or controlled study is opened.
