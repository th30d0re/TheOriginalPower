## 1. Entry into US press and law

1. **Mexican usage preceded the federal campaign. Established, scholarly secondary.** Isaac Campos describes Mexican botanical writing of the 1850s using *mariguana* and *rosa maría*. His account places the subsequent stigmatization of cannabis within nineteenth-century Mexican medical, social, and newspaper discourse. This supports Mexican Spanish usage well before Anslinger. The word’s ultimate linguistic derivation and its first-ever US occurrence were not established in this investigation. [Campos, *Home Grown* (UNC Press, 2012), introduction, pp. 1–5, publisher’s excerpt](https://uncpressblog.com/2012/05/14/excerpt-home-grown-by-isaac-campos/).
   - **Artifact opened:** publisher-authorized HTML excerpt, retrieved with `curl` after the browser research tool returned an access error; the excerpt identifies its book page range.

2. **A documented route was newspaper republication from Mexico. Established for the example; broader chronology rests on the historian’s research.** The *Cairo Bulletin*, October 8, 1910, p. 5, printed “Smoking That Maddens,” used *marihuana*, attributed violent and delusional behavior to smoking it, and credited the *Mexican Herald*. This gives direct evidence of the name and associated fear narrative circulating in a US newspaper by 1910. [Library of Congress page image](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn93055779/1910-10-08/ed-1/?sp=5).
   - **Artifact opened:** LOC newspaper image through its IIIF service; full-page date and printed folio read, followed by an enlarged view of the bottom-left article. **Tier:** primary/contemporaneous press; evidence of the published allegation, with no validation of its pharmacological assertions.

3. **The 1910s record contains several names and regional concentrations. Established as Campos’s research findings.** Campos traces the cross-border press route to the 1890s. His study of 1,225 articles from 1910–1919 finds *hashish* especially prominent and *marihuana* concentrated in Southwestern coverage, including El Paso, San Antonio, and Phoenix. His findings establish circulation before the 1930s and variation across outlets and regions. [Campos, “Is the Word Marijuana Racist?”, sections “Methodology and Findings” and “Marihuana Stories”](https://www.thedrugpage.org/racism-marijuana-nomenclature).
   - **Artifact opened:** historian’s HTML research essay and linked source locators. **Tier:** named historian’s original research presentation, outside a peer-reviewed journal; the entire newspaper corpus was not independently reproduced.

4. **Early state restrictions used botanical and other names. Established in scholarly secondary evidence.** Massachusetts’s 1911 pharmacy law included *cannabis indica* and *cannabis sativa*. California’s 1913 law, effective August 10, referred to preparations of hemp and *loco-weed*. These examples require preserving each statute’s terminology when describing the early legal history. [Dale H. Gieringer, *The Origins of Cannabis Prohibition in California*, revised October 2019, pp. 24 and 29, especially n. 128](https://www.canorml.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/Cal-Origins-MJ-Proh-2019.pdf).
   - **Artifact opened:** PDF text and rendered pages with printed folios 24 and 29. **Tier:** scholarly secondary, author’s revised version of an article originally published in *Contemporary Drug Problems* 26(2), 1999; this access establishes the revised text, without asserting fresh peer review of its revisions. Original state session-law pages were not opened.

5. **“Marihuana” appeared in municipal law by 1915. Established, primary.** An El Paso ordinance prohibited specified dealings and possession involving “Marihuana or Indian Hemp.” It was approved June 3, 1915, and printed in the *El Paso Herald*, June 4, 1915, p. 14. This is a municipal example; it supplies no claim about the first state statute using the word. [LOC ordinance image](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn88084272/1915-06-04/ed-1/?sp=14).
   - **Artifact opened:** LOC IIIF image, full-page date and folio, then enlarged ordinance column. Sections 1–3 and the approval date were read visually.

6. **The federal Act’s spelling is “Marihuana.” Established, primary.** The August 2, 1937 Act, chapter 553, 50 Stat. 551–556, uses *marihuana* and defines it by reference to *Cannabis sativa L.* in §1(b). [Official *Statutes at Large* scan, p. 551](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-50/pdf/STATUTE-50-Pg551-2.pdf#page=1).
   - **Artifact opened:** GovInfo PDF; rendered printed p. 551 read visually. Preserve *Marihuana Tax Act of 1937* when naming the law.

## 2. Documented racial associations in the 1937 records

These quotations document historical rhetoric. Their allegations about racial groups and drug effects remain the speakers’ claims.

1. **House: Anslinger submitted an explicitly disparaging letter about Spanish-speaking residents. Established, primary.** Floyd K. Baskette, city editor of the *Alamosa Daily Courier*, wrote to the Bureau on September 4, 1936. His letter calls residents “degenerate Spanish-speaking residents” and attributes alleged deficiencies to racial and social conditions. The hearing identifies the subsequent materials as submissions by Anslinger. Cite the editor as author and Anslinger as submitter. [House Ways and Means, *Taxation of Marihuana*, H.R. 6385, April 27–30 and May 4, 1937, printed pp. 29 and 32](https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c8b75eff-aa91-47f3-9a55-7914cc4ddbe3/MARIHUANA%20TAX%20ACT%20HEARINGS%20APRIL%20THRU%20-0d5de93.pdf#page=36); [searchable transcription](https://www.druglibrary.net/schaffer/hemp/taxact/t10a.htm).
   - **Artifact opened:** independent reviewer rendered and read printed pp. 29 and 32 of the Google-digitized University of Michigan copy, available through a private mirror. The entire Baskette letter is on p. 32. The transcription assisted discovery.

2. **House: an inserted paper explicitly associated the terminology with Black Americans. Established association; limited evidence of intent.** Eugene Stanley’s “Marihuana as a Developer of Criminals,” beginning on printed p. 37, says: “In the South, amongst the Negroes, it is termed ‘mooter.’” Its presence establishes a racial identification in the material Anslinger supplied. That sentence alone provides limited evidence about discriminatory motivation. [Same House scan, p. 37](https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/c8b75eff-aa91-47f3-9a55-7914cc4ddbe3/MARIHUANA%20TAX%20ACT%20HEARINGS%20APRIL%20THRU%20-0d5de93.pdf#page=41).
   - **Artifact opened:** independent reviewer read the rendered page and printed folio. **Tier:** primary/contemporaneous paper reproduced in the official hearing record.

3. **Senate: Anslinger presented a crime narrative that identified Black participants. Established, primary.** A March 18, 1937 letter from Richard Hartshorne describes a case in which “one colored young man killed another” and attributes the brutality to cannabis. Anslinger introduced the letter to support the asserted connection with crime. This establishes the racial identification and his use of the account; claims about its persuasive impact require further evidence. [Senate Finance subcommittee, *Taxation of Marihuana*, H.R. 6906, July 12, 1937, p. 11](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/75HrgMarihuana.pdf#page=15).
   - **Artifact opened:** official Senate PDF, rendered printed p. 11, independently read by both agents. The poor OCR failed searches for some racial terms; the image contains the quoted passage.

4. **The widely circulated “satanic jazz” attribution remains unverified.** Penn State Special Collections reports that the cited “Marijuana Users – Musicians, 1933–1937” folder contains no document bearing the alleged quotation. Its answer also notes publications citing 1937 testimony and describes the attribution as unresolved. [Penn State Libraries, FAQ 391108, answer by Alex Bainbridge, updated August 7, 2026](https://psu.libanswers.com/faq/391108).
   - **Artifact opened:** institution’s HTML answer via `curl`. **Tier:** primary institutional statement about its archival inquiry. It supports excluding the quotation. It does not independently verify other allegations about Anslinger.

## 3. Scholarly counterpoint and the limits of each interpretation

1. **Campos, *Home Grown* (2012): established account of an earlier Mexican history; implications for US causation require qualification.** The introduction documents Mexican stigmatizing narratives and the 1920 Mexican prohibition. This supplies a chronology and a source of ideas that circulated northward. It challenges accounts that place the creation of the fear narrative entirely within the US federal campaign. Its introductory argument leaves the causal weight of particular US officials and laws for further investigation. [Publisher’s excerpt, pp. 1–5](https://uncpressblog.com/2012/05/14/excerpt-home-grown-by-isaac-campos/). **Tier/access:** scholarly secondary; authorized excerpt opened as described above.

2. **Campos’s reassessment: contested causal interpretation, accurately attributable to him.** His article challenges the hypothesis that Mexican migrant workers’ cannabis use and efforts to control them explain US prohibition generally. The abstract describes weak original evidence, limited use in immigrant communities, and alternative explanatory factors. [“Mexicans and the Origins of Marijuana Prohibition in the United States: A Reassessment,” *Social History of Alcohol and Drugs* 32 (2018), pp. 6–37, DOI: 10.1086/SHAD3201006](https://doi.org/10.1086/SHAD3201006).
   - **Artifact opened:** publisher’s DOI landing page and abstract; the page gives January 22, 2019 as its online publication date. **Tier:** peer-reviewed scholarly secondary. Full-article retrieval failed, so this report limits its account of that article to the abstract.

3. **The intentional foreign-name story: contested; direct evidence remains unverified.** Campos’s nomenclature essay specifically disputes the Hearst-led naming story and reports finding no evidence for a deliberate campaign to introduce the name. The earlier press evidence supports prior circulation. Establishing a later official’s motive for selecting a particular word would require direct evidence of that choice. [Campos, “The R Word” and concluding discussion](https://www.thedrugpage.org/racism-marijuana-nomenclature). **Tier/access:** named historian’s research essay, HTML opened. This conclusion concerns an alleged naming strategy; the documented racist uses in the hearings remain established.

4. **George Fisher challenges the scale and public influence attributed to racial appeals. Contested historical interpretation.** Fisher acknowledges the Alamosa material and racialized federal statements, then questions evidence of their contemporary dissemination and influence. He argues that protection of youth dominated Anslinger’s public campaign. His analysis directly tests the manuscript’s claim about manufacturing public consent. [“Racial Myths of the Cannabis War,” *Boston University Law Review* 101 (2021), pp. 933–977, especially pp. 940–944](https://www.bu.edu/bulawreview/files/2021/07/FISHER.pdf#page=8).
   - **Artifact opened:** journal-hosted, digitally typeset PDF, including pp. 942–944 and their source notes. **Tier:** scholarly secondary, named legal historian in a law review; standard law-review editorial review differs from conventional journal peer review. Fisher’s interpretation requires assessment alongside the surviving records; it supplies no proof that racial prejudice had zero influence.

**What the evidence establishes:** particular officials and contributors used racial classifications and disparagement in cannabis-control advocacy. **What remains contested:** the relative causal importance of those appeals, their reception, and a deliberate strategy to introduce a foreign-sounding name. The preferred term *cannabis* is an editorial decision that can be explained through the documented examples.

## 4. Institutions changing terminology on these grounds

1. **Washington enacted an explicit change with a stated racial rationale. Established, primary.** Second Substitute House Bill 1210, chapter 16, Laws of 2022, §1, directs replacement of *marijuana* with *cannabis* throughout the Revised Code of Washington and states that the former term’s US use has discriminatory origins. The section characterizes the changes as technical. [Enacted session law, §1](https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/biennium/2021-22/Htm/Bills/Session%20Laws/House/1210-S2.SL.htm).
   - **Artifact opened:** official enacted-law HTML via `curl`; §1 read directly. This verifies the legislature’s action and stated rationale. Its historical characterization remains a legislative finding subject to the scholarly qualifications above.

2. **Washington’s Liquor and Cannabis Board implemented the change in its rules. Established, primary.** WSR 22-14-111, filed July 6, 2022 and effective August 6, 2022, repeats the discriminatory-origin rationale and describes replacement throughout its Title 314 rules. [Official rule-making order, opening “Purpose” paragraphs](https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/law/wsr/2022/14/22-14-111.htm).
   - **Artifact opened:** official HTML rule text. These are two documented actions within one state. This investigation establishes no universal institutional consensus or nationwide renaming.
