### Book internal tension to flag

The book says the Buffer Class was created by the 1705 codes (tex:3362, 3372, 3262) and also that pre-1865 had "no Buffer Class" (tex:1277, 1115). The author's "Buffer and Enforcement were one tier at first" matches the Proto-Enforcement reading at tex:3262 and tex:663-670. It does not match tex:4880-4881, which already has four distinct tiers by 1704--1865.

### Verdicts on the author's four claims

1. Slave patrols drew on poor whites: **PARTLY / DISAGREE**. They drew on the white male population by law, and Hadden's county data put patrollers in the middle, not the bottom. Rich owners often paid a substitute or skipped duty (Reichel). The book itself says "recruited from the lower classes" (tex:4942, 4944), so the book and the author agree with each other and sit on the poor-white side of Hadden. Safer line: "ordinary white men, and the owners could pay someone else to ride for them."
2. Policing after the 13th Amendment became the institutional Enforcement tier, especially in the South: **PARTLY, and DISAGREE with the book's timing**. The book dates the institutional Enforcement tier to 1704--1865 ("Convert slave patrols into permanent state apparatus", tex:4880) and treats the 13th as the kernel loophole (tex:5265). The postwar South did channel Black Codes arrests and convict leasing through sheriffs and police (search snippet only). So "the exception clause fed a paid, permanent policing layer" is defensible; "the tier was born after the 13th" contradicts tex:4880-4881. `BOOK-UPDATE?` if the author wants the postwar institutionalization as the birth.
3. Buffer and Enforcement were one tier before that: **PARTLY SUPPORTED by the book's own "proto" framing** (tex:3262, tex:663-670), but the book also separates them in the antebellum table (tex:2484) and in the four-tier ordering (tex:4975, eq. 6.2). Cleanest accurate framing: same people, two roles. A Buffer member occupies the Enforcement node "for the duration of that act" (tex:667).
4. Paid for it through formal policing: **PARTLY**. Patrol pay and exemptions existed already before the war (Reichel: $25-$50 a year in 1734 South Carolina). Postwar pay through sheriff fee systems is supported only by a search snippet on Blackmon.

### Spoken-register summary (for the writer)

After the Civil War, the same job turned into a paid, permanent one: sheriffs and police, working through laws that let a person be arrested for being unemployed, and then leased out to work."

**Verdict Q1:** PARTLY. The two named framings conflict with tex:4880-4881 on timing.
