### Chronological Catalog

#### 1486 — Establishment of the *Casa dos Escravos* in Lisbon
* **Event:** King João the Second of Portugal creates the *Casa dos Escravos* (House of the Slaves) as a distinct department of the *Casa da Guiné e Mina* in Lisbon. The crown mandates that all enslaved Africans arriving on Portuguese vessels pass through this centralized customs warehouse for registration, religious baptism, physical branding, and collection of the royal fifth (*quinto*).
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In fourteen eighty-six, the Portuguese Crown opened the House of the Slaves in Lisbon. Every captive arriving from the African coast had to be logged, branded, and taxed under royal supervision.
* **Sources:** A.C. de C.M. Saunders, *A Social History of Black Slaves and Freedmen in Portugal 1441–1555* (Cambridge University Press, 1982), pp. 47–59; `tex:2763`, `tex:2790`.
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Archival records, Portuguese royal decrees).

#### 1493–1499 — Colonization and Sugar Monoculture of São Tomé
* **Event:** Álvaro de Caminha initiates the permanent colonization of São Tomé in the Gulf of Guinea under royal charter. The Portuguese Crown establishes the world's first plantation economy powered exclusively by enslaved African laborers imported from the Kingdom of Kongo and the Niger Delta. São Tomé serves as the operational prototype for Atlantic sugar cultivation and captive transshipment.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In fourteen ninety-three, Portugal turned the equatorial island of São Tomé into a brutal prototype. It became the first economy in history dedicated entirely to growing sugar with enslaved African labor.
* **Date Nuance:** King João the Second granted the island to João de Paiva in fourteen eighty-five; successful demographic colonization and large-scale sugar planting began under Caminha in fourteen ninety-three.
* **Sources:** Robert Garfield, *A History of São Tomé Island, 1470–1655* (Edwin Mellen Press, 1992); David Eltis, *The Rise of African Slavery in the Americas* (Cambridge University Press, 2000), pp. 19–21.
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Colonial registers, sugar export receipts).

#### 1501 — First Documented African Captives in Spanish Hispaniola
* **Event:** The Spanish Catholic Monarchs (Ferdinand and Isabella) issue instructions to Nicolás de Ovando, the incoming governor of Hispaniola, authorizing the introduction of enslaved Africans who had been Christianized in Spain (*ladinos*). This marks the earliest recorded arrival of enslaved Africans in the Americas.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In fifteen zero-one, Spanish authorities authorized the first transport of enslaved Africans to the Caribbean mines of Hispaniola.
* **Sources:** Hugh Thomas, *The Slave Trade: The Story of the Atlantic Slave Trade: 1440–1870* (Simon & Schuster, 1997), pp. 88–92; Carlos Esteban Deive, *La esclavitud del negro en Santo Domingo* (Museo del Hombre Dominicano, 1980).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Spanish royal cédulas).

#### 1512 — Portuguese Crown Issues the *Regimento* to the Kingdom of Kongo
* **Event:** King Manuel the First of Portugal issues a formal *regimento* (royal regulatory code) to the Christian king of Kongo, Afonso the First. The treaty outlines legal, religious, and commercial relations, formalizing the delivery of European craftsmen, ships, and weapons in exchange for captive laborers.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In fifteen twelve, Portugal signed a formal trade agreement with the Kingdom of Kongo, exchanging European manufactured goods and weapons for captured laborers.
* **Sources:** John K. Thornton, *The Kingdom of Kongo: Civil War and Transition 1641–1718* (University of Wisconsin Press, 1983); Linda M. Heywood, *Central Africans, Atlantic Creoles, and the Foundation of the Americas* (Cambridge University Press, 2007).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Vatican and Portuguese diplomatic archives).

#### 1518 — King Charles the First Grants the First Direct *Asiento*
* **Event:** King Charles the First of Spain (Emperor Charles the Fifth) issues a royal patent (*asiento*) to Laurent de Gouvenot (Lorenzo de Gorrevod), governor of Bresse. The contract licenses the shipment of four thousand enslaved Africans directly from the African coast to the Spanish Caribbean without landing in Seville. This decree inaugurates the direct transatlantic Middle Passage.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In fifteen eighteen, the King of Spain signed the first official contract for direct slave voyages. Four thousand human beings were shipped straight from Africa to the Caribbean, bypassing European ports entirely.
* **Date Nuance:** Older historiography occasionally cites fifteen seventeen; the formal patent bears the date August eighteen, fifteen eighteen.
* **Sources:** Georges Scelle, *La Traite Négrière aux Indes de Castille* (Larose, 1906); David Wheat, *Atlantic Africa and the Spanish Caribbean, 1570–1640* (University of North Carolina Press, 2016), pp. 24–28.
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Archivo General de Indias, Seville).

#### 1526 — King Afonso the First of Kongo Protests Depopulation by Slave Raids
* **Event:** King Afonso the First writes three surviving diplomatic letters to King João the Third of Portugal. Afonso documents that Portuguese merchants operate lawless kidnapping rings, seize noble subjects, corrupt local authorities, and leave his kingdom depopulated.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In fifteen twenty-six, King Afonso of Kongo wrote directly to the Portuguese monarch, warning that Portuguese merchants were corrupting his realm and kidnapping free noblemen off the roads.
* **Sources:** Louis Jadin and Mireille Dicorato, *Correspondance de Dom Afonso, roi du Congo* (Académie royale des sciences d'outre-mer, 1974); Adam Hochschild, *King Leopold's Ghost* (Houghton Mifflin, 1998), pp. 11–13.
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Original letters in Torre do Tombo, Lisbon).

#### 1532–1534 — First Brazilian Sugar Mill (*Engenho*) Constructed at São Vicente
* **Event:** Martim Afonso de Sousa establishes the settlement of São Vicente and constructs the first water-powered sugar mill in Brazil. King João the Third divides the Brazilian coast into fifteen hereditary captaincies in fifteen thirty-four. Planters rely initially on captured Indigenous laborers before shifting toward African captives as Indigenous populations collapse under warfare and epidemic diseases.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In fifteen thirty-two, Portuguese settlers built the first commercial sugar mill in Brazil. As epidemics destroyed Indigenous communities, planters turned to the African slave market to supply their cane fields.
* **Sources:** Stuart B. Schwartz, *Sugar Plantations in the Formation of Brazilian Society: Bahia, 1550–1835* (Cambridge University Press, 1985), pp. 15–28; Leslie Bethell (ed.), *Colonial Brazil* (Cambridge University Press, 1987).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Colonial municipal records).

#### 1575 — Paulo Dias de Novais Founds Luanda (Angola)
* **Event:** Paulo Dias de Novais arrives on the Angolan coast under a royal charter granting him hereditary rights as captain-governor. He founds São Paulo de Assunção de Luanda. The city evolves into the single largest port of captive human exportation in world history, establishing the primary umbilical route feeding Brazilian plantations.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In fifteen seventy-five, the Portuguese military founded the port city of Luanda in Angola. Over the next three centuries, Luanda became the largest human export harbor on the planet.
* **Date Nuance:** Novais landed in fifteen seventy-five; the formal municipal charter was confirmed in fifteen seventy-six.
* **Sources:** Joseph C. Miller, *Way of Death: Merchant Capitalism and the Angolan Slave Trade 1730–1830* (University of Wisconsin Press, 1988), pp. 140–148; David Birmingham, *Trade and Conflict in Angola* (Clarendon Press, 1966).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Portuguese colonial charters).

#### 1580–1640 — Iberian Union Unifies Spanish and Portuguese Slave Trade Networks
* **Event:** Philip the Second of Spain assumes the crown of Portugal, creating the Iberian Union. Portuguese merchant syndicates (*conversos* and contract capitalists) secure exclusive Spanish *asientos*. Portuguese slavers transport hundreds of thousands of Angolan captives directly into Spanish imperial ports, primarily Cartagena de Indias and Veracruz.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In fifteen eighty, the Spanish and Portuguese crowns merged under one monarch. Portuguese slave merchants secured lucrative monopoly contracts to supply hundreds of thousands of African laborers to Spanish silver mines and Caribbean ports.
* **Sources:** David Wheat, *Atlantic Africa and the Spanish Caribbean, 1570–1640* (University of North Carolina Press, 2016); Linda A. Newson and Susie Minchin, *From Capture to Sale: The Portuguese Slave Trade to Spanish South America in the Early Seventeenth Century* (Brill, 2007).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Notarial and customs registries in Seville, Cartagena, and Lisbon).

#### 1619 (August) — Privateer *White Lion* Lands Twenty African Captives in Virginia
* **Event:** The English privateer ship *White Lion*, captained by John Colyn Jope and sailing with letters of marque from the Prince of Orange, anchors at Point Comfort, Virginia. The crew offloads "20. and odd Negroes" seized from the Portuguese slaver *San Juan Bautista*, which was transporting captive Angolans from Luanda to Veracruz. The Virginia Company officials trade food and supplies for these captives, marking the documented start of African presence in British North America.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In August sixteen nineteen, an English privateer named the White Lion anchored at Point Comfort, Virginia, carrying twenty captured Angolans stolen from a Portuguese slave ship.
* **Sources:** John Rolfe, Letter to Sir Edwin Sandys (January 1620); John K. Thornton, "The African Experience of the '20. and Odd Negroes' Arriving in Virginia in 1619," *William and Mary Quarterly* 55, no. 3 (1998): 421–434; `tex:1389`, `tex:1456`.
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Virginia colonial correspondence; High Court of Admiralty records).

#### 1621 (June 3) — Dutch West India Company Chartered
* **Event:** The States-General of the Dutch Republic charters the *Geoctroyeerde Westindische Compagnie* (WIC). The company receives a state monopoly on Dutch trade and privateering across the Atlantic basin. The company devises the *Groot Desseyn* (Grand Design) to seize Iberian sugar production in Brazil and its captive labor supply points in West Africa.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In sixteen twenty-one, the Dutch government chartered the West India Company to break the Iberian monopoly on sugar and slaves.
* **Sources:** Johannes M. Postma, *The Dutch in the Atlantic Slave Trade, 1600–1815* (Cambridge University Press, 1990); P.C. Emmer, *The Dutch Slave Trade, 1500–1850* (Berghahn Books, 2006).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Dutch National Archives, The Hague).

#### 1627 — English Colonization of Barbados Commences
* **Event:** Captain John Powell claims Barbados for King Charles the First, and Henry Powell brings the first eighty English settlers aboard the *Olive Blossom*. The colony cultivates tobacco, indigo, and cotton, relying initially on white indentured servants imported from the British Isles.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In sixteen twenty-seven, English colonists settled Barbados. They began by planting tobacco and cotton with indentured English servants before turning to industrial sugar production.
* **Sources:** Richard S. Dunn, *Sugar and Slaves: The Rise of the Planter Class in the English West Indies, 1624–1713* (University of North Carolina Press, 1972), pp. 46–58; Hilary McD. Beckles, *White Servitude and Black Slavery in Barbados, 1627–1715* (University of Tennessee Press, 1989).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Barbados Council minutes).

#### 1630–1654 — Dutch Forces Invade Sugar Captaincies of Northeastern Brazil
* **Event:** The Dutch West India Company captures Olinda and Recife in Pernambuco, establishing the colony of New Holland (*Nieuw-Nederland*). Under Governor-General Johan Maurits van Nassau-Siegen (1637–1644), Dutch authorities administer the world's most lucrative sugar mills. The WIC realizes that sustaining Brazilian sugar yields requires direct control over West African slaving factories.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In sixteen thirty, the Dutch invaded northeastern Brazil, seizing control of the richest sugar fields in the Atlantic.
* **Sources:** C.R. Boxer, *The Dutch in Brazil, 1624–1654* (Clarendon Press, 1957); Ernst van den Boogaart, *Johan Maurits van Nassau-Siegen 1604–1679: A Humanist Prince in Europe and Brazil* (The Johan Maurits van Nassau Stichting, 1979).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (WIC archival ledgers).

#### 1637 (August) — Dutch West India Company Seizes Elmina Castle from Portugal
* **Event:** Dutch expeditionary forces under Colonel Hans Coine capture the fortress of São Jorge da Mina (Elmina Castle) on the Gold Coast from Portugal after one hundred and fifty-five years of Portuguese control. Elmina becomes the administrative center for Dutch transatlantic human trafficking.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In sixteen thirty-seven, Dutch military forces captured Elmina Castle from Portugal, securing a fortified slave factory on the West African coast.
* **Sources:** Johannes M. Postma, *The Dutch in the Atlantic Slave Trade, 1600–1815* (Cambridge University Press, 1990), pp. 56–79; P.E.H. Hair, *The Founding of the Castelo de São Jorge da Mina* (University of Wisconsin, 1994).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Dutch colonial war dispatches).

#### 1640s — Sugar Revolution Begins in Barbados
* **Event:** Barbados planters, led by James Drax, introduce sugar cane monoculture, windmills, and three-roller industrial crushers. Dutch merchants and Sephardic Jewish sugar masters expelled from Brazil provide the capital, machinery, and enslaved labor techniques. Between sixteen forty and sixteen sixty, Barbados eliminates indentured European labor and imports tens of thousands of captive Africans, converting the island into a Black-majority slave colony.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In the sixteen forties, Barbados underwent an industrial transformation known as the Sugar Revolution. Planters cleared the island for cane fields and replaced European indentured servants with thousands of enslaved Africans.
* **Sources:** Richard S. Dunn, *Sugar and Slaves* (1972), pp. 59–83; Richard B. Sheridan, *Sugar and Slavery: An Economic History of the British West Indies, 1623–1775* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1974).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Colonial agricultural tax ledgers).

#### 1660 / 1672 — Royal African Company Receives Formal Imperial Monopoly
* **Event:** King Charles the Second charters the Company of Royal Adventurers Trading into Africa in sixteen sixty, led by his brother James, Duke of York. Following financial collapse during the Anglo-Dutch Wars, the crown reconstitutes the venture in sixteen seventy-two as the **Royal African Company** (RAC). The RAC receives an exclusive English royal monopoly over trade on the African coast and brands captive Africans on the chest with the letters "DoY" (Duke of York) or "RAC".
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In sixteen seventy-two, the English Crown created the Royal African Company, headed by the King's brother, the Duke of York. The company held a complete monopoly, branding human beings with the royal initials before shipping them to Caribbean sugar estates.
* **Sources:** K.G. Davies, *The Royal African Company* (Longmans, Green and Co., 1957); William A. Pettigrew, *Freedom's Debt: The Royal African Company and the Politics of the Atlantic Slave Trade, 1672–1752* (University of North Carolina Press, 2013).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (British Treasury and Colonial Office archives).

#### 1661 — Barbados Assembly Enacts the Barbados Slave Code
* **Event:** The Barbados Assembly passes "An Act for the better ordering and governing of Negroes." The statute strips enslaved Africans of legal personhood, designates them as permanent chattel property, and establishes explicit corporal punishment structures. This legislation forms the legal archetype for slave codes adopted across the British Empire, including Jamaica (sixteen sixty-four), South Carolina (sixteen ninety-six and seventeen twelve), and Virginia (seventeen zero-five).
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In sixteen sixty-one, Barbados codified the first comprehensive British slave law. It treated human beings as livestock and became the legal model copied across Jamaica, South Carolina, and Virginia.
* **Sources:** Richard S. Dunn, *Sugar and Slaves* (1972), pp. 238–246; Edward B. Rugemer, "The Development of Blacks Codes in the British Caribbean," *Journal of American History* 100, no. 2 (2013): 428–452; `tex:3262`.
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Barbados statutory records).

#### 1673 — *Compagnie du Sénégal* Formed to Supply French Caribbean
* **Event:** Jean-Baptiste Colbert, finance minister to King Louis the Fourteenth, reorganizes French Atlantic commerce by establishing the *Compagnie du Sénégal*, succeeding the bankrupt *Compagnie des Indes Occidentales* (chartered sixteen sixty-four). The company receives a state monopoly to capture and deliver African laborers to the sugar plantations of Martinique, Guadeloupe, and western Hispaniola.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In sixteen seventy-three, France created the Company of Senegal, a state-backed monopoly tasked with delivering enslaved Africans to French sugar islands in the Caribbean.
* **Sources:** Robert Louis Stein, *The French Slave Trade in the Eighteenth Century: An Old Regime Business* (University of Wisconsin Press, 1979); Jean Mettas, *Répertoire des expéditions négrières françaises au XVIIIe siècle* (Société française d'histoire d'outre-mer, 1984).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (French naval archives, *Archives Nationales d'Outre-Mer*).

#### 1697 (September 20) — Treaty of Ryswick Formally Cedes Saint-Domingue to France
* **Event:** The Treaty of Ryswick concludes the Nine Years' War. Spain formally cedes the western third of Hispaniola to the French Crown, establishing the colony of **Saint-Domingue**. Over the eighteenth century, Saint-Domingue develops into the wealthiest and most lethal plantation colony in world history, dubbed the "Pearl of the Antilles."
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In sixteen ninety-seven, Spain surrendered the western third of Hispaniola to France under the Treaty of Ryswick. The French named the territory Saint-Domingue and transformed it into the most productive and brutal sugar colony on Earth.
* **Sources:** Laurent Dubois, *Avengers of the New World: The Story of the Haitian Revolution* (Harvard University Press, 2004); C.L.R. James, *The Black Jacobins: Toussaint L'Ouverture and the San Domingo Revolution* (Dial Press, 1938); `tex:14132`, `tex:15394`.
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (Treaty texts in European diplomatic archives).

#### 1698 — English Parliament Deregulates African Trade via the Ten Percent Act
* **Event:** English independent merchants defeat the Royal African Company's royal monopoly in Parliament by passing the "Ten Percent Act" (An Act to Settle the Trade to Africa). The statute opens the slave trade to all private English merchants ("separate traders") on condition of paying a ten percent ad valorem duty on African exports to maintain RAC coastal forts. The act triggers explosive commercial expansion in Bristol and Liverpool.
* **Spoken-Register Phrasing:** In sixteen ninety-eight, the English Parliament deregulated the slave trade. Stripping the Royal African Company of its monopoly allowed private merchant fleets in Bristol and Liverpool to launch hundreds of slaving voyages.
* **Sources:** William A. Pettigrew, *Freedom's Debt* (2013); Eric Williams, *Capitalism and Slavery* (University of North Carolina Press, 1944).
* **Confidence:** Tier 1 (English Parliamentary statutes).

*Methodological Note on Spanish Figures:* Spain maintained direct carrier operations primarily in the early sixteenth century and the nineteenth century. ### Period Analysis and Corporate Vehicles

#### 1. The Iberian Monopoly Era (1501–1600)
* **Dominant Carriers:** Portugal (154,191 embarked) and Spain (119,961 embarked).
* **Operating Apparatus:** The Portuguese Crown operated through the *Casa dos Escravos* and chartered individual captaincies. The Spanish Crown utilized *licencias* (individual permits) and early *asientos* awarded to Genoese, German (Welser), and Flemish banking syndicates. Captives were drawn from Upper Guinea (Senegambia) and West-Central Africa (Kongo and Angola).

#### 2. The Rise of Northern European Monopolies (1601–1675)
* **Dominant Carriers:** Portugal (713,921 embarked), Great Britain (156,062 embarked), and the Netherlands (134,084 embarked).
* **Operating Apparatus:** The Dutch West India Company (chartered 1621) challenged the Portuguese Empire militarily, capturing Elmina (1637) and occupying northeastern Brazil (1630–1654). The Dutch served as the primary carrying agents for the early French and English Caribbean. In England, the Company of Royal Adventurers (1660) and the Royal African Company (1672) organized state-backed human extraction.

#### 3. The British Ascendancy and Deregulation Boom (1676–1750)
* **Dominant Carriers:** Great Britain (1,236,839 embarked), Portugal (1,308,415 embarked), and France (409,518 embarked).
* **Operating Apparatus:** The English Parliament passed the Ten Percent Act of 1698, opening the African trade to independent private merchants. Bristol and Liverpool developed specialized shipyards, provisioning networks, and manufacturing ties. In France, private merchant syndicates in Nantes and Bordeaux supplanted the monopolistic *Compagnie des Indes*.

#### 4. The Peak Atlantic Extraction Engine (1751–1800)
* **Dominant Carriers:** Great Britain (1,580,659 embarked), Portugal/Brazil (1,201,860 embarked), and France (758,979 embarked).
* **Operating Apparatus:** This period recorded the highest throughput in the history of the transatlantic trade, averaging over seventy-eight thousand embarked captives per year. British merchants supplied Jamaica and Saint-Domingue, while Portuguese merchants operated the bilateral South Atlantic trade to Rio de Janeiro and Bahia.

#### 5. The Nineteenth-Century Illegal Trade and Final Suppression (1801–1866)
* **Dominant Carriers:** Portugal/Brazil (2,469,870 embarked) and Spain/Cuba (910,815 embarked).
* **Operating Apparatus:** Great Britain and the United States abolished the maritime slave trade in 1807 and 1808. British naval squadrons patrolled West Africa. The trade shifted south of the equator: Portuguese-Brazilian and Spanish-Cuban syndicates operated fast clipper ships to supply expanding coffee and sugar frontiers in southeastern Brazil and Cuba until final suppression in 1866.

### The North American Demographic Divergence

A fundamental demographic divergence characterizes mainland North America within the Atlantic system:

1. **Volume of Direct Transatlantic Arrivals:** Mainland North America imported 388,776 African captives directly from Africa. This volume constitutes 3.63 percent of the transatlantic total.
2. **Expansion to 1860 Baseline:** By the eve of the American Civil War in eighteen sixty, the enslaved population in the United States reached 3,953,760 persons (US Census 1860). The United States held roughly one-third of all persons of African descent in the Western Hemisphere.
3. **Structural Mechanism:** In the Caribbean and Brazil, sugar production generated severe negative natural population growth. Planters calculated that working enslaved laborers to exhaustion within seven to ten years and purchasing fresh captives was cheaper than sustaining families. High infant mortality, caloric deficits, and tropical diseases required continuous replacement imports (`tex:2122`, `tex:2770`). In mainland North America, tobacco and cotton cultivation permitted natural biological reproduction. The Virginia statute of sixteen sixty-two (*partus sequitur ventrem*, `tex:2446`) made the child inherit the mother's legal status, converting reproductive biology into capital accumulation.

## 4. The Triangle Trade and the South Atlantic Bilateral Critique

### The Classic North Atlantic Triangular Circuit

The traditional "Triangular Trade" describes the mercantilist commercial flows operated by British, French, and Dutch shipping networks in the North Atlantic:

```
[European Metropole]
   │  Outgoing Leg: Guns, Powder, Textiles, Iron Bars, Rum, Brass Manillas
   ▼
[West African Coastal Forts]
   │  Middle Passage: Enslaved Human Cargo (14.5% Aggregate Mortality)
   ▼
[American Plantation Colonies]
   │  Homeward Leg: Raw Sugar, Molasses, Tobacco, Indigo, Rice, Coffee, Cotton
   ▼
[European Metropole]
```

1. **Outward Leg (Europe to Africa):** Merchant vessels carried European manufactured commodities: firearms, gunpowder, textiles (Indian cottons, British woolens, linen), bar iron, brass basins, copper rods, alcohol (rum, brandy), glass beads, and cowrie shells.
2. **Middle Passage (Africa to the Americas):** Slavers loaded human captives into lower decks, packed spoonways in extreme confinement. Mortality averaged 14.52 percent over four centuries. Primary causes of mortality included epidemic dysentery ("the bloody flux"), smallpox, dehydration, ophthalmia, and despair-driven suicide jumps (`tex:2122`).
3. **Homeward Leg (Americas to Europe):** Vessels loaded cash crops harvested on slave plantations: raw unrefined muscovado sugar, molasses, rum, tobacco, cotton, indigo, coffee, rice, and timber for European refineries and factories.

### The Bilateral South Atlantic Critique

The triangular trade concept is an Anglo-centric model that misrepresents the mechanics of the South Atlantic trade, which carried 45.45 percent of all enslaved Africans to the Americas.

```
       [Salvador da Bahia / Rio de Janeiro]
             ▲                       │
             │                       │ Outward Cargo:
             │                       │  • Cachaça (Geribita)
             │ Return Cargo:         │  • Molasses-cured Roll Tobacco
             │  • Enslaved Captives  │  • Manioc Flour
             │  • Ivory, Wax         │  • Re-exported Asian Cottons
             │                       ▼
               [Luanda / Benguela / Cabinda]
```

* **Direct Bilateral Shuttle:** Trade between Brazil and West-Central Africa (Angola) followed a direct bilateral trajectory. Ships cleared Rio de Janeiro, Salvador da Bahia, or Recife, sailed straight east-southeast across the South Atlantic, loaded captives in Luanda, Benguela, or Cabinda, and sailed directly back to Brazil.
* **Brazilian-Produced Exchange Goods:** Brazilian merchants relied on domestically produced commodities for exchange currency:
  * **Cachaça (*geribita*):** High-proof Brazilian sugar cane brandy, produced directly on Brazilian plantations.
  * **Roll Tobacco (*fumo de rolo*):** Third-grade, high-nicotine tobacco leaves soaked in molasses and coiled in leather rolls, produced exclusively in the Recôncavo of Bahia.
  * **Manioc Flour (*farinha de mandioca*):** Used to feed captives on the voyage back.
  * **Re-exported Textiles:** Asian textiles traded directly through Lisbon without English or Dutch intermediaries.
* **Autonomous Brazilian Capital:** The ships were owned, built, captained, and crewed by resident Brazilian colonists (*Luso-Brazilians*). Profits accrued directly to mercantile houses in Rio de Janeiro and Salvador, remaining within the colonial Brazilian economy.
* **Scholarly Authority:** Luis Felipe de Alencastro demonstrated in *O Trato dos Viventes: Formação do Brasil no Atlântico Sul* (São Paulo: Companhia das Letras, 2000) that colonial Brazil and Angola formed a single territorial, political, and economic system joined by maritime wind systems. The institutional reproduction of Brazilian society depended structurally on the military and commercial control of Angola.

## 5. The Americas in 1619: Geopolitical and Demographic Baseline

The year sixteen nineteen represents an essential historical benchmark. It marks the arrival of the first documented enslaved Africans in British North America. At this date, the balance of territorial control and demographic concentration in the Americas belonged to the Iberian powers.

```
                    THE AMERICAS IN 1619
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ 1. BRAZIL (Portuguese Colony, under Iberian Union)          │
│    • 200–350 Sugar Mills (Engenhos)                         │
│    • 100,000–150,000 Enslaved Africans                      │
│    • Primary Focus: Pernambuco and Bahia                    │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2. SPANISH AMERICA (Viceroyalties of New Spain and Peru)    │
│    • Potosí and Zacatecas Silver Conduits                   │
│    • 100,000–150,000 Enslaved Africans                      │
│    • Primary Focus: Mexico City, Lima, Cartagena, Veracruz  │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. THE CARIBBEAN (Spanish Sphere of Influence)              │
│    • Spanish Naval Strongpoints (Havana, Santo Domingo)     │
│    • 20,000–30,000 Enslaved Africans                        │
│    • Northern European Plantation Empires Absent in 1619    │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 4. THE CHESAPEAKE (British Settlement, Virginia Company)    │
│    • Early Tobacco Boom; Total Population: ~1,000–1,500     │
│    • Labor Force: White English Indentured Servants         │
│    • Enslaved Africans: Fewer than 30 Individuals (<2%)     │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 1. Brazil (Portuguese America, Iberian Union)
* **Sovereignty:** Ruled by Philip the Third of Spain (Philip the Second of Portugal) under the Iberian Union.
* **Economic Organization:** Brazil was the undisputed global center of cane sugar production. Between two hundred and three hundred and fifty *engenhos* operated across the coastal captaincies of Pernambuco, Bahia, and Rio de Janeiro.
* **Demographic Concentration:** By sixteen nineteen, Brazilian planters had completed the transition from Indigenous slave labor to African slave labor. Brazil contained between one hundred thousand and one hundred and fifty thousand enslaved Africans, representing the largest concentration of African laborers in the Western Hemisphere.

### 2. Spanish America (Viceroyalties of New Spain and Peru)
* **Sovereignty:** Spanish Habsburg Crown. Encompassed the Viceroyalty of New Spain (Mexico and Central America) and the Viceroyalty of Peru (South America).
* **Economic Organization:** The Spanish imperial economy revolved around silver extraction at Potosí (Upper Peru) and Zacatecas (Mexico), supported by agricultural haciendas and urban craft production. Two designated ports—Cartagena de Indias (New Granada) and Veracruz (New Spain)—held the legal monopoly to receive slave ships under the royal *asiento*.
* **Demographic Concentration:** Spanish America contained an estimated one hundred thousand to one hundred and fifty thousand Africans and Afro-descendants in sixteen nineteen. Portuguese *asientistas* imported over two hundred and fifty thousand captives into Spanish America between fifteen ninety-five and sixteen forty. Enslaved and free Africans populated Mexico City, Puebla, Veracruz, Lima, and the Pacific coast of Peru, where they performed urban crafts, port labor, and agricultural transport.

### 3. The Caribbean in 1619
* **Sovereignty:** Spain claimed the entire Caribbean archipelago under the Papal Bulls of fourteen ninety-three and the Treaty of Tordesillas.
* **Geopolitical Reality:** Spain fortified its primary naval convoy stops: Havana (Cuba), San Juan (Puerto Rico), and Santo Domingo (Hispaniola). The Spanish Crown largely ignored the Lesser Antilles (Barbados, Antigua, Guadeloupe, Martinique), leaving them inhabited by Indigenous Kalinago (Carib) peoples and frequented by Dutch and English smugglers.
* **Absence of Northern European Empires:** In sixteen nineteen, Great Britain and France held zero plantation colonies in the Caribbean. British Barbados was eight years away (settled sixteen twenty-seven); British Jamaica was thirty-six years away (seized sixteen fifty-five); French Saint-Domingue was seventy-eight years away (ceded sixteen ninety-seven).
* **Demographic Concentration:** The Caribbean held a minor fraction of the hemisphere's enslaved African population (roughly twenty to thirty thousand people), engaged primarily in cattle ranching, copper mining, and urban fortifications in Havana and Santo Domingo.

### 4. The Chesapeake (Virginia Colony)
* **Sovereignty:** England, governed by the Virginia Company of London (a joint-stock commercial corporation).
* **Economic Organization:** Settlers cultivated sweet Orinoco tobacco, introduced by John Rolfe in sixteen twelve. Jamestown had survived early famine, and planters were expanding settlements along the James River.
* **Labor Force:** The overwhelming majority of the colonial labor force consisted of English indentured servants working under contractual indentures of four to seven years. Total English settler population numbered between one thousand and fifteen hundred individuals.
* **Arrival of August 1619:** The arrival of "20. and odd Negroes" aboard the *White Lion* at Point Comfort was a peripheral, anomalous event. Africans accounted for less than two percent of the Virginia population. Statutory chattel slavery entered Virginia common law decades later, beginning with the reproductive statute of sixteen sixty-two and culminating in the Slave Codes of seventeen zero-five (`tex:2446`, `tex:3262`).
