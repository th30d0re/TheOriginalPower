# I_book_to_podcast_adaptation

**Question.** What have scholars and producers learned about adapting academic books or research into podcast series? Cover public scholarship podcasts like Hannah McGregor's work and the Amplify Podcast Network, evidence on learning outcomes from educational podcasts, and common failures of academic podcasts.

### 1. Public Scholarship & Scholarly Podcasting Frameworks

Adapting academic research and books into podcasting has evolved from informal science communication into a recognized subfield of digital scholarship. Scholars distinguish between **communicating *about* scholarship** (disseminating existing findings to external audiences) and **operating *within* scholarship** (using audio as a primary, dialogic mode of peer-reviewed knowledge production) [1, 2].

#### A. Hannah McGregor, WLU Press, and the Amplify Podcast Network
* **The *Secret Feminist Agenda* Peer-Review Experiment**:
  * **Mechanism & Structure**: Assistant Professor Hannah McGregor (Simon Fraser University) and Senior Editor Siobhan McMenemy (Wilfrid Laurier University Press) launched the *Scholarly Podcasting in Canada* project [3, 4]. Using McGregor's podcast *Secret Feminist Agenda* as a pilot case, they designed an open, post-season peer-review model where external scholars evaluated full podcast seasons rather than individual pre-recorded text scripts [3-5]. Reviewers evaluated whether a podcast could serve as an equivalent to a university press monograph [5].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: **Qualitative Action Research & Practitioner Case Study** [4–8, 105].
* **Architecture of the Amplify Podcast Network**:
  * **Mechanism & Structure**: Developed by Hannah McGregor and Stacey Copeland with SSHRC funding, the Amplify Podcast Network establishes two operational streams for academic audio [6, 7]:
    1. **Resonate Stream**: Peer-reviewed scholarly podcasts published under the Wilfrid Laurier University Press imprint (e.g., *Secret Feminist Agenda* and Brenna Clarke Gray's *Community of Praxis*) [6, 7].
    2. **Sustain Stream**: A community of practice for ongoing, self-published scholarly podcasts that embody critical pedagogy and open scholarship without undergoing formal press peer review (e.g., *Keeping It 101: A Killjoy's Introduction to Religion*, *Critical and Curious*, and *Wrinkle Radio*) [6, 7].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: **Practitioner Case Study & Network Architecture Guidelines** [111, 118–121, 227–235].
* **Core Theoretical Concepts**:
  * **Layered Knowledge Ecologies**: Tenna Foustad Harbo (2026) conceptualizes scholarly podcasting as part of a "layered knowledge ecology" where audio files, transcripts, references, author bios, DOIs, and accompanying peer-reviewed short essays interact to form a multimodal research output [1, 8, 9].
  * **Audio Academia & Embodied Scholarship**: Mack Hagood (2021) and Amber Sewell (2025) position scholarly podcasts within "audio academia," highlighting that spoken audio allows researchers to **audibly embody their scholarship**, bringing tone, vulnerability, affect, and personhood into knowledge production [10-12].
  * **Queer Feminist Ethics of Care ("No One Is In Trouble")**: McGregor, Burr, McMenemy, Rehak, & Sharman (2025) articulate a queer feminist production ethos centered on team well-being, collaborative dialogue, and relational knowledge generation rather than rigid institutional competition [6, 13, 14].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: **Qualitative Media Analysis & Critical Sound Studies** [1, 10, 11, 13, 15].

### 2. Empirical Evidence on Learning Outcomes from Educational Podcasts

Research on educational podcasting evaluates two distinct interventions: **student-generated creation** (active learning through podcast production) and **student consumption** (listening to instructional audio).

#### A. Student-Generated Podcasts (Podcast Creation as Assessment)
* **Academic Performance & Pass Rates**:
  * **Hopkins (2012)**: Conducted a **controlled quantitative assessment study (ANOVA)** with undergraduate Education Studies students comparing performance across seven course assignments marked under identical criteria.
    * *Findings*: Student achievement on the group podcast creation task was **statistically significantly higher** than on all six traditional assignments (\\(F = 5.378, p < .001\\)) [16, 17]. Content analysis of reflective journals (**qualitative data**) revealed that podcast creation drove deeper intellectual engagement, rigorous evidence selection, and collaborative problem-solving [17, 18].
  * **Hernández-López & Mendoza-Jiménez (2025)**: Surveyed 190 university students across Pedagogy and Tourism degree programs evaluating student-created podcasts.
    * *Findings*: **80% of students** reported that podcast creation significantly enhanced subject comprehension, and **86.2%** reported it directly aided in passing the subject [19, 20]. Statistical testing confirmed that active content creation fostered constructivist learning, peer relationships, and self-regulated study [21, 22].
* **Pedagogy & Law Assessment**:
  * **Janssen & Van Toor (2023 / Utrecht University Guide)**: Evaluated law students creating podcasts as a formal assessment tool (**observational case data**), finding that translating formal legal doctrines into conversational audio forced students to operate at the highest level of Bloom's Taxonomy ("Creation") [23, 24].
* **Evidentiary Basis**: **Controlled Quantitative Assessment Experiments (ANOVA)** [16, 17], **Quantitative Survey Evaluations** [141, 152–157], and **Observational Classroom Studies** [23, 24].

#### B. Student Consumption of Instructional Audio
* **Direct Recall & Cognitive Load Constraints**:
  * **Davis, Lee, Vincent, & Lee (2026)**: A **controlled laboratory experiment** (\\(N = 62\\)) evaluated video podcast instruction under control (narration + aligned visuals), elaborative details, and seductive details conditions.
    * *Findings*: The control condition **statistically significantly outperformed** the seductive details condition in free recall (\\(p = .004, g = 1.03\\)) [25]. Qualitative interviews (**survey/qualitative data**) confirmed that non-essential elaborations overwhelmed working memory, whereas aligned visuals stabilized comprehension [25, 26].
* **Attitudinal & Technology Acceptance Outcomes**:
  * **Ecuadorian Higher Education TAM Study (Frontiers 2025)**: Executed **Structural Equation Modeling (SEM)** on university students.
    * *Findings*: Confirmed that Perceived Usefulness (PU) and Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU) strongly drive student engagement, topic comprehension, and long-term intention to integrate podcasts into academic study routines [27-29].
* **Lectures vs. Mobile Revisions**:
  * Literature syntheses (**Hew 2009; McGarr 2009; Andersen & Dau 2021**) confirm that simply providing audio recordings of lectures serves a **substitutional function** with minimal direct impact on exam scores [30, 31]. However, targeted supplementary audio enhances student motivation, flexibility, and felt connection to faculty [31, 32].
* **Evidentiary Basis**: **Controlled Laboratory Experiments** [25], **Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) Surveys** [27, 28], and **Literature Syntheses / Meta-Analyses** [30, 31].

### 3. Common Failures & Structural Challenges of Academic Podcasts

Literature across higher education, publishing studies, and media production identifies primary structural failure points when adapting academic books and research into audio:

1. **Institutional Misalignment & Promotion Gatekeeping**:
   * **The Failure**: Hiring, tenure, and promotion committees overwhelmingly privilege traditional print monographs and paywalled journal articles over audio scholarship [33, 34]. Consequently, scholars who produce podcasts face an institutional burden—doing podcasting as uncompensated "side hustle" labor on top of standard publishing requirements, leading to high burnout [35-38].
   * **Evidentiary Basis**: **Qualitative Survey & Interview Studies** [78, 124, 251–255] and **Institutional Repository Analyses** [39, 40].
2. **The "Direct Translation" Trap (Ruined by Academia)**:
   * **The Failure**: Attempting to force written academic conventions—such as reading dense prose verbatim, reciting complex citations/formulas, or enforcing multi-month peer-review delays before recording—destroys the medium-specific audio logic of podcasting [41, 42]. When audio scripts sound like stiff lectures, listener retention collapses [41].
   * **Evidentiary Basis**: **Practitioner Experience & Qualitative Media Analysis** [251–255, 296, 302].
3. **Elite Institutional "Vanity Projects"**:
   * **The Failure**: Ian M. Cook (*Scholarly Podcasting: Why, What, How?*, 2023) highlights that university leadership and Rectors frequently misallocate research funding to create top-down "vanity podcasts" that ape commercial mainstream media [43]. These shows lack authentic community engagement, niche focus, or pedagogical structure, resulting in negligible listenership [43, 44].
   * **Evidentiary Basis**: **Qualitative Field Interviews (\\(N = 101\\) Academic Podcasters)** [43, 45].
4. **Information Overload & Seductive Detail Failures**:
   * **The Failure**: Stacking complex jargon, un-moderated rabbit holes, or non-essential "seductive" anecdotes without structural signposting or visual companion support overloads listener working memory [25, 26].
   * **Evidentiary Basis**: **Controlled Laboratory Experiment & Qualitative Interviews** [25, 26].

### 4. Disagreements, Thin Evidence, & Methodological Gaps

1. **What Qualifies as "Scholarly"? (Science Communication vs. Scholarly Contribution)**:
   * **The Conflict**: Tenna Foustad Harbo (2026) and Ian M. Cook (2023) critique the field for vague definitions, pointing out that many creators use "scholarly podcasting" merely as a branding label for basic science communication or promotional interviews [46, 47]. Harbo argues that true scholarly podcasting must operate *within* research practices (incorporating peer review, dialogic exchange, and open methodologies) rather than merely talking *about* research [2, 48].
2. **Peer Review Models: Traditional Blind Review vs. Open Conversational Review**:
   * **The Conflict**: Traditional academic publishing mandates double-blind, asynchronous written peer review. In contrast, the Amplify Podcast Network, WLU Press, and Lori Beckstead's *Open Peer Review Podcast* advocate **open, conversational peer review via podcast** [5, 49, 50]. In this format, reviewers and authors discuss the work openly on-mic, making review labor audible and collaborative [49, 50].
3. **Complete Absence of Controlled Experiments on Scholarly Audio Retention**:
   * **Evidence Gap**: While Cognitive Load Theory provides controlled experimental data on short instructional videos [25], **there are zero controlled or field experiments** evaluating public listener retention, comprehension, or long-term attitude shifts when consuming long-form, multi-episode scholarly podcast adaptations of academic books. Existing scholarship relies entirely on **qualitative case studies, observational platform analytics, action research, and survey data**.
