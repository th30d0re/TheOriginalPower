# E_multilevel_and_analogy

**Question.** Does explaining the same idea at several levels of difficulty, as in the WIRED 5 Levels format, help learning for mixed audiences, or does it cause redundancy or expertise reversal problems for advanced listeners? What does research on analogy, including structure-mapping theory and the Teaching-With-Analogies model, say about designing analogies and stating where they break down?

Grounding instructional design in Cognitive Load Theory (CLT), Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML), and analogical reasoning research provides clear, evidence-based guidance regarding multi-level explanations and analogy design.

---

### Section 1: Explaining Concepts at Multiple Difficulty Levels (e.g., WIRED *5 Levels* Format)

#### 1. Educational Value vs. Cognitive Risks
The WIRED *5 Levels* format (where an expert explains a single concept sequentially to five individuals: a child, a teenager, an undergraduate, a graduate student, and a peer expert) is popular in science communication [1-3]. However, cognitive science research reveals that presenting multi-level explanations in a single continuous stream carries severe instructional trade-offs:

* **For Novice / Intermediate Listeners (Information Overload & Seductive Redundancy)**:
  * *Mechanism*: Progressing into graduate- or expert-level explanations imposes extreme **intrinsic cognitive load** on non-experts because they lack the long-term memory schemas required to process high element interactivity material [4-7].
  * *Evidentiary Basis & Specific Studies*: 
    * **Controlled Experiment & Qualitative Interview Data**: Davis, Lee, Vincent, & Lee (2026) conducted a controlled experiment (\\(N=62\\)) evaluating educational video podcasts [8, 9]. Qualitative interviews revealed that adding advanced elaborations or non-essential details overwhelmed students, causing information overload and confusion rather than deeper understanding [10, 11].
    * **Controlled Experiments**: Mayer, Heiser, & Lonn (2001) [12-14] and Leahy, Chandler, & Sweller (2003) [15, 16] demonstrate that adding unneeded or complex details to instruction increases extraneous load and impairs learning outcomes.

* **For Advanced / Expert Listeners (The Expertise Reversal Effect)**:
  * *Mechanism*: Instructional support and simplified explanations designed for novices become redundant and counter-productive as learner expertise increases [5, 17-19]. When knowledgeable listeners are exposed to elementary explanations or basic analogies, they cannot simply ignore them; they are forced to mentally cross-reference and reconcile the external simplified guidance with their existing, highly integrated internal schemas [5, 19]. This cross-checking wastes working memory capacity and creates extraneous cognitive load [5, 18, 19].
  * *Evidentiary Basis & Specific Studies*:
    * **Controlled Experiments**: Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler, & Sweller (2003) [5, 20, 21], Kalyuga & Sweller (2004) [22], and Oksa, Kalyuga, & Chandler (2010) [22, 23]. Oksa et al. demonstrated that expert readers suffered higher subjective cognitive load and lower performance when provided with simplified explanatory notes alongside Shakespearean text, whereas novices benefited [23].
    * **Controlled Experiments on Segmentation**: Spanjers, Wouters, van Gog, & van Merriënboer (2011) [24, 25] found an expertise reversal effect where low-knowledge learners benefited from segmented instruction, but high-knowledge learners experienced higher mental effort under segmentation compared to continuous presentation.

#### 2. Disagreements & Thin Evidence Areas
* **Practitioner Experience vs. Cognitive Science**:
  * *Practitioner / Media View*: Media producers (e.g., Storythings / *Formats Unpacked*, 2023) highlight observational platform data showing that multi-level videos garner millions of views [26, 27]. They argue the format acts as a structural "scaffolding" or dropping-in mechanism where diverse audiences can find an entry point or appreciate the progression of an idea [2, 28-30]. Popular lore often cites the "Feynman Technique" or Albert Einstein quotes ("If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it") as justification for multi-level simplification [1, 31, 32].
  * *Cognitive Science View*: Cognitive load theorists (Sweller, Kalyuga, Mayer) argue that presenting four or five difficulty levels in a non-adaptive, single continuous stream creates an **Aptitude-by-Treatment Interaction (ATI)** where no single audience segment receives optimal instruction—novices are overloaded by the dissertation-level material, and experts are distracted and slowed down by the elementary-level material [33-36].
* **Thinness of Evidence**: **Direct controlled experimental research evaluating the WIRED *5 Levels* format as a unified audio podcasting technique is nonexistent.** Existing evidence consists of media analytics (observational) and individual cognitive load experiments on isolated expertise levels (controlled laboratory studies).

### Section 2: Designing Analogies (Structure-Mapping & Teaching-With-Analogies)

#### 1. Structure-Mapping Theory (Dedre Gentner)
* **Mechanism**:
  * Structural alignment involves mapping a well-understood **base/analog domain** onto an unfamiliar **target domain** [37-39].
  * **Relational Commonalities over Surface Attributes**: Gentner (1983, 1997, 2003) established that effective analogies map **systems of interconnected relationships** (higher-order causal structures) rather than superficial object features [38, 40-42]. For example, in comparing an electric circuit to a water circuit, what maps are the relational dynamics (voltage/pressure driving current/flow through resistance/narrow pipes), not physical properties like color, shape, or material [37, 38, 41, 43].
  * **The Systematicity Principle**: Relations that belong to an interconnected system of mutually binding relationships are far more likely to be successfully imported into the target domain than isolated surface facts [40, 44, 45].
  * **Analogical Encoding**: Explicitly asking learners to compare two analogous cases fosters the abstraction of a common relational schema, facilitating transfer to new problems [46, 47].
* **Evidentiary Basis & Specific Studies**:
  * **Controlled Experiments**: Gentner (1983) [48], Gentner & Toupin (1986) [49, 50], Gentner & Markman (1997) [51, 52], and Gentner, Loewenstein, & Thompson (2003) [47, 53]. Gentner et al. (2003) showed in a controlled study that management students who explicitly compared two analogical cases were nearly three times more likely to transfer the underlying strategy than those who analyzed the cases separately [47].

#### 2. The Teaching-With-Analogies (TWA) Model (Shawn M. Glynn)
* **Mechanism & The 6-Step Model**:
  * Developed by Shawn M. Glynn (1991, 1998, 2004, 2007) to provide a systematic, six-step framework for introducing instructional analogies in science and multimedia [54-57]:
    1. **Introduce the target concept** [56, 58].
    2. **Review/cue retrieval of the analog concept** [56, 58].
    3. **Identify relevant features** of both target and analog [56, 58].
    4. **Map similarities** between target and analog [56, 58].
    5. **Indicate explicitly where the analogy breaks down (limitations)** [58, 59].
    6. **Draw conclusions** about the target concept [58, 59].
* **Why Stating Breakdown Points Is Essential**:
  * Analogies are "double-edged swords" (Duit et al., 2001) [60]. Because analogies map relationships, unaddressed differences can lead learners to incorrectly map non-analogous base features onto the target concept, creating persistent misconceptions [60-62].
  * *Specific Examples from Research*:
    * **Glynn (2004/2008)** illustrates TWA using Newton's law of gravity (analog) and Coulomb's law of electrical force (target) [59, 63]. The teacher explicitly warns where the analogy breaks down: gravitational force only attracts, whereas electrical force can attract or repel; additionally, the gravitational constant \\(G\\) is tiny, while the electrical constant \\(k\\) is huge [59, 61, 63].
    * **Taber (2013)** (*Upper secondary students' understanding of analogous atomic and solar systems*): Demonstrated that using the solar system as an analog for the atom led students to mistakenly believe electrostatic forces decay or operate identically to gravity or that electrons follow literal rigid orbits, unless teachers explicitly instruct where the physical model breaks down [64-67].
* **Evidentiary Basis & Specific Studies**:
  * **Controlled Experiments & Cognitive Task Analyses**: Glynn & Takahashi (1998) [68] and Paris & Glynn (2004) [69, 70] conducted controlled evaluations showing that elaborate analogies following the TWA model significantly increased conceptual retention and student interest compared to simple, unmapped assertions (e.g., "a cell is like a factory") [69, 70].
  * **Observational & Diagnostic Science Education Studies**: Duit (1991) [71], Duit et al. (2001) [60, 72], Harrison & Treagust (1994) [73], Thiele & Treagust (1994, 1995) [74, 75], and Taber (2013) [64, 76] document how analogies are used in textbooks and classrooms, confirming that omitting breakdown points frequently generates erroneous mental models.

#### 3. Evidence Thinness, Disagreements, & Methodological Caveats
* **The "Analog Familiarity" Assumption**:
  * *Constraint*: Both Gentner's Structure-Mapping Theory and Glynn's TWA model assume the learner thoroughly understands the base/analog concept [40, 77]. 
  * *Evidence Gap / Disagreement*: **Taber (2013) (Observational/Diagnostic Study)** demonstrated empirically that students often hold deep alternative conceptions about the *analog itself* (e.g., holding non-canonical physics beliefs about how gravity works in the solar system) [77, 78]. When the analog is misunderstood, mapping it onto a new target concept compounds misconceptions rather than resolving them [67, 77].
* **Audio-Only Modality Limitations**:
  * *Evidence Gap*: Most TWA and Gentner studies evaluate printed text, visual diagrams, or interactive computer simulations [47, 79-81]. In **audio-only formats**, analogies rely entirely on verbal descriptions without visual support. As **Davis et al. (2026) (Controlled Experiment)** observed, complex verbal elaborations without visual diagrams can overload working memory [10, 51].
