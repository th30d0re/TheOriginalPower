# H_writing_for_the_ear

**Question.** What concrete rules do radio and podcast writers follow when writing for the ear rather than the page? Cover sentence length and structure, attribution placement, numbers, jargon, active voice, reading scripts aloud, script formatting, and directing voice performance. List them as a practical checklist with sources.

Writing for the ear is fundamentally different from writing for the page. A reader can pause, re-read a complex sentence, or scan a chart; an audio listener gets one chance to absorb information in real time as speech flows past [1-3]. 

Below is a practical checklist of concrete rules followed by radio reporters, podcast producers, and audio scriptwriters, along with their evidentiary foundations and areas where sources disagree [4-7].

---

### 1. Sentence Length & Syntactic Structure

* ☐ **Enforce the "One Thought Per Sentence" (OTPS) Rule**: Each spoken sentence must carry only one clear, independent idea. Avoid stacking relative or subordinate clauses ("who," "which," "where") that force listeners to mentally hold multiple concepts before the main point resolves [7-10].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (Journalism University; Corinne Podger / *Mobile Journalism Manual*) [7, 9, 11] and *Controlled Laboratory Experiments* in Cognitive Load Theory showing that complex, nested clauses in transient audio cause rapid working memory overload [12, 13].
* ☐ **Cap Word Counts at 15–20 Words per Sentence**: Keep spoken lines to an average of 15 to 20 words (or a maximum of 17 words under scriptwriting right-margin rules) [8, 10, 11, 14]. 
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (Journalism University; R. Toscan / *Dramarrator*; *Media & Learning Association*) [8, 10, 14, 15].
* ☐ **Apply the Single-Breath Test**: A sentence must not require more than a single natural breath to utter aloud. If a writer runs out of air mid-sentence, the line must be split into two separate declarative statements [4, 15-17].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (AFTRS Media Lab; Wikiversity; NBC News correspondent Ken Dilanian) [4, 16, 17].
* ☐ **Stick to Subject–Verb–Object (SVO) Order**: Place the actor first, followed immediately by the action verb and the object. Keep subjects and verbs physically close together in the script to ensure instant processing [7, 8, 10].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (University of Arkansas Journalism Guide; University of Dayton Style Guide) [1, 7].

---

### 2. Attribution Placement

* ☐ **Always Place Attribution BEFORE the Claim**: In broadcast copy, state who is speaking or making a claim *before* presenting the information (e.g., write *"Police Chief Smith says the investigation is ongoing"* rather than *"'The investigation is ongoing,' said Police Chief Smith"*) [18-20].
  * **Why**: When attribution is placed at the end of a spoken sentence, listeners hear an un-anchored claim and assume the host/reporter is stating it as a personal fact until the very end, causing confusion [18-20].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (Dave Busiek; Jonathan Kern / *NPR Guide*; NBCU Academy) [18, 20, 21].

---

### 3. Handling Numbers & Financial Data

* ☐ **Round Off Large or Exact Figures**: Never recite exact, multi-digit numbers aloud (e.g., turn *"1,987,452 people"* into *"nearly two million people"* or *"close to 20-lakh people"*) [22, 23].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (University of Dayton Style Guide; NBC News) [22, 23].
* ☐ **Limit Statistical Density**: Restrict data to one or two primary figures per story segment. Never stack multiple statistics in a single sentence or paragraph [23, 24].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (NPR Training; Journalism University) [23, 24].
* ☐ **Spell Out Numbers and Symbols for the Speaker**: Write out symbols as full words (e.g., write `"dollars"` instead of `$`, `"percent"` instead of `%`, and `"five-thousand"` or `"15-hundred"`) so the speaker or text-to-speech engine does not stumble [5, 25, 26].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience & Technical Documentation* (AFTRS Media Lab; ElevenLabs Text Normalization Guidelines) [5, 25, 26].
* ☐ **Use Concrete Physical Comparisons**: Translate abstract financial figures or physical measurements into tangible comparisons (e.g., translating a \$50 million budget cut into *"enough money to fund three new schools"*) [24].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (NBCU Academy / Yasmin Vossoughian) [24].

---

### 4. Jargon, Acronyms, & Vocabulary

* ☐ **Enforce the "Phone a Friend" / 8th-Grade Reading Level Standard**: Write technical or academic concepts using plain language that a middle schooler or a friend on a phone call could grasp immediately [25, 27, 28].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (*Writing for Electronic Media*; AFTRS Media Lab) [25, 27].
* ☐ **Expand Acronyms and Abbreviations Fully**: Replace print abbreviations with their full spoken equivalents (e.g., write `"Doctor"` instead of `"Dr."`, `"United States"` instead of `"U.S."`, and `"F-B-I"` with hyphens so each letter is enunciated separately) [25, 29, 30].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience & Technical Guidelines* (New York Film Academy; ElevenLabs Documentation) [29, 30].
* ☐ **Eliminate Print-Specific Language**: Strip out visual or spatial references like `"as mentioned above,"` `"the chart below,"` `"the former,"` or `"the latter."` Audio has no "above" or "below"—only *before* and *after* [31, 32].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (Journalism University) [31, 32].

---

### 5. Active Voice, Present Tense, & Contractions

* ☐ **Mandate the Use of Contractions**: Use `"don't,"` `"it's,"` `"they're,"` and `"we'll"` throughout the script. Uncontracted phrasing sounds stiff, academic, and distant when spoken aloud [4, 33-35].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Controlled Laboratory Experiments* (Mayer's Personalization Principle in Multimedia Learning, showing conversational speech with contractions significantly improves learning outcomes) [34, 36] and *Practitioner Experience* (AFTRS Media Lab; Wikiversity) [4, 37].
* ☐ **Favour Present Tense for Immediacy**: Use present or present-progressive tense to make coverage feel current (e.g., *"The council is meeting right now"* or *"The mayor says..."* rather than *"said..."*) [33, 38, 39].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (KSL News Radio Style Guide; Journalism University) [38, 39].
* ☐ **Use Active Voice to Assign Agency**: Write *"The committee rejected the proposal"* rather than *"The proposal was rejected by the committee."* Active voice reduces cognitive re-processing in working memory [4, 8, 40].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Controlled Laboratory Experiments* (Mayer's Coherence and Modality Principles) [36, 40] and *Practitioner Experience* (AFTRS Media Lab) [4].

---

### 6. Reading Aloud, Script Testing, & Annotations

* ☐ **Conduct Mandatory "Read-Aloud" Audits**: Read every draft aloud at performance pace before recording or airing. Any phrase that causes the writer to stumble, hesitate, or run out of air must be rewritten on the spot [6, 16, 35, 41].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (NBC News correspondent Ken Dilanian; Corinne Podger) [16, 41].
* ☐ **Use "Drafting Out Loud" Brain Dumps**: For complex topics, record an unscripted, informal conversation explaining the core concept to a colleague as if in a coffee shop, then transcribe and edit that natural speech into the script [42-44].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Observational / Qualitative Case Study Data* (*Radiolab* production workflow in *The Open Notebook*) [42, 44].
* ☐ **Annotate Scripts with Performance Cues**: Include explicit script markings in brackets or bold text for vocal emphasis, intentional dramatic pauses, or emotional delivery notes [45, 46].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (Spotify for Creators; ElevenLabs Documentation) [45, 46].

---

### 7. Script Formatting & Scene Layout

* ☐ **Format in Standard Courier 12pt Font**: Maintain standard 12pt Courier formatting (where 1 double-spaced page equates to roughly 60 seconds of spoken audio) [47, 48].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (San Jose State University Writing Center; Keith Crawford / *Little Wonder Radio Plays*) [47, 48].
* ☐ **Use Standard Script Margins & Two-Column Layouts**: Use a 2-column format (left column for SFX/video cues, right column for dialogue) or flush-left character headings in ALL CAPS with indented dialogue [49-51].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience & Qualitative Content Analysis* (StudioBinder; *Dramarrator* study of professional audio drama standards) [49, 50, 52].
* ☐ **Enforce the "Two-Hander" Rule for Scenes**: Limit active dialogue scenes to two primary characters at a time. Introducing three or more simultaneous voices without visual cues creates severe character-identity confusion for listeners [6, 53].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Qualitative Content Analysis & Professional Guidelines* (*Dramarrator* / arXiv 2026; Toscan 2023; Wayland et al. 2020) [6, 52, 53].
* ☐ **Annotate Sound Effects with Precise Functions**: Place sound cues in `[SQUARE BRACKETS]`, specifying type (SFX, AMBIENCE, MUSIC), exact description (e.g., `[SFX: heavy wooden door slamming, echoing]` rather than `[door sound]`), duration, and whether the sound is diegetic (heard by characters) or non-diegetic (heard only by listeners) [50, 51, 54].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Practitioner Experience* (Keith Crawford; *Dramarrator* / arXiv 2026) [50, 51, 54].

---

### 8. Directing Voice Performance (Human & Synthetic AI/TTS)

* ☐ **Human Voices: Direct Physical Traits & Authentic Delivery**: Specify breath patterns, chest vs. head voice resonance, and acoustic positioning (close-mic dry recording for narrator intimacy). Avoid artificial "radio voice" or stiff "performance neutrality" in favour of authentic, conversational self-presentation [55-57].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Observational & Media Studies Data* (Powers 2024; David O. Dowling 2026; Jonathan Menjivar / *Classy*) [57-59].
* ☐ **Synthetic / AI Voices: Apply IPA, Audio Tags, & Text Normalization**:
  * For ElevenLabs v3 models, wrap International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) transcriptions in forward slashes (e.g., `"/ˌbaɪoʊˈkemɪstri/"`) for precise pronunciation [60, 61].
  * Insert emotional/delivery audio direction tags in square brackets (e.g., `[laughing]`, `[sighs]`, `[appalled]`, `[thoughtful]`) immediately before or after the modified dialogue line [62, 63].
  * Avoid SSML `<break>` tags in v3 models; use ellipses (`...`), dashes (`—`), or punctuation to control pauses naturally [64, 65].
  * Pass raw text through an LLM pre-normalization prompt to convert currency, dates, URLs, and mathematical symbols into full spoken words prior to synthesis [12, 26].
  * **Evidentiary Basis**: *Controlled Laboratory Experiments* (Davis et al. 2026 using ElevenLabs synthetic voices) [66, 67] and *Technical Documentation* (ElevenLabs Best Practices 2026) [26, 65, 68].

---

### 9. Disagreements, Evidence Thinness, & Methodological Caveats

1. **Academic Nuance vs. Strict Jargon Elimination**:
   * *The Disagreement*: Broadcast journalism style guides (*Journalism University*; NPR) mandate stripping out all academic and technical jargon [28, 69]. Conversely, academic podcasting researchers (Hannah McGregor / *Amplify Podcast Network*) argue that completely removing domain terminology strips scholarly work of its rigor, demonstrating that academic podcasts succeed by *explaining complex jargon in-stride* through conversational metaphors rather than banning it outright [70, 71].
2. **Full Scripting vs. Bulleted Conversational Outlines**:
   * *The Disagreement*: Instructional production guides (*AFTRS Media Lab*; SJSU Writing Center) recommend full, word-for-word scripts to guarantee precise timing and complete message accuracy [72, 73]. In contrast, professional podcast performance coaches (Tim Wohlberg) and audio documentary producers (*Radiolab*; *You're Wrong About*) warn that reading word-for-word off a page flattens emotional resonance and alters natural speech rhythms. They recommend a hybrid "Oreo" structure: fully script the intro and outro, but use bulleted outlines for the middle body [74-77].
3. **Absence of Controlled Experiments on Copy Editing Rules**:
   * *Evidence Gap*: While Cognitive Load Theory provides **controlled experimental evidence** for the benefits of modality, segmenting, active voice, and contractions (Paivio; Mayer; Sweller) [12, 34, 36], rules regarding **attribution placement, sentence word counts, and broadcast script formatting rest entirely on practitioner experience and newsroom tradition**, with no controlled experimental studies evaluating listener recall across these specific layout variables [5-7].
