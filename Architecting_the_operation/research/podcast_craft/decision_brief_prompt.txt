Write an evidence-based decision brief for a podcast team. Ground every recommendation in the sources and cite them. For each recommendation, label the strength of support as one of: controlled or field experimental evidence, observational or survey evidence, or practitioner experience. Say plainly where the sources disagree or where evidence is thin.

The show: "Architecting the Operation" adapts a roughly 1,150-page academic book, The Original Power, which models systemic racism as an elite extraction algorithm using formal mathematics, circuit analogies, and electrodynamics. Listeners range from people at a very early stage of awareness about structural racism to technically trained readers.

Current practice to evaluate:
1. Fully scripted episodes of 40 to 75 minutes, around 240 to 290 dialogue turns each, one book chapter per episode.
2. Three voices: the author as a host, plus two recurring co-hosts who alternate asking questions and explaining. The voices are synthetic, cloned text-to-speech rather than live recordings.
3. The public series opens on the book's second chapter, a plain-language definition of racism, instead of the mathematical front matter. The opener explicitly tells new listeners they need no prior episodes.
4. The latest episode explains each major idea four times at four depths: elementary school, high school, college, and dissertation level.
5. Equations and formal notation are spoken aloud in the dialogue, with a companion video shot list planned.
6. The house writing style bans formulaic contrast constructions such as "it is not X, it is Y" and prefers direct declarative sentences.

Answer these decisions, each as its own section with a recommendation, the evidence, and the risk if we are wrong:
A. Episode length and segmentation for dense material, including whether 60 to 75 minute episodes should be split.
B. How to assign roles across three voices, including a surrogate learner, and how scripted dialogue avoids sounding staged.
C. How often and how to use recaps, signposting, and repetition in audio-only learning, given transient information and cognitive load research.
D. How to speak equations, notation, and diagrams without visuals, and what the audio must carry on its own when video is absent.
E. Whether the four-level explanation device helps or harms, including expertise reversal and redundancy effects for advanced listeners.
F. Season order and entry-point episodes for a serialized nonfiction show.
G. How to present structural racism to skeptical or identity-threatened listeners, including what the evidence says about backfire, messengers, narrative, and nonjudgmental exchange.
H. Any evidence on audience trust and synthetic or AI-generated voices.
I. Writing-for-the-ear rules we should adopt as a checklist for script review.

Close with a prioritized list of the five changes most likely to improve comprehension and retention, and a list of open questions the sources cannot answer that we would need to test with our own listeners.
