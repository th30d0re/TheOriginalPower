# Relational Mapping, Cognitive Scaffolding, and the Architecture of Expertise: 
A Multilevel Pedagogical Framework

## Relational Architecture and the Sub-Processes of Analogical Reasoning

The human capacity to navigate complex conceptual spaces relies heavily on 
analogical reasoning, which acts as a cognitive bridge between familiar 
knowledge systems and novel, abstract domains . Rather than evaluating 
similarity as a mere accumulation of overlapping physical features or object 
attributes—as postulated by classical theories such as Amos Tversky's contrast 
model—Dedre Gentner's Structure-Mapping Theory demonstrates that analogies are 
fundamentally systems for mapping relational patterns across distinct domains . 
The interpretative rules governing structural alignment operate on the syntactic
properties of mental representations rather than feature salience, allowing 
learners to identify deep relational commonalities independently of the specific
objects in which they are embedded . Within this framework, conceptual 
representations are structured as propositional networks consisting of entities,
attributes, and relations . Attributes are characterized as unary predicates 
describing surface-level properties, such as stating that an object is large, 
while relations are multi-argument predicates establishing structural 
connections, such as asserting that one entity attracts another . 

Analogical reasoning is not a singular, monolithic operation but rather a 
sequence of distinct, highly coordinated cognitive sub-processes . The first 
sub-process is retrieval, wherein a learner accesses a familiar case from 
long-term memory when confronted with a target scenario . The second sub-process
is mapping, which occurs in working memory and involves aligning the 
corresponding structures of the base and target . The third sub-process is 
evaluation, where the learner assesses the factual correctness, systemic 
adaptability, and goal-relevance of the imported inferences . Finally, the 
fourth sub-process is schema abstraction, which allows the learner to extract 
the shared relational template as a generalized, domain-independent mental model
.

The structural alignment phase of mapping requires strict adherence to syntactic
rules, specifically one-to-one correspondence and parallel connectivity . 
One-to-one correspondence dictates that each element in the base maps to at most
one element in the target, preventing cognitive cross-contamination . Parallel 
connectivity ensures that if a relation in the base matches a relation in the 
target, their respective arguments must also match, maintaining structural 
consistency . This process is driven by the systematicity principle, which 
states that human cognition inherently privileges deeply nested systems of 
relations governed by higher-order relations—predicates that take other 
relations as their arguments, such as causal links or logical implications—over 
isolated, lower-order facts . Consequently, highly apt explanatory analogies 
suppress highly salient surface-level details, such as the physical color of 
vessels in a plumbing system, to focus purely on the dynamic relation of fluid 
flow .

| Analytical Parameter | Low-Order Predication | High-Order Predication |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Arity of Predicate**  | Unary (one-argument predicates)  | Multi-argument 
(two or more arguments, or predicates of predicates)  |
| **Representational Focus**  | Surface features, object attributes, and 
physical qualities  | Relational structures, causal dependencies, and logical 
implications  |
| **Mapping Priority**  | Lower priority; suppressed during deep structural 
alignment  | High priority; driven by the systematicity principle  |
| **Example**  | "The nucleus is dense and positively charged"  | "The positive 
nucleus attracts negative electrons, causing them to orbit"  |

The alignment of structural relations is a highly sophisticated process that can
be disrupted by developmental or experiential constraints . In child 
development, structural mapping undergoes a critical shift from a focus on 
surface attributes to relational alignment . A seminal study by Toupin evaluated
four-to-six-year-old children and found that while they succeeded in 
transferring story structures to new characters when similar characters occupied
similar roles—such as mapping a squirrel onto a chipmunk and a trout onto a 
salmon—they completely failed when the match was cross-mapped, meaning similar 
characters occupied conflicting roles, such as mapping a squirrel onto a salmon 
. Conversely, young children demonstrate an intuitive capacity for relational 
reasoning when leveraging spontaneous personification analogies, using their 
highly familiar understanding of human biology as a base to correctly infer 
growth and nutritional dynamics in other living organisms .

For older learners and novices, a persistent cognitive dissociation exists 
between the accuracy of transfer and the ease of memory retrieval . Mary Jo 
Novick's empirical research in 1988 demonstrated that while experts possess 
highly organized schemas that allow them to reject spurious surface-level 
remindings almost instantly, novices struggle with the inert knowledge problem .
Novices possess the required conceptual structures in long-term memory but fail 
to retrieve them when solving novel problems because memory retrieval is heavily
driven by surface similarities, whereas accurate transfer depends strictly on 
the degree of structural match . To bridge this gap, instructional designers 
often utilize experiential grounding and embodied cognition, as argued by 
Niebert and colleagues in 2012, to anchor abstract structure-mappings in 
everyday physical experiences . This positions analogical reasoning as a 
foundational epistemological approach to scientific discovery, placed alongside 
classical deduction and induction within modern science curricula .

## Pedagogical Scaffolding and Typologies of Analogical Design

When introduced to highly abstract, multi-component scientific systems, novice 
learners frequently experience cognitive overwhelm due to their lack of 
structured domain schemas . To systematically support schema construction, Shawn
M. Glynn formalized the Teaching-with-Analogies (TWA) model . The TWA model 
comprises six operational steps designed to construct conceptual bridges :

* First, the instructor introduces the target concept to establish the learning 
parameters .
* Second, the instructor cues the retrieval of a highly familiar analog concept,
activating the student's prior knowledge .
* Third, the instructor identifies the relevant features of both the target and 
the analog to prepare them for alignment .
* Fourth, the instructor maps the structural similarities explicitly, drawing 
clear one-to-one connections between the matching features .
* Fifth, the instructor explicitly indicates where the analogy breaks down to 
prevent incorrect over-extensions .
* Sixth, the instructor draws concrete conclusions about the target concept, 
synthesizing the conceptual gains .

The implementation of the TWA model is highly visible when introducing Coulomb’s
Law of electrical force (the target) through Newton’s Law of Universal 
Gravitation (the analog) . Both laws describe a central-force system governed by
mathematically parallel inverse-square relationships . 

In step three and four, the instructor highlights that the mass ($m$) in 
Newton’s law maps directly to the electric charge ($q$) in Coulomb’s law, while 
the distance ($r$) decays as a square in both denominators . 

In step five, the instructor must deliberately reflect on the breakdown of the 
analogy: gravitational forces are strictly attractive and governed by an 
extremely small constant ($G$), whereas electrostatic forces can be either 
attractive or repulsive, are governed by a massive constant ($k$), and can 
experience shielding effects that have no cosmic counterpart .

To mitigate the rigid application of a single analog, John Clement developed the
"bridging analogies" method . This instructional approach presents a graduated 
sequence of analogs, beginning with an intuitive, physically close match and 
progressively moving through intermediate cases until the abstract target model 
is reached, effectively scaffolding the transition . Additionally, empirical 
research by Coll and colleagues in 2005 shows that incorporating collaborative 
peer discussions during analogical reasoning tasks enhances both cognitive 
alignment and metacognitive monitoring, allowing students to actively negotiate 
and refine their explanatory models .

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Simple Analogy**  | Direct, often singular mapping of obvious attributes  | 
Fast conceptual anchoring for low-complexity targets  | Comparing a cell

| **Enriched Analogy**  | Detailed structural-functional mapping with explicit

| **Embedded Activators**  | Analogies built directly into class activities or

Modeling the lungs with physical balloons and plastic bottles  |

## Theoretical Breakdowns: Empirical Misconceptions in the Bohr-Rutherford 
Analogy

The classical Rutherford-Bohr model of the atom, which analogizes an atomic 
structure to a "tiny solar system," is one of the most widely used analogies in 
science education, yet it represents a prime example of analogical breakdown and
cognitive cross-contamination . While it provides an intuitive entry point for 
secondary students, empirical investigations reveal that without highly 
structured intervention, the analogy produces deep-seated misconceptions that 
actively block the acquisition of modern physical concepts . 

A diagnostic probe administered to upper secondary students aged 15-18 by Keith 
S. Taber revealed massive structural mapping failures . While nearly eighty 
percent of the respondents correctly understood that physical forces decrease 
with distance in both the atomic and astronomical systems, only a small fraction
could correctly identify the nature of the forces . The vast majority identified
gravitational force in the solar system, but only a tiny minority could identify
the electrostatic force binding the atom, frequently assuming gravity or an 
undefined mechanical pull held the atomic constituents in place . 

Furthermore, the study exposed a widespread failure to apply Newton's third law 
of motion to either system . Only about one-third of the students recognized 
that the attractive force exerted by the central body (the sun or the nucleus) 
is identical in magnitude to the force exerted on it by the orbiting body (the 
planet or the electron) . Instead, they fell victim to a force-asymmetry 
misconception, asserting that because the sun and nucleus are massive, they 
exert a one-way pull, and that the minor constituents experience no reciprocal 
attraction or emit no force of their own . 

Additionally, only half of the students expected any physical interactions to 
occur between the minor constituents . In the solar system, planets exert minor 
attractive gravitational forces on one another, while in the atomic system, 
electrons exert highly powerful repulsive electrostatic forces . By failing to 
map these peripheral interactions, students struggle to grasp chemical 
principles such as electron-electron repulsion, effective nuclear charge, and 
the spatial distribution of atomic shells .

| Empirical Student Misconception  |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary Force Type** | Gravitational (mass-dependent)  | Electrostatic 
(charge-dependent)  | **Mismatch** | Students assume gravity or a generic 
physical rope holds the electron in place . |

$F_{\text{nucleus}\rightarrow\text{electron}} =

Three-dimensional probability orbitals  | **Mismatch** | Students visualize

The most severe cognitive risk of the solar system analogy is its fundamental 
incompatibility with quantum mechanics . The planetary model locks the learner 
into visualizing the atom as a classical central-force system where solid 
spherical particles travel along defined, deterministic, planar trajectories . 
Under classical electromagnetic laws, a charged electron accelerating in a 
circular orbit would continuously radiate electromagnetic energy, lose kinetic 
momentum, and spiral into the nucleus within approximately $10^{-11}$ seconds, 
making all matter inherently unstable . 

In quantum mechanics, this classical trajectory is replaced by a probability 
distribution or electron "cloud" governed by the wave function ($\psi$), which 
solves the Schrödinger equation . Electrons do not possess defined coordinates 
and velocities simultaneously, as described by the Heisenberg uncertainty 
principle . Because secondary students are taught the Bohr model as a literal 
reality, they develop rigid, classical mental structures that are highly 
resistant to quantum-mechanical transitions in higher education . 

A highly striking secondary finding in the Taber study was the emergence of 
"reverse mapping error" or cross-contamination . Some students, when analyzing 
the solar system, tentatively proposed that inner planets might shield outer 
planets from the sun's gravity, or that planets would repel each other . When 
questioned, these students revealed they were "thinking about atoms" and 
transferring atomic properties (such as electron shielding and electrostatic 
repulsion) back onto the solar system . This demonstrates that poorly integrated
analogies can lead to bi-directional conceptual decay, corrupting the learner's 
understanding of the familiar base domain .

## Cognitive Load Theory and the Expertise Reversal Effect

The cognitive load generated by any instructional intervention is heavily 
moderated by the learner's level of domain expertise . John Sweller’s Cognitive 
Load Theory assumes that our cognitive architecture is defined by a working 
memory with highly constrained processing limits, and a long-term memory that 
acts as an infinite repository for organized knowledge structures known as 
schemas . Intrinsic cognitive load represents the inherent difficulty of the 
task, dictated by its element interactivity, which is the number of conceptual 
elements that must be processed simultaneously . Extraneous cognitive load 
represents the unnecessary mental processing imposed by the format of the 
instruction, which diverts cognitive resources away from schema construction . 
Germane cognitive load represents the productive working memory capacity 
dedicated to the construction, refinement, and automation of these schemas . 
Prominent educational scientists, including Dylan Wiliam, have underscored the 
foundational nature of this cognitive model, calling it the single most 
important concept for educators to master .

| Instructional Condition | Impact on Novice Learners  | Impact on Expert 
Learners  | Cognitive Load Cause  |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **High Guidance / Worked Examples** | **Highly Beneficial:** Bypasses 
search-based overload; scaffolds schema construction . | **Detrimental / 
Redundant:** Restricts processing speed; creates extraneous load . | Experts 
must dedicate working memory to align redundant instruction with internal 
schemas . |
| **Minimal Guidance / Explored Tasks** | **Detrimental:** Causes working memory
failure due to trial-and-error searching . | **Highly Beneficial:** Facilitates 
retrieval, schema application, and automation . | Novices lack internal 
structures, while experts easily retrieve automated procedural schemas . |
| **Auditory & Visual Integration (Modality)** | **Highly Beneficial:** 
Decreases load by utilizing separate sensory channels . | **Detrimental:** 
Causes redundancy effect if audio repeats visual text . | Processing redundant 
spoken and written text simultaneously overloads sensory buffers . |
| **Imagination Instructions** | **Detrimental:** Novices lack the structural 
details to mentally simulate procedures . | **Highly Beneficial:** Promotes deep
schema integration and retrieval practice . | Novices make errors when guessing,
while experts benefit from active mental schema manipulation . |

The Expertise Reversal Effect represents a critical Aptitude-by-Treatment 
Interaction (ATI) wherein the effectiveness of an instructional design reverses 
as a function of the learner's prior knowledge . For novice learners, highly 
guided instructional techniques—such as worked-out examples, detailed structural
analogies, and integrated diagrams—are essential because they provide the 
external schema structures that the novice's working memory cannot generate . As
the learner's expertise increases, however, these external supports become 
redundant . 

Formally, this interaction can present as either an ordinal or a disordinal 
interaction . In an ordinal interaction, the performance gap between guided and 
unguided conditions narrows as expertise increases, but the relative advantage 
does not flip . In a disordinal interaction, the graphical lines representing 
learning efficiency cross over: novices perform significantly better with high 
guidance, while experts perform significantly better with minimal guidance, 
showing a absolute drop in learning outcomes when forced to process highly 
structured, guided instruction .

This drop occurs because the expert already possesses highly automated internal 
schemas in long-term memory that coordinate the task variables . When presented 
with high-guidance instruction, the expert cannot easily ignore the external 
support . Instead, they must dedicate active working memory capacity to process,
coordinate, and cross-reference the redundant instructional guidance with their 
internal, automated schemas . This forced mental coordination generates massive 
extraneous cognitive load, distracting the expert and actively hindering their 
performance . 

This reversal is clearly seen in several specific cognitive phenomena:

* *The Redundancy and Modality Effects:* For novices, presenting a technical 
diagram accompanied by spoken audio explanations (utilizing the modality effect)
is vastly superior to a diagram with written text because it distributes the 
load across separate visual and auditory channels . For experts, however, these 
audio explanations become highly redundant, creating a redundancy effect that 
forces them to integrate the spoken stream with their pre-existing visual 
schema, leading to cognitive drag .
* *The Imagination Effect:* As prior knowledge increases, imagining a procedure 
or a set of structural relations becomes a highly effective learning activity . 
While low-knowledge learners benefit significantly more from physically studying
concrete, dual-coded visual materials, higher-knowledge learners benefit from 
mentally simulating the steps, which drives deep schema retrieval and 
integration .
* *The Segmentation Effect:* While segmenting animations or processes into 
short, discrete videos is vital for novices to prevent working memory overflow, 
it can disrupt the expert's holistic view of the system, preventing them from 
leveraging their pre-existing schema connections across the entire temporal 
sequence .

To resolve these cognitive conflicts, instructional systems must employ adaptive
fading, systematically stripping away external guidance, worked-out steps, and 
explicit analogies as learners demonstrate increased domain mastery, 
transitioning them toward self-directed curation, exploration, and 
self-evaluation .

## Progressive Multilevel Translation: Cognitive Discursive Framing and the 
Feynman Loop

To communicate highly technical concepts to audiences with varying levels of 
prior knowledge, science communicators and educators must perform progressive 
conceptual translation . The popular WIRED *5 Levels of Difficulty* series 
operationalizes this by challenging an academic expert to explain a highly 
complex topic—such as zero-knowledge proofs, machine learning, or nuclear 
fusion—to five individuals: a primary school child, a teenager, an 
undergraduate, a graduate student, and a professional peer . 

A rigorous analysis of this format reveals that the levels do not merely 
represent linear simplifications of terminology, but rather separate 
conversations characterized by alternative discursive frames . Each level 
represents a qualitative shift in structural complexity, semantic distance, and 
representational codes, carefully aligned with the specific schema capacity of 
each audience .

| WIRED Expert Level | Target Participant  | LTM Schema State  | Cognitive 
Discursive Frame & Analog Design  | Dual Coding & Classroom Practice  |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Level 1** | Primary School Child (Ages 5-6)  | No domain-specific schemas; 
highly reliant on direct sensory perceptions . | **Embodied / Experiential 
Conversation:** Focuses on immediate physical sensations, personification, and 
concrete objects . | Concurrently built live physical models and verbal 
narratives; high-imagery icons . |
| **Level 2** | Teenager (Ages 13-14)  | Simple, general schemas; developing 
abstract logical relations . | **Functional / Macroscopic Analogy:** Focuses on 
macroscopic functional systems and structural comparisons . | Simplified process
flowcharts with direct visual connections; explicit arrows for change . |
| **Level 3** | Undergrad Student (College)  | Intermediate domain schemas; 
capable of processing symbolic notations . | **Theoretical / Relational 
Mapping:** Employs formal structural models with close semantic distance . | 
Symbolic mathematical notations directly aligned with visual bar models and 
graphic organizers . |
| **Level 4** | Graduate Student  | Advanced, highly active schemas; capable of 
processing complex variables . | **Multi-Variable Systems Analysis:** Focuses on
multi-analog comparisons, contrasting cases, and mathematical parameters . | 
Complex process mapping, feedback loop diagrams, and comparative data tables . |
| **Level 5** | Professional Peer (Expert)  | Fully automated, dense, integrated
network of schemas . | **Relational Primitive Dialogue:** Directly modifies 
variables and structural frameworks without standard analogies . | Highly 
abstracted spatial maps, raw data streams, and unguided mathematical equations .
|

This progressive scaffolding directly addresses the "short consideration span" 
problem identified by Ros Atkins in his framework, *The Art of Explanation* . In
modern information ecosystems, audiences do not necessarily suffer from short 
attention spans, but rather from short consideration spans; they make rapid, 
split-second decisions about whether to commit cognitive bandwidth to a topic . 

The WIRED format solves this by explicitly signaling the niche subject and 
difficulty level in the title . This signaling allows curious viewers to 
self-evaluate their entry point and select a level of discursive complexity that
precisely matches their pre-existing schema structures . For instance, a viewer 
might watch Bobby Kasthuri explain the "connectome" to a five-year-old child 
using simple spatial metaphors to capture the basic wiring diagram of the brain,
and then progressively watch the conversations scale in complexity up to the 
graduate student and the connectome entrepreneur .

This multi-level translation is driven by the Feynman Technique, a recursive 
self-directed learning and communication loop designed to isolate conceptual 
gaps and simplify abstract structures . The success of the Feynman Technique 
lies in its four basic operations :

* First, the learner studies the target topic, gathering information and 
recording conceptual relationships .
* Second, the learner attempts to teach the concept to a non-specialist 
audience, or a child, using simple, plain language .
* Third, the learner analyzes the explanation to identify the precise gaps where
their understanding failed or where they relied on technical jargon to mask a 
conceptual vacuum .
* Fourth, the learner simplifies and refines the explanation, returning to the 
source material to resolve those confusion points, incorporating concrete 
analogies and dual-coded representations .

This methodology is fundamentally inductive . Rather than starting with 
abstract, deductive laws and forcing them onto specific examples, the learner 
begins with concrete, highly localized explanations, using simplification and 
analogical alignment to build upward toward a deep, generalized understanding .

To ensure that the translation process does not lead to cognitive overload or 
decorative distraction, instructional designers must ground their materials in 
Allan Paivio's Dual Coding Theory . Paivio’s theory states that information is 
processed along two separate, non-interfering channels: a verbal channel for 
linguistic codes and a nonverbal/visual channel for mental imagery . By 
simultaneously activating both channels, educators can double the capacity of 
working memory, facilitating the construction of integrated mental 
representations . 

However, an evidence review by the Education Endowment Foundation (EEF) warns 
that dual coding is a double-edged sword; if images are purely decorative, they 
act as "seductive details" that compete for limited working memory resources, 
dragging down comprehension . 

To optimize dual coding in the classroom, educators must translate these 
insights into concrete, structural activities :

* *Build Diagrams Live:* Instead of presenting a pre-completed, highly complex 
diagram that causes visual split-attention, educators should draw and label each
relational step on a whiteboard in real-time, matching their verbal narration 
directly to the visual element .
* *Label Directly:* Place written labels and short explanations directly 
adjacent to the visual features they describe . Utilizing a remote key forces 
the learner to perform a visual search, which drains working memory capacity .
* *Turn Prose into Structure:* Convert long, complex paragraphs of text into 
visual process chains, flowcharts, or conceptual maps . Once students grasp the 
visual structure, challenge them to translate that structure back into complete,
subject-specific prose .
* *Complete, Then Create:* For novice learners, avoid demanding independent, 
unguided drawings . Instead, present partially completed diagrams or visual 
organizers, allowing them to fill in the missing relational links before asking 
them to construct an original model from scratch .
* *Retrieve the Visual:* After a designated delay, instruct students to redraw 
the conceptual diagram from memory using a single color, and then correct any 
structural or relational errors using a contrasting color to drive retrieval 
practice .

## Conclusions and Actionable Design Recommendations

Based on the synthesis of cognitive load limits, structural mapping dynamics, 
and expertise-based pedagogy, the following system-level design recommendations 
are presented for instructional designers, curriculum authors, and science 
communicators:

* *Calibrate Analog Selection to the Target's Structural Matrix:* When designing
an analogy, prioritize structural systematicity and high-order relations over 
surface similarities . Ensure that the base domain is thoroughly understood by 
the target audience through pre-evaluation, as an unfamiliar or misunderstood 
base will lead to mapping failures or reverse-contamination .
* *Enforce Explicit Boundary Mapping:* Do not allow learners to explore 
analogies without structure . Every instructional intervention using an analogy 
must explicitly walk through the six operations of the Teaching-with-Analogies 
model, highlighting where the mapping breaks down to prevent long-term 
misconceptions .
* *Implement Adaptive Guidance Fading:* Systematically reduce external 
scaffolds, worked examples, and explicit analogies as learners demonstrate 
competency in a domain . Transition high-knowledge learners toward unguided 
problem-solving, mental simulation (imagination), and self-evaluation to prevent
the cognitive drag associated with the expertise reversal effect .
* *Deploy Strict Informational Dual Coding:* Ensure that all visual 
representations are strictly informational and map exact relations rather than 
serving as decorative illustrations . Use live diagram building, direct 
proximity labeling, and progressive visual completion to minimize 
split-attention and optimize sensory channels .
