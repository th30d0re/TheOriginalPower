# Epistemic Soundscapes: Empirical Realities, Pedagogical Trajectories, and 
Transmedial Adaptations of Scholarly Podcasting

## Empirical Learning Outcomes and Pedagogical Dynamics in Higher Education

The integration of digital audio formats into higher education has transitioned 
from a novel experiment to a recognized pedagogical strategy aligned with modern
learner-centered frameworks . Rather than serving merely as a passive channel 
for content delivery, educational podcasting operates within a dynamic 
environment where audio, text, institutional infrastructure, and student 
engagement interact to reshape knowledge acquisition, retention, and student 
agency . The proliferation of these auditory materials has prompted a systematic
reevaluation of classical lecturing paradigms, moving educational practices 
toward highly flexible, student-centered digital spaces .

To understand the pedagogical impact of this medium, researchers classify 
educational podcasting into three primary operational categories: 
substitutional, supplementary, and creative use . Substitutional use, which 
predominantly involves the distribution of recorded lecture archives via Really 
Simple Syndication (RSS) feeds, provides students with convenience, control, and
the opportunity to replay complex arguments to aid revision and comprehension . 
Early research by Williams and Fardon (2007) as well as Lazzari and Betella 
(2007) confirmed this trend toward distributing structural lecture materials to 
bolster student revision . Supplementary use expands on this by introducing 
additional materials, such as short summary notes, audio study guides, or 
"orientation" inputs provided prior to lectures to scaffold foundational 
concepts . 

A critical shift occurs when institutions move from consumption-based models to 
creative use, where students design and produce their own podcasts . 
Student-generated podcasting yields some of the most robust cognitive and 
affective learning outcomes in modern digital pedagogy . This active creative 
application acts as a catalyst for motivation, engagement, and self-regulated 
learning . By requiring students to synthesize complex scientific or academic 
concepts and translate them into accessible colloquial language, 
student-generated podcasting drives deep cognitive processing and conceptual 
mastery . 

This creative process is substantiated by empirical performance metrics. In a 
controlled evaluation of undergraduate students who produced podcasts alongside 
reflective journals, researchers observed a statistically significant 
improvement in academic achievement . The marks obtained by students in the 
podcast creation assignment were significantly higher than those achieved in six
other traditional course assignments evaluated under comparable rubrics, 
yielding an Analysis of Variance score of:

$$F = 5.378, \quad p < 0.001$$

This finding supports prior evidence demonstrating that structured podcast 
assignments serve as a more effective revision and comprehension tool than 
traditional textbooks . Despite these clear benefits, researchers observe that 
approximately $40\%$ of students in some cohorts admit to using revision 
podcasts as complete replacements for core textbooks rather than as 
supplementary materials, highlighting the ongoing tension between auditory 
summaries and deep reading practices .

The development of active podcasting assignments also facilitates the 
acquisition of core 21st-century skills: creativity, communication, 
collaboration, and critical thinking . When working in groups, students develop 
reflective learning habits, critical inquiry, oral expression, and technical 
self-learning capabilities . These exercises foster social skills and a shared 
community identity within their academic group . 

Pedagogical researchers connect these benefits to modern digital models, 
integrating podcasting into the flipped classroom framework where instructors 
provide pre-class audio content to enable active, synchronous seminar 
discussions . This integration is often paired with gamification strategies and 
mobile technologies, presenting a holistic instructional approach that can be 
combined with other learning activities .

Despite these benefits, the integration of educational podcasting introduces 
structural challenges. Stöhr et al. (2020) highlight that podcasting can 
contribute to performance polarization, where self-directed and tech-literate 
students excel while others experience difficulty adapting to digital workflows 
. Concerns persist regarding declines in physical lecture attendance and the 
substantial increase in instructor workload required to facilitate and grade 
technical audio projects . Gribbins (2007) also indicates a discrepancy in 
student perceptions: while students value podcasting as a learning aid, they 
remain skeptical of its actual efficacy in raising quantitative examination 
scores . 

To systematically analyze how students accept and sustain the use of podcasts, 
researchers apply the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), extending the framework
to incorporate user experience and student engagement . Under this model, the 
behavioral intention to adopt educational podcasting is determined by perceived 
usefulness and perceived ease of use . In emerging and non-Western educational 
contexts, such as Latin America, these variables are heavily influenced by the 
digital divide and socioeconomic inequalities . This dynamic means that low 
technical requirements and mobile compatibility are critical to ensuring 
equitable access and high-quality user experiences .

## Practices of Successful Academic Podcasters and Public Scholarship

Beyond formal classroom environments, podcasting has emerged as a transformative
medium for scholarly communication, knowledge mobilization, and public 
scholarship . This shift requires a conceptual distinction between communicating
about scholarship and operating within scholarship . While the former focuses on
translating completed research to external publics, the latter positions 
academic podcasting as a primary, dialogic, and multimodal research output in 
its own right . 

As a form of social scholarship, academic podcasting leverages digital platforms
and RSS distribution to expand the traditional boundaries of research discovery,
integration, and application . This practice provides a collaborative, 
relational, and embodied framework that directly challenges traditional 
print-centric models of knowledge creation . It bypasses traditional 
institutional gatekeepers and fosters transdisciplinary collaborations that are 
highly accessible to marginalized groups and communities outside of academia .

The central barrier to the widespread adoption of scholarly podcasting lies in 
academic validation. Traditional hiring, tenure, and promotion committees 
maintain a conservative bias toward standard print artifacts, such as 
peer-reviewed journal articles and university press monographs . To counter 
this, scholarly podcasters use alternative metrics of public impact, including 
global download distributions, geographic reach, the Listen Score, and the 
Podcast Success Index, to demonstrate the quantitative societal impact of their 
research .

To analyze the actual practices of successful practitioners, researchers turn to
the work of academic podcasters like Hannah McGregor, Lori Beckstead, and Ian M.
Cook, who examine the practical, technical, and conceptual configurations of 
academic audio . Cook’s *Scholarly Podcasting: Why, What, How* (Routledge, 2022)
analyzes interviews with 101 podcasting academics, highlighting that podcasting 
serves as a direct counter-response to traditional scholarship trapped behind 
expensive journal paywalls, written in opaque language, and framed in a 
standoffish tone . Successful practitioners leverage these unique auditory 
qualities to bring their personhood and identities directly into the expression 
of their research, showing that sound-based scholarship does not require a 
written text to establish academic rigor .

These practices are evident in Hannah McGregor's extensive audio portfolio. Her 
projects include *Secret Feminist Agenda* (a weekly feminist cultural podcast), 
*Material Girls* (a pop-culture series utilizing critical theory to unpack the 
contemporary zeitgeist), *Witch, Please* (a feminist discussion of the Harry 
Potter series), and *The SpokenWeb Podcast* (a collaborative project exploring 
audio literary archives) . 

Through Witch, Please Productions, McGregor, Marcelle Kosman, and Hannah Rehak 
developed a collaborative "care-based ethos" guided by the motto "no one is in 
trouble" . This collaborative framework directly challenges the individualistic,
competitive, and masculinist norms of traditional academic production, 
emphasizing instead relational safety, collective care, and mutual support .

Furthermore, practitioners identify four major genres within academic podcasting
that demonstrate how the medium supports collaborative knowledge production:

*   **Conversation:** Informal dialogues between academic peers that mirror 
collegial debates, allowing ideas to emerge through verbal exchange and raw 
reflection .
*   **Interview:** Structured dialogues that bring external experts, 
practitioners, or community members into active conversation with academic 
researchers .
*   **Debate:** Synchronous, critical engagements that address controversial 
topics, making multiple perspectives visible to the public .
*   **Crafted Audio:** High-production soundworks, audio documentaries, and 
experimental sound forms that use sound design, music, and narration to 
construct complex scholarly arguments .

These genres make the collaborative nature of research visible, allowing 
academic podcasters to explore alternative forms of scholarly authority . This 
auditory space also supports queer, feminist, and anti-racist knowledge 
production by centering the physical body, oral storytelling, and emotional 
resonance—epistemic elements that are systematically excluded from standard 
print publications .

## Decentering Peer Review and Infrastructure in Audio Publishing

A key milestone in academic validation is the development of rigorous 
peer-review models designed specifically for digital soundworks . The Scholarly 
Podcasting in Canada project, led by Hannah McGregor and Siobhan McMenemy 
(Senior Editor at Wilfrid Laurier University Press), used McGregor’s *Secret 
Feminist Agenda* as a formal test case to design a peer-review model that fits 
the medium . 

Rather than adopting the standard double-blind review process—which McGregor 
critiques as a gatekeeping mechanism designed to keep people out rather than 
welcome them in—the team implemented an open, public peer-review process . Under
this framework, the names, professional affiliations, and comments of the 
reviewers were published openly on the university press website alongside the 
creator’s responses .

To evaluate *Secret Feminist Agenda*, McMenemy and the reviewers had to address 
two core questions:

1.  Could a podcast series of this kind be considered scholarly? 
2.  Could reviewers find reasonable equivalencies between a podcast series of 
this type and a traditional academic book publication under a university press 
imprint? 

The peer-review process evolved over three distinct seasons . In Season 1, the 
review focused primarily on form, design, and alternative scholarly 
communication, which prompted practical changes in the show’s format and 
technical execution . In Season 2, the evaluation transitioned to content, 
assessing the podcast's specific contributions to critical feminist theory . For
Season 3, the press designed a collaborative roundtable review model to evaluate
the complete narrative arc of the project . This open framework transformed peer
review into a generous and collaborative community process that helps scholars 
improve their work .

This model has been integrated into the SSHRC-funded Amplify Podcast Network, a 
partnership between Simon Fraser University's Publishing program, the Digital 
Humanities Innovation Lab (DHIL), Wilfrid Laurier University Press, the Wilfrid 
Laurier University Library, and The Documentary Media . The network aims to 
develop sustainable frameworks for producing, reviewing, and preserving 
scholarly podcasts, organizing its catalog into two distinct streams . 

The *Resonate* stream publishes academic podcasts under the formal peer-reviewed
imprint of WLUP, whereas the *Sustain* stream features scholarly podcasts 
selected by an editorial board based on critical pedagogy and open scholarship 
without undergoing formal press review . These initiatives ensure that digital 
audio is treated as a primary scholarly contribution rather than just a public 
relations tool .

This alternative validation framework also supports recorded open peer-review 
podcasts, as demonstrated by the pilot episode of *The LibParlor Podcast* . In 
this model, an academic host facilitates a critical conversation between a 
reviewer and a researcher before a manuscript is submitted for publication . 
This process records the active development of ideas, making the collective 
shaping of research visible to the public . 

To help scholars present these outputs on their academic CVs, *A Guide to 
Academic Podcasting* provides clear strategies: submitting podcasts to 
peer-reviewed digital humanities journals such as *Kairos* or *Reviews in 
Digital Humanities*, or submitting episodes with a short abstract and 
bibliography to regional journals like *BC Studies* . Furthermore, scholars are 
encouraged to collect syllabus adoptions and partner with university libraries 
to index audio files in institutional repositories, ensuring long-term digital 
preservation .

## From Bound Pages to Auditory Lab: Translating the Scholarly Monograph

The academic publishing industry is experiencing a transition driven by 
institutional demands for open access (OA) and global reach . This shift is 
changing traditional monographs, edited volumes, and theses, encouraging 
publishers to move beyond physical books toward interactive, dynamic digital 
formats . 

This movement is supported by historical analyses of the academic book, such as 
Michael Jubb's report, *Academic Books and Their Future*, which shows how the 
definition of a book has expanded over time to include audio and electronic 
formats . This expansion frees research from the constraints of bound, physical 
pages and allows scholars to explore alternative modes of scholarly expression .

This evolution is accelerated by the citation advantages of open-access 
monographs, which show a clear citation increase of:

$$\Delta C \approx 30\%$$

compared to closed-access volumes . While traditional publishers often limit 
content length, structure, and editorial direction to meet commercial market 
constraints, open-access formats offer authors greater academic freedom and 
direct control over their research .

The systemic shift toward open-access publishing models is driven by major 
global policy changes and funding agency priorities, including Plan S and the 
UNESCO open science guidelines . To navigate these changes, publishers are 
adopting hybrid revenue models, institutional agreements, and library 
consortiums to cover production costs while keeping scholarly monographs freely 
available under Creative Commons licenses . 

This is illustrated by initiatives like Knowledge Unlatched (now part of Wiley),
which uses library funding to shift closed monographs to open access, and 
university presses like the University of Michigan Press, which are adapting 
their digital files and EPUB standards to support both global accessibility and 
open-access distribution .

Adapting a written monograph into a narrative podcast series represents a 
process of transmedial translation . This translation requires the decomposition
of expository written text into dialogic scripts, where soundscapes, voice, and 
music are used to convey complex theoretical arguments . This acoustic shift 
highlights the body and personal identity of the scholar, presenting a contrast 
to the print-only monograph . 

This contrast is illustrated by comparing Helen Merrick’s print-only history 
monograph, *The Secret Feminist Cabal: A Cultural History of Science Fiction 
Feminisms* (2009), with Hannah McGregor’s born-digital, open-reviewed podcast, 
*Secret Feminist Agenda* . While both works offer radical feminist critiques of 
cultural production, the podcast uses sound-based, open-access distribution to 
engage public communities directly, bypassing the traditional print gatekeepers 
that govern academic monographs .

This transmedial translation is demonstrated by the *Qualitative Research 
Methods for Everyone* podcast, which adapted a methodology textbook by Karen 
O'Reilly . Produced in partnership with Bristol University Press, the series 
converts written textbook chapters into conversational dialogues between the 
author, practitioners, and commissioning editor Paul Stevens . 

Rather than summarizing the text, the podcast addresses challenging methodology 
concepts (such as ontology, epistemology, reflexivity, and iterative induction) 
by anchoring them to "toolkit boxes" from the book, and contextualizing them 
within modern challenges like generative artificial intelligence . This 
adaptation makes the conceptual tools of qualitative methodology highly 
accessible to audiences both inside and outside academia .

Similarly, the Center for New Critical Politics and Governance at Aarhus 
University developed *Planetary Choices* (beginning with the season *Mapping the
Planetary*), conceptualized as an "audible research lab" to communicate research
as an active, ongoing process . Under a formal agreement with the journal 
*Global Perspectives* (University of California Press), the center plans, 
records, and produces the podcast for publication on the journal’s digital 
platform . 

Because the journal does not have a dedicated podcast category, episodes are 
published under the existing "Discussion" classification as standard, indexable 
academic publications with DOIs, references, transcripts, keywords, and author 
bios . To support this audio series, the center publishes shorter, 2000-word 
peer-reviewed companion essays as "Research Articles," creating a multimodal 
publishing model that integrates audio and text into traditional journal 
indexing systems .

## Strategic Recommendations for Institutional Integration

To fully realize the potential of academic podcasting as a valid scholarly 
medium, universities, scholarly presses, and researchers must implement systemic
reforms that support digital audio workflows:

### For University Administration and Tenure Committees
*   Formally amend departmental tenure, promotion, and hiring criteria to 
recognize peer-reviewed, born-digital scholarly podcasts as equivalent to 
traditional book chapters or journal articles .
*   Create evaluation rubrics that integrate qualitative peer assessments of 
podcast content with quantitative altmetrics, such as global geographic reach, 
syllabus adoptions, and specialized indices like the Podcast Success Index .

### For Academic Libraries and Information Professionals
*   Develop dedicated library podcasting studios equipped with high-quality 
recording equipment, and provide technical training in audio editing, 
transcription, and production .
*   Standardize metadata schemas for academic podcasts, assign persistent 
Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs), and index scholarly audio outputs within 
institutional repositories to prevent digital obsolescence .

### For Scholarly Presses and Journals
*   Establish dedicated, peer-reviewed podcast streams modeled on the Amplify 
Podcast Network, integrating open, collaborative, and conversational peer-review
structures .
*   Provide opportunities to publish short, peer-reviewed essay companions 
alongside podcast episodes, creating layered publications that are highly 
citable within traditional scholarly ecosystems .

### For Individual Scholars and Researchers
*   Adapt traditional printed works into narrative, audio-first adaptations that
use sound design and structured interviews to expand the accessibility and 
citation reach of the research .
*   Participate in collaborative, interdisciplinary networks to share resources,
standard audio templates, and peer-evaluation strategies, helping to build a 
more equitable, open, and diverse academic ecosystem .
