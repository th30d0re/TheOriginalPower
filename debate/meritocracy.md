# Is America a Meritocracy

**Resolution as it will be phrased:** "Does hard work determine success?" / "Is America a meritocracy?"

---

## Never argue

"The system is rigged."

It sounds like an excuse, and it invites every anecdote they have about someone who made
it. You cannot beat anecdotes with resentment.

**Fix:** argue with a **single statistic that is definitionally about opportunity** —
intergenerational elasticity. It measures how much your parents predict you. In a perfect
meritocracy it would be zero. That is not a metaphor, that is what the number means.

---

## Hook (10s, no stats)

> "If this were a meritocracy, the correlation between your income and your parents' income
> would be near zero. It's about one-half."

---

## Core (20s)

> "That number is called intergenerational elasticity, and it's the actual measurement of
> opportunity — how much of your outcome your parents already determined. In the US it's
> around 0.4 to 0.5. In Denmark it's about 0.15. So the country that talks about mobility
> the most has less of it than most of Western Europe. A kid born in the bottom fifth has
> roughly a seven percent chance of reaching the top fifth. That's not a meritocracy.
> That's a slow-motion hereditary system with a good marketing department."

---

## Numbers

Say these as approximations. ⚠ = verify before going live.

| Claim | Figure |
|---|---|
| Intergenerational income elasticity, US ⚠ | ~0.4–0.5 |
| Same, Denmark / Nordics ⚠ | ~0.15 |
| Bottom-quintile → top-quintile odds (Chetty) ⚠ | ~7.5% |
| Harvard legacy admit advantage ⚠ | ~5–6x base rate |
| Harvard white admits who are ALDC (athlete/legacy/dean's/faculty) ⚠ | ~43% |
| Elite colleges: students from top 1% vs bottom 60% (Chetty) | more from the top 1% |

That last one is the single most quotable statistic in this entire debate. Have it ready.

---

## Counters

**1. "Anyone can make it in America."**
> "Anyone can win the lottery. We don't call it a retirement strategy."

**2. "You're confusing equality of outcome with equality of opportunity."**
> "I'm measuring opportunity. Elasticity *is* the opportunity metric — it's the degree to
> which your birth predicts your life. If you want opportunity measured, that's the number."

**3. "Hard work does matter."**
> "It does, at the margin, within your band. Hard work reliably moves you inside a
> quintile. It very rarely moves you across two. We've confused the variance with the mean."

**4. "Immigrants come with nothing and succeed."**
> "Selection effect. Immigration policy filters for credentials and for the specific kind
> of person willing to cross an ocean. That's a screened sample, not a control group."

**5. "So you want to punish success?"**
> "I want to be able to detect it. Right now inherited advantage and earned achievement
> produce the same résumé, which means we can't reward merit even when we want to."

That answer is worth memorizing. It reframes you as *pro*-merit, which is a position they
cannot attack, and it makes their side the one defending unearned advantage.

**6. "Legacy admissions are private business."**
> "Harvard holds a fifty-billion-dollar tax-exempt endowment. The public is paying for the
> exemption. That makes it our business."

---

## Close

> "Meritocracy is a genuinely excellent system. I'm in favor of it. My complaint is that we
> haven't tried it — we've been running inheritance and calling it merit, which lets the
> people at the top believe they earned a position that was assigned to them."

---

## If you only have 10 seconds

> "Meritocracy would mean your parents' income doesn't predict yours. It predicts about
> half of it. We're describing an aristocracy and grading it on vibes."
