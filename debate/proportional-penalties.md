# Proportional Penalties / Day-Fines

**Resolutions this covers:** "Fines should scale with income." / "Are corporate penalties
adequate?" / "Is everyone equal under the law?"

Also the single best *supporting* card in the deck. The parking analogy deploys inside the
wealth-cap, capitalism, and policing debates whenever fairness or deterrence comes up.

---

## Never argue

"The rich get away with everything."

It reads as resentment and invites a hundred counterexamples of prosecuted rich people.

**Fix:** argue **deterrence mechanics**. A penalty's whole purpose is to alter behavior. If
the amount doesn't alter behavior, the penalty failed at its own stated job. This is a
functional argument, not a moral one, and it is nearly unanswerable.

---

## Hook (10s, no stats)

> "If you're wealthy enough, a parking ticket isn't a fine. It's the price of parking."

---

## Core (20s) — the parking analogy

> "If I park illegally, I'm risking money I can't absorb, so the rule actually changes what
> I do. Someone wealthy enough pays the same ticket and feels nothing — so for them it
> isn't a law, it's a purchase. Same rule, same dollar amount, two completely different
> things. A fine that doesn't hinder you didn't punish you. It sold you a permit. And
> hindrance is the entire point of a penalty, so equal treatment under the law actually
> requires unequal amounts."

**Then scale it, same breath:**
> "Now run it at corporate scale. The FTC hit Meta with a record five billion dollar fine
> in 2019 and the stock went *up* that day, because investors had priced in worse. A
> penalty that raises your market cap isn't a penalty. It's a receipt."

---

## The pre-emption they never see coming

Deploy the instant they say "unworkable," "unprecedented," or "how would you even do that."

> "Finland has done this since 1921. It's called a day-fine — calculated from daily
> disposable income. A Nokia director paid a hundred and sixteen thousand euros for
> speeding. Switzerland, Germany, Denmark, Sweden, and Austria run versions of it. This
> isn't a thought experiment. It's a century-old policy in functioning democracies."

Most opponents assume this is your invention. Finding out it is settled law elsewhere
takes their entire "impractical" branch off the table at once.

## Counters

**1. "Equal under the law means the same fine for everyone."**
> "Same number isn't same treatment. If the point of a fine is deterrence, and the amount
> deters one person and not another, then you've applied the law to one of them. Equality
> of hindrance is the only equality that does anything."

**2. "That's punishing people for being successful."**
> "It's charging them the same amount of deterrence. A fine is priced in pain, not dollars.
> We just got lazy and started quoting the dollars."

**3. "It'd be impossible to administer — how do you know someone's income?"**
> "The IRS knows. Finland's system runs off the tax return and has for a century. We
> already means-test food assistance down to the dollar for poor people. The administrative
> capacity exists — we just only point it downward."

That last sentence is the sharpest thing on this card. Use it.

**4. "Corporations do face serious consequences."**
> "Name one where the penalty exceeded the profit from the conduct. When the fine is
> smaller than the revenue it protects, you haven't deterred the behavior — you've licensed
> it and taken a cut."

**5. "This would let poor people break the law for free."**
> "Day-fines have a floor. And a poor person already faces a penalty that scales — they
> just pay it in jail time and lost jobs instead of money. Two-thirds of the jail
> population is pretrial because they couldn't post bail. Scaling already exists. It only
> runs in one direction."

**6. "Deterrence isn't the only purpose — there's retribution and denunciation."**
> "All three fail the same way. If the amount is invisible to the payer, it doesn't deter,
> doesn't punish, and doesn't denounce. It processes."

## Close

> "A rule that binds one person and not another isn't a law. It's a toll on the people who
> can feel it. We can keep calling that equal treatment, but the person writing the check
> without looking at it already knows better."
