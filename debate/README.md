# Debate Deck

Compressed, live-fire distillations of the framework for short-turn debate formats
(20–30 seconds per turn). Each card is built to be *spoken*, not read.

The manuscript proves the framework. These cards deploy it against someone who has
never heard of it and will not grant you a single definition.

## The method

Seven rules. They are what make the cards work, and they generalize to any topic
you have not written a card for yet.

### 1. Argue the rate, not the pile

Static claims about quantity ("they have too much, so we have too little") invite the
single most rehearsed counter in every debate: *"it's not zero-sum."* Your opponent has
that line loaded before you open your mouth.

Rate claims are immune. "The pie grows at 2%, the claim on it grows at 6%" concedes
their entire counter as your own premise, and still wins. Find the two rates. Compare them.

### 2. Argue mechanism, not motive

You cannot prove what anyone intended. You can read a statute. Every card anchors on a
document with a date — the 1705 Virginia codes, the FHA underwriting manual, the
13th Amendment's exception clause. A document cannot be called a feeling, an opinion,
or a chip on your shoulder.

This is also what makes the argument *systemic* rather than personal. The machine runs
whether or not anyone at the controls means harm. Say that out loud — it defuses the
listener's defensiveness before it forms.

### 3. Concede their best counter in your first sentence

Whatever they were about to say, say it first and price it in. It costs you nothing and
it deletes their prepared turn. "The economy grows — agreed. Now compare the rates."

### 4. One intuition reset before any number

People cannot feel large numbers, and a stat delivered into an uncalibrated audience does
nothing. Reset the scale first, in one line, with no data:

> A million seconds is 11 days. A billion seconds is 31 years.

Ten seconds, no statistics, and every number you say afterward lands differently.

### 5. Three numbers, stated as approximations

More than three and you are lecturing. Say "about" and "roughly" — a precision attack
cannot land on a figure you never claimed precisely. Verify anything marked ⚠ before
you go live; those move.

### 6. Never defend the maximal position

They will try to attach you to the most extreme version of your side — abolish police,
seize wealth, socialism. Refuse the attachment and narrow to the mechanism:

> "I'm not arguing for that. I'm asking why four companies own 80% of the market."

Let them defend the indefensible half of *their* coalition. That is where the ground is soft.

### 7. Close by naming the machine, not the villain

The framework's whole content is that the outcome is structural. Land there. It is the
most persuasive move available because it stops sounding like an accusation and starts
sounding like a diagnosis — and it is the one thing your opponent has no script for.

## Universal counters

Moves that work on any topic. Memorize these; they are worth more than any single card.

**"That's just correlation."**
> "It's a statute. You can read it."

**"You're being divisive."**
> "I'm describing a division that was legislated. I didn't write it."

**"Group X succeeded, so the barrier can't be real."**
> "That's a selection effect. The 1965 Immigration Act selected for advanced degrees.
> You're comparing a population selected for professional credentials to one selected
> for chattel labor and calling it a control group."

**"That would be too expensive."**
> "We found $700 billion over one weekend in 2008 and $2 trillion in 2020.
> Cost is a priority statement, not a constraint."

**"Things are better than they were."**
> "Rate, not level. The level improved. Ask what the rate is doing."

**"Correlation with race is really about class."**
> "Then explain why a Black and a white household at the *same income* hold roughly
> 6x different wealth. Income is what you earn. Wealth is what was legislated to your
> grandparents."

**"That's government overreach / freedom."** — the anarchy frame
> "Do you want a democratic republic or anarchy? The worst case of anarchy isn't chaos in
> the street. It's a small group holding the supplies, writing rules they aren't bound by.
> We already have that. It's anarchy at the top and law at the bottom."

**"Everyone's equal under the law."** — the parking analogy
> "If the fine doesn't hinder you, you weren't fined. You bought a permit. Equal treatment
> requires unequal amounts, which is why Finland has calculated fines from income since 1921."

**Demand the control group.**
> "Crime fell in Canada on the same curve, without the incarceration. When the medicine
> and the placebo produce the same result, it isn't the medicine."

## Building a trap opener

The opposition case on any of these motions is finite. Map it, then design an opening that
forces them to overcommit to the one vector you can disprove. Six steps:

**1. Enumerate their vectors.** On most economic motions there are three. On the wealth cap
they are property, incentive/risk, and capital flight — and the practice AI played all
three in a single opening paragraph.

**2. Find the one resting on a factual claim.** Value claims ("property is sacred") can only
be disputed. Factual claims ("billionaires bear the risk") can be *disproven*. Always bait
toward the factual one. That's where receipts beat rhetoric.

**3. Concede the principle out loud.** "Risk deserves reward — I'm not disputing that."
This costs nothing, because you're about to show they don't satisfy it. It also makes you
the reasonable party, which is persuasiveness points you get for free.

**4. Ask the question that forces them onto the factual ground.** "So who actually carried
the risk?" They now have to assert something checkable.

**5. Predict their answer out loud.** "My opponent is about to tell you they carried it."
Two outcomes and you win both: they walk in and the judge watches it happen, or they dodge
their strongest argument to avoid looking predictable.

**6. Attach a demand they can't meet.** "Name one who lost everything." Specific, sounds
answerable, isn't. Their best available example usually proves your point instead.

**Then, every following turn: open by naming what they didn't answer.** Judges score
dropped arguments, and a trap that gets dodged still pays.

**Build the bridge into the trap.** An opener that is only a trap gets marked for having no
positive case. "Whoever carries the risk owns the upside" is bait and remedy in one clause.

## Quarantine — lines that lose

Arguments that feel strong and cost you rounds. Each one has a replacement that makes the
same point without the vulnerability.

**"The economy is a closed system / capital is finite / thermodynamics."**
The fixed-pie claim. It is economically false in the form stated, and *"the economy isn't
zero-sum"* is the most rehearsed counter that exists. Any opponent with an econ course
ends the clash here.
→ **Replace with the rate argument.** The pie grows at 2%, the claim on it grows at 6%.
Concedes their counter and still wins.

**Framework vocabulary of any kind.** Systemic diode, kinetic energy, apex node, drift
velocity, min-max function, Δmax = 0, extraction architecture.
Using the framework's own terms to prove the framework is circular, and the audience hears
jargon. The framework is the instrument you see with, not the thing you say.
→ **Replace with an example, every time.** Instead of "systemic diode," say *"public money
funds the research, public money pays for the bailout, and the profit stops in the middle."*
Give three examples and the listener derives the equation on their own — and then they own
it, which is worth more than being told.

**"Enact the cap globally, across all jurisdictions simultaneously."**
Makes your position require world government. Hands them "utopian and impossible" for free,
and you never needed it.
→ **Replace with citizenship-based taxation + immobile assets.** The US taxes citizenship,
not residence; leaving means renouncing and there's already an exit tax; the land, customers,
and corporate charter can't board a plane. Beats capital flight without a single treaty.

**"Government control and billionaire control are functionally equivalent."**
Self-defeating. Your entire remedy runs through government. If the two are equivalent, your
own proposal is pointless — and you've conceded their "bureaucrats are just as bad" line.
→ **Replace with capture.** *"The problem isn't government versus private. It's that one
bought the other."*

**"Forced liquidation" / "seize" / "redistribute their assets."**
Contradicts the definitional move that wins this debate. You win by establishing the cap is
a marginal rate on future growth, then Gemini-style phrasing hands confiscation back to them.
→ **Replace with:** marginal rate, paid in shares, on growth, phased.

**"Literal treason" / foreign-lobby control of Congress.**
Three separate problems. Treason has a constitutional definition — levying war, or aiding
*enemies* — so an allied nation cannot satisfy it and a judge will mark the error. The
characterization of the underlying bill is almost certainly overstated. And it drags a
winnable economic debate into a charged foreign-policy fight where you spend every turn on
defense.
→ **Replace with domestic, unimpeachable capture evidence:** Citizens United, lobbying
spend, the revolving door, and the hedged Gilens & Page finding. Same conclusion, no exposure.

**"Gilens and Page scientifically proved public opinion has no impact."**
Overstated and contested (Enns; Branham/Soroka/Wlezien).
→ **Replace with the surviving core:** *"where the preferences of the affluent and the
average citizen diverge, the affluent win."* Critics concede this version.

## The spine (do not say this out loud)

This is the framework underneath every card. It is the thing you are *never* going to
have time to explain, which is why each card smuggles one piece of it in plain language.

Elites face a coalition threat from below. The population that could remove them is
always larger than they are. Race is the technology that partitions that population:
it hands a subordinate group a *psychological wage* — status without material gain — in
exchange for policing the group below it. The buffer class defends a system that
extracts from it, because the alternative is losing the only thing it was given.

Portugal, 1450s: invention. Virginia, 1676–1705: the American application, written down
after Black and white laborers rebelled together and nearly won.

Every card is one instance of that machine. Never name the machine in a 20-second turn.
Point at one gear and let them watch it turn.

## Practice protocol

1. **Hook cold.** Say the opener with no runway. If it needs setup, it is not an opener.
2. **Time yourself.** Core block must land under 25 seconds spoken. Cut until it does.
3. **Rehearse the counters, not the case.** You will spend 80% of a debate responding.
   The four counters on each card are the actual deliverable; the core is just the entry.
4. **Log what actually got thrown at you.** If a counter is not on the card, add it.
   The cards should get better every debate, not sit still.
