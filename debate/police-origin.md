# The Police Were Built to Uphold the Status Quo, Not to Protect

**Motion:** "The police was not made to protect the people, but to uphold the status-quo by force."

Your best available motion. It is a claim about **founding purpose and legal duty** — both
documented, neither requiring you to defend a single policy position. You never have to say
"abolish," "defund," or anything about any individual officer.

---

## Never argue

- **Abolition or defunding.** Not the motion. If you get attached to it you spend the round
  explaining what you don't mean.
- **"Cops are bad people."** Motive claims are unprovable and make you the aggressor.
- **Present-day effectiveness alone.** They'll counter with a rescue story and win the
  exchange on feeling.

**Fix:** hold the frame on **what the institution was built to do and what it is legally
obligated to do.** Both have paper. Neither depends on anyone's character.

---

## 🪤 THE TRAP OPENER ⭐

> Let me concede something up front. Individual officers save lives. People run toward
> danger in that uniform and that's real — I'm not disputing it for a second.
>
> So the question isn't the people. It's what the institution was built to do. South
> Carolina wrote slave patrols into law in 1704 — and made patrol duty compulsory for white
> men who owned no slaves at all. Poor men conscripted to police the enslaved, on behalf of
> the people who owned them.
>
> And the obligation question is already settled. The Supreme Court has ruled police have no
> constitutional duty to protect any individual. Warren v. DC. Castle Rock v. Gonzales.
>
> My opponent is about to tell you protection is the whole point. Ask them why the courts
> disagree.

**Why this works:** the concession disarms the emotional defense before it's deployed, then
you produce a legal fact they cannot dispute, only reframe. And it forces them onto duty —
the ground where you have case law and they have sentiment.

---

## THE KILL SHOT — no legal duty to protect

Most opponents have never heard this and cannot answer it live.

- **Warren v. District of Columbia (1981)** — three women, fourteen hours of assault after
  repeated 911 calls. Court: no general duty to provide police protection to any particular
  individual.
- **Castle Rock v. Gonzales (2005, SCOTUS)** — Jessica Gonzales had a restraining order.
  Police repeatedly declined to enforce it. Her three daughters were murdered. The Supreme
  Court ruled she had no constitutional right to enforcement.
- **DeShaney v. Winnebago County (1989, SCOTUS)** — no affirmative duty to protect an
  individual from private violence.

> "If protection were the purpose of the institution, those rulings would be impossible.
> You cannot have a duty you cannot be held to. The courts didn't invent that — they
> described what the institution actually is."

**"To Protect and to Serve" is a motto, not a mandate.** LAPD adopted it in 1955 as an
academy slogan contest winner. It has never been a legal obligation anywhere.

---

## ⭐ ORIGINS — SLAVE PATROLS

The strongest material on this motion. It gets discounted only when stated vaguely, so
state it with statutes and dates, and pre-empt the counter before they reach it.

**South Carolina, 1704.** The colony created formal slave patrols by law. Their statutory
function: recover human property, break up gatherings, search quarters, enforce pass
requirements. Georgia and Virginia followed with their own patrol acts.

**Patrol duty was compulsory — and that's the whole framework in one statute.**
> "Here's the part people miss. Patrol duty was a legal obligation for white men, including
> men who owned no slaves at all. You could be fined for not showing up. So the colony
> conscripted poor whites — people with nothing — to police the enslaved on behalf of the
> people who owned the property. That's not a race system with a class side effect. That's
> a class system using race as the enforcement mechanism."

That is the buffer class in plain English. Never say "buffer class." Say the statute.

**The continuity is the argument, not the origin.** Patrols didn't disappear at emancipation:
- Charleston's paid, uniformed City Guard grew directly out of patrol and watch function —
  one of the earliest full-time municipal forces in the country ⚠
- After 1865 the same function was rebuilt as the Black Codes: vagrancy laws that made
  unemployment a crime, enforced by the same men
- Which fed convict leasing, through the Thirteenth Amendment's own exception clause —
  "except as a punishment for crime"
- The word *patrol* itself carries over. So does the beat, the watch, the badge

> "The institution wasn't abolished and replaced. It was renamed and kept its function."

---

## 🛡 PRE-EMPT THE COUNTER — "that's only the South"

**This is the attack that gets the slave-patrol argument discounted.** Do not wait for it.
Concede the geography and take the function — which is stronger, because it's your framework.

> "You'll hear that this is only Southern history, that Northern police came from the Boston
> watch and Robert Peel. Fine — look at what the North actually built. Boston 1838, New York
> 1845, Philadelphia 1854. Not created during a crime wave. Created during strikes, labor
> unrest, and immigrant 'disorder,' because property owners wanted a standing force.
>
> South: control the labor force by race. North: control the labor force by class. Same
> function, two targets. That's not two separate histories. That's one institution with two
> local implementations."

**Pennsylvania's Coal and Iron Police, 1865** — the cleanest single fact in the deck:
> "The state commissioned private police, paid directly by the coal and railroad companies,
> to break strikes. Public authority, corporate payroll, written into law. You don't have to
> interpret that. That's the resolution as a statute."

## Counters

**1. "Maybe that's the origin, but that's not what they do now."** *(their best argument)*
> "Then explain the case law. Warren and Castle Rock are 1981 and 2005, not 1704. The
> courts confirmed the absence of a duty to protect in living memory. And the function
> shows in the numbers — about half of murders go unsolved, burglary clearance is around
> thirteen percent. If protection is the job, the institution isn't organized to do it."

**2. "Modern policing began with Robert Peel in 1829 — policing by consent."** *(the trap
inside their trap)*
> "Peel built the Royal Irish Constabulary first. The London model was adapted from a
> colonial pacification force. He didn't invent policing by consent and then export it —
> he ran it on a subject population and then brought it home."

**3. "Ask a victim. Police stop murders and rapes."**
> "Individual officers absolutely save people. I said that first. The question is what the
> institution is obligated to do — and when Jessica Gonzales asked exactly that, the
> Supreme Court told her the answer was nothing."

**4. "Without police there's chaos."**
> "I haven't proposed removing them. I've made a claim about what they were built for.
> Notice they'd rather debate abolition than the founding, because the founding is written
> down."

**5. "This is just anti-cop rhetoric."**
> "It's a claim about an institution, supported by statutes and Supreme Court rulings. If
> describing what a law says is rhetoric, take it up with the Court."

**6. "Uvalde was one failure."**
> "Three hundred seventy-six officers. Seventy-seven minutes. And no one was legally
> required to go in — that's the point. Not cowardice, structure."

**7. "Police protect property, and property protection helps everyone."**
> "Now we agree. Protecting property *is* upholding the status quo. That's the motion.
> Whether it's good is a separate debate — you've just conceded what the institution is for."

Counter 7 is the one to fish for. Many opponents wander into it, and it concedes the
resolution outright.
