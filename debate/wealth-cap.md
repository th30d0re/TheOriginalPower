# Personal Wealth Should Be Legally Capped

**Resolution as phrased:** "Personal wealth should be legally capped to prevent the existence of billionaires."

Policy resolution, not a moral one. That is an advantage — "they earned it" is nearly
irrelevant here, and the opposition has to defend *unlimited accumulation as a principle*,
which is a much worse position than it sounds like in the abstract.

---

## Never argue

Do not defend a **hard ceiling that seizes existing wealth**. If the audience pictures
federal agents zeroing out a bank account, you lose on mechanics — valuation, liquidity,
capital flight — and never get back to substance.

Also: do not lead with morality. The resolution is about a policy instrument. Argue the
instrument.

**Fix:** define the cap as a **marginal rate**, in your first sentence, before they can
define it as confiscation.

---

## The definitional land-grab (say this FIRST)

> "Let's be precise about what a cap is, because the other side needs you to picture
> something else. Nobody takes anything anyone already has. A cap is a marginal rate — the
> same instrument we've used since 1913. Above a threshold, the rate on further
> accumulation approaches one hundred percent, satisfied in shares rather than cash, phased
> over years. The top bracket was ninety percent in the 1950s. That was a cap. We called it
> a bracket."

This one move pre-empts three of their five best counters. Never skip it.

---

## Hook (10s, no stats)

> "We tell ourselves there's already a ceiling on private power — that nobody can own an
> army or an arsenal. Prigozhin was a billionaire who owned an army and marched it on
> Moscow. There's a private military company under contract in Haiti right now. Every
> warhead in the US arsenal is built and maintained by private shareholders. The ceiling
> isn't real. It's a story we tell ourselves about a ceiling."

**Do not use the older version of this line** — *"you can't own a nuclear weapon or a
private army"*. It is false, and an informed opponent will end the exchange with a single
counterexample. Wealth has already converted into every form of power we assumed was
fenced off. That fact makes the cap argument urgent instead of theoretical, so run toward
it rather than around it.

**Precision that keeps you out of trouble:** warheads are federally owned — NNSA holds
title and launch authority is not for sale. Say *built and maintained by private
shareholders*, which is true and lands just as hard. Claim ownership of the warheads and
you lose an exchange you should win.

---

## Core (20s)

> "Wealth compounds at five to seven percent. The economy grows at two. So concentration
> isn't a moral failure by anybody — it's the arithmetic running unattended. A cap is
> just a governor on an engine that has no upper limit built in. Every other form of power
> in a democracy has a ceiling: term limits, antitrust, one person one vote. We put
> ceilings on power specifically because we don't trust the people who have it, including
> the good ones. Financial power got left out."

---

## THE WINNING MOVE — make them state their number

Deploy early, and return to it every time you're on defense.

> "You have a cap. You just haven't said it out loud. Is it a trillion? Ten trillion? If a
> single person held ninety percent of all wealth in this country, would you object? Then
> you're not against caps — you're negotiating the number. So am I. We're having the exact
> same debate and you're pretending you're not in it."

Both exits are losses for them: name a number and they concede the principle, refuse and
they're defending infinity in front of an audience. Get this on the record in your first
or second turn so you can call back to it.

---

## The anarchy frame (use when they invoke freedom or government overreach)

This is the strongest turn on the card. It offers them a choice nobody wants to answer
wrong, then collects.

> "Do you want to live in a democratic republic, or in anarchy? Because when people picture
> anarchy they picture chaos in the street. The actual worst case is much quieter: a small
> group ends up holding the supplies, and they write the rules everyone else lives under
> while not being bound by them. Now tell me how that differs from a country where Gilens
> and Page found average citizens have near-zero independent influence on policy, and where
> a record-breaking fine makes the stock price go up. We already have anarchy. It's just
> anarchy at the top and law at the bottom."

**The compression:** *anarchy at the top, law at the bottom.*

The underlying logic is rigorous, and worth understanding so you can defend it: a rule that
binds one party and not the other is not a law. Law requires universality. When a penalty
is a real constraint on one person and a rounding error on another, only one of them is
actually governed. The wealthy occupy a permanent state of exception — they live under no
binding rules while funding the rules everyone else lives under.

**Gilens & Page precision:** 2014, *Perspectives on Politics*, 1,779 policy issues from
1981–2002. Finding: economic elites and organized business interests have substantial
independent influence; average citizens' preferences show near-zero, statistically
non-significant independent impact. ⚠ It has been contested (Enns; Branham/Soroka/Wlezien)
on the grounds that rich and average preferences correlate too closely to separate cleanly.
Know this before you cite it. The defensible core survives the critique: **where the
preferences diverge, the affluent win.** Say it that way and the objection has nowhere to go.

---

## The parking analogy (deploy on fairness, deterrence, or "equal under the law")

Best single device in the deck. Small, relatable, scales perfectly.

> "If I park illegally, I'm risking a ticket I can't absorb, so the rule actually changes
> what I do. If you're wealthy enough, that ticket is just the price of parking. Same rule,
> same dollar amount — and it's a law for me and a purchase for you. A fine that doesn't
> hinder you didn't punish you. It sold you a permit."

Then scale it in the same breath:

> "Now run that at corporate scale. The FTC hit Meta with a record five billion dollar fine
> in 2019 and the stock went *up* that day. Not down. Up — because investors had priced in
> worse. A penalty that raises your market cap isn't a penalty. It's a receipt."

**Then close the loop back to the resolution:**
> "So equal treatment under the law requires *unequal* dollar amounts. Otherwise the
> hindrance isn't equal, and hindrance is the entire point of a penalty."

**The pre-emption they never see coming — this is already law:**
> "Finland has done exactly this since 1921. It's called a day-fine, calculated from daily
> disposable income. A Nokia director paid a hundred and sixteen thousand euros for
> speeding. Switzerland, Germany, Denmark, and Sweden all run versions of it. So when you
> say it's unworkable — it's been working in a functioning democracy for a century."

## Opponent decision tree — the Holy Trinity

The opposition case on this motion is finite. It has three moves and it will play one or
more of them. Identify the vector in their first sentence and run the matching trap.
Confirmed live: the AI judge opened with all three in a single paragraph.

**VECTOR 1 — PROPERTY ("theft," "violates property rights," "punishing success")**
→ Run: *property rights are statutory* (slavery precedent) **+** *the objection proves too
much* (all taxation is a taking). See counter 3b. This pairing won the clash.

**VECTOR 2 — INCENTIVE ("destroys innovation," "they take the risk")**
→ Run: *socialized risk* — DARPA at the front, bailouts at the back, tollbooth in the
middle. Full card at [socialized-risk.md](socialized-risk.md). Then the 90% bracket and
the moon landing.

**VECTOR 3 — FLIGHT ("they'll leave and take it with them")**
→ Run: *the empty suitcase* **+** *citizenship taxation*. Then the hostage inversion.

**The tell that wins the round:** Vectors 2 and 3 contradict each other, and opponents
almost always play both. If the incentive is *erased*, investment stops. If capital
*shifts* abroad, the incentive is intact. Watch for it and name it the moment it appears —
this is what won the live round.

---

## The empty suitcase (Vector 3)

> "They'll leave — and take what with them? You can't pack the American consumer base into
> a suitcase. You can't take the roads, the courts, the educated workforce, or the public
> infrastructure their company was built on. All they can move is the legal right to
> extract from us."

**Then invert it into a hostage frame:**
> "And notice their strongest argument is a threat. 'Let us accumulate without limit or
> we'll crash your economy.' That isn't a defense of the free market. If one person leaving
> can take down a country, you've just described exactly why nobody should be allowed to
> get that big."

---

## If you speak first — the bait

Open by attacking the *origin* of the wealth, which reliably provokes the risk defense.
Then spring it.

**Turn 1 (bait):**
> "A cap is an accounting correction. Nobody builds a billion dollars alone — it's built on
> public roads, publicly educated workers, and publicly funded research. The internet came
> out of DARPA. But my opponent is going to tell you billionaires earned every cent by
> taking on enormous personal risk."

**Turn 2 (kill), after they take it:**
> "They just told you billionaires deserve it because they carry the risk. The public
> funded the research at the start and paid for the bailouts at the end. Our risk, their
> reward. They ran the tollbooth in the middle."

Naming their next move out loud in Turn 1 is the whole trick — they either walk into it or
abandon their strongest argument to avoid looking predictable. Both outcomes are yours.

## Counters

**1. "How would you even value illiquid assets? Private companies, art, founder stakes?"**
> "We already do. Every single day. It's called the estate tax, and it values exactly those
> assets every time someone dies. Property tax does it annually on every house in America.
> The valuation problem is solved — we just currently only solve it for dead people and
> homeowners."

This is the objection they think is fatal. It isn't, and knowing that is worth the debate.

**2. "They'd just leave. Capital flight."**
> "Two answers. The US taxes citizenship, not residence — one of about two countries that
> does — so leaving requires renouncing, and we already have an exit tax for that. And the
> assets can't move. The land is here, the customers are here, the company is chartered
> here. A person can board a plane. A market cannot."

**3. "It would destroy companies — you'd force founders to liquidate."**
> "Paid in shares, not cash. No sale, no liquidation. The stake converts over time into
> non-voting stock or a public trust. Founder keeps operational control, loses infinite
> accumulation. Those are separable things — we just never bothered to separate them."

**3b. "A cap violates property rights."** — *tested live, won the clash*
> "Property rights are defined by statute, and this country has redefined them before.
> There was a time when human beings were private property in America. We removed that
> property and did not compensate the owners. So 'forced removal of private property' isn't
> a line we've never crossed — it's a line we crossed for the best reason we ever had. The
> question was never whether property can be redefined. It's what for."

Pair it immediately with the reductio and the two land as one blow:

> "And 'forced removal of private property' describes all taxation anyway. Income, property,
> estate — forty percent at death. If that phrase settles it, you're not arguing against a
> cap, you're arguing against taxes."

Together these neutralize the entire property-rights branch. The first denies that property
rights are natural; the second shows the objection proves too much. Opponents have no
prepared answer to the sequence because it attacks the premise and the inference at once.

**4. "It's unconstitutional. It's a taking."**
> "The income tax needed the Sixteenth Amendment. So this is an amendment-level question,
> which makes it hard, not wrong. 'We'd have to change the law' is a description of every
> law we've ever passed."

**5. "Who decides the number? It's arbitrary."**
> "Every threshold in the tax code is arbitrary in exactly that sense. The bracket
> boundaries, the estate exemption, the age you can drink. Arbitrary-but-specified beats
> unlimited-and-unexamined. And you have a number too — you just won't say it."

**6. "It kills the incentive to build."**
> "At a billion dollars? Name the founder who stopped at nine hundred million because the
> tenth wasn't worth it. Past a certain point it isn't compensation anymore, it's a score.
> And we had ninety percent brackets during the interstate system, the moon landing, and
> the transistor."

**7. "It's envy. You just resent successful people."**
> "I'm arguing about a rate of compounding. You're arguing about my feelings. One of those
> is checkable."

**8. "The money's not sitting in a vault, it's invested — it's already working."**
> "Then a cap paid in shares costs the economy nothing, by your own argument. The capital
> stays deployed. Only the ownership claim changes hands. You've just conceded the mechanism."

That last one is a trap worth setting deliberately — their "it's already invested" defense
is incompatible with their "it would wreck the economy" defense. Make them pick.

**9. "The government owns the warheads, not the companies."**
> "Title, yes. Operation, no. Los Alamos is run by a private LLC. Pantex and Y-12 are run
> by a Bechtel-led consortium. Northrop builds the ICBM, Lockheed builds the sub-launched
> missile, General Dynamics builds the boat. Shareholders don't hold the launch key. They
> hold the revenue, and they hold the lobbyists who set the budget that decides how many
> get built."

**10. "Wagner is Russia. That doesn't happen here."**
> "Contractors outnumbered US troops in Afghanistan. Blackwater killed civilians in Nisour
> Square and the operator rebranded twice and kept the contracts. Erik Prince has a private
> force under contract in Haiti right now. The difference between us and Russia is
> paperwork, not the existence of the army."

**11. "So you think defense contractors start wars?"**
> "I'm not claiming motive, I'm describing an interest. They hold a direct financial stake
> in conflict, they spend roughly a hundred and thirty million a year lobbying the body
> that authorizes conflict, and senior Pentagon officials retire onto their boards. You
> don't need anyone to be a villain for that arrangement to produce more war than a country
> would otherwise choose."

**12. "Anarchy means no government. We have an enormous government."**
> "We have a government that binds you. It doesn't bind them. A rule that only applies
> downward isn't a law, it's a toll. The size of the state tells you nothing about who it
> actually governs."

**13. "The law already treats everyone equally — same fine, same rule."**
> "Same dollar amount isn't same treatment. It's a law for me and a purchase for you. If
> the penalty doesn't hinder you, you weren't penalized — you were billed. Equal treatment
> requires unequal amounts, which is exactly why Finland calculates fines from income and
> has since 1921."

**14. "Corporations do get punished. There are massive fines."**
> "Meta's record five billion in 2019 moved the stock up. Fines are a line item priced
> against revenue. When the penalty is smaller than the profit from the conduct, you
> haven't deterred the conduct — you've licensed it and taken a cut."

Counter 11 is the one to be disciplined about. *They cause wars* is unprovable in twenty
seconds and makes you sound conspiratorial. *They hold a structural interest in war and
fund the apparatus that decides on war* is checkable, and it is worse for them.

---

## Close

> "We put term limits on presidents. We break up monopolies. We give each person one vote.
> Wealth walks straight past every one of them — it buys the army, the arsenal contract,
> and the legislator who votes on both. Capping wealth isn't adding a new limit to the
> pile. It's the only thing that makes the limits we already passed mean anything."

The logic here matters: wealth is the master key. Every other ceiling in the system is
real on paper and unlocked by money in practice. That is why this is the load-bearing one.

## If you get assigned CON

Your best ground, in order — do not bother with "envy" or "socialism," they're weak:

1. **Valuation of illiquid assets is genuinely hard** at the margin (pre-IPO, crypto, IP).
   Press for a specific mechanism and attack the implementation, never the goal.
2. **Concede the diagnosis, contest the instrument.** "Concentration is real. A cap is the
   worst available tool for it." Then offer alternatives — close the stepped-up basis
   loophole, tax unrealized gains at borrowing, restore antitrust. You look reasonable and
   they have to defend the clumsiest possible version.
3. **The 100% marginal rate creates a behavioral cliff** — activity relocates or restructures
   rather than stopping. Ask what happens at $999M.
4. **Concentration of the enforcement power itself.** A state that can zero a fortune can
   zero a dissident's fortune. This is the strongest CON argument that isn't about money,
   and most PRO debaters have no prepared answer.

Note that #2 wins more debates than #1, #3, and #4 combined. Conceding the problem buys
enormous credibility and moves the fight onto ground the PRO side rarely prepares.
