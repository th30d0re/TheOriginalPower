# Socialized Risk — "Our Risk, Their Reward"

**Covers:** "Billionaires take the risk" / "They earned it" / "Entrepreneurs deserve the reward"

The strongest offensive line in the deck. It works because it doesn't dispute that risk
deserves reward — it accepts the premise and then shows the risk isn't theirs. Tested live
and won the clash.

---

## Never argue

Do not argue that risk doesn't deserve reward. You lose the room instantly and you don't
need to. **Concede the principle entirely, then show they don't meet it.**

> "Risk absolutely deserves reward. That's why I'm asking who's actually carrying it."

---

## Hook (10s, no stats)

> "Our risk, their reward. The public funds the research at the start and eats the losses
> at the end. They just run the tollbooth in the middle."

That's the whole argument in two sentences. The tollbooth image is the keeper.

---

## Core (20s)

> "My opponent says billionaires deserve uncapped wealth because they carry the financial
> danger. Check both ends of the pipeline. The public funded DARPA, which built the
> internet. Public money funded GPS, touchscreens, and the research behind most of what's
> in your phone. Then when things collapse, the public bails them out. Taxpayers took the
> risk at the front and the risk at the back. The billionaire took the middle. That's not
> entrepreneurship, that's a toll."

---

## The four pillars (deploy whichever fits)

**1. DARPA / the public R&D engine**
> "The internet is a DARPA project. GPS is military. Touchscreens, voice recognition, the
> core technologies in the iPhone — publicly funded research, then privatized at the point
> of sale."

**2. The 2008 bailout**
> "When the banks crashed the economy, the public rescued them."

⚠ **Anticipate the escape:** they will say *"the bailouts were repaid with interest."*
Mostly true, and it doesn't save them:
> "The repayment isn't the subsidy — the guarantee is. Being known as too big to fail
> lowers your borrowing costs permanently, every day, whether or not you ever draw on it.
> That's a public insurance policy nobody voted for and they never paid a premium on."

**3. "Too big to fail" → "too big to exist"** — best compression in this card
> "If an entity is so large that taxpayers are *forced* to rescue it, the free market is
> already dead and you're describing a state-backed monopoly. Too big to fail means too big
> to exist. That's not my argument against them — it's their own words."

**4. Occupancy quotas — the modern receipt**
> "Private prison contracts have contained occupancy guarantees — the state pays for the
> beds whether or not they're filled. The public absorbs the downside of a private business
> by contract. Same structure, present tense."

⚠ Precision on #4: the clause obligates the *government to pay*. Say that. Do **not** say
police arrest people to hit quotas — that's a causal claim you can't carry, and it turns a
documented contract term into a conspiracy allegation you'll spend two turns defending.
The defensible version is stronger anyway: *a fiscal interest in incarceration, written into
a contract.*

## Counters

**1. "The bailouts were paid back."**
> "The guarantee is the subsidy, not the loan. Knowing you'll be rescued lowers your cost
> of capital every single day. They never paid a premium on that insurance and we never
> voted to write it."

**2. "They were too big to fail — we had no choice."**
> "Agreed, and that's my argument. If one firm's failure can take down the country, it's
> already too powerful to be private. Too big to fail means too big to exist."

**3. "Musk built real companies. Subsidies are small relative to what he created."**
> "Then he should welcome the accounting. I'm not saying he built nothing. I'm saying the
> public carried the downside and got none of the equity. Take the risk, take the shares."

**4. "Government research is wasteful. The private sector commercializes it."**
> "Commercialization is real work and deserves real reward. It doesn't deserve *all* of it.
> The public took the loss on every DARPA project that failed and got zero on the one that
> became the internet. That's a portfolio where we eat every loss and book no gains."

**5. "Everyone benefits from those companies existing."**
> "Benefit isn't the question. Ownership is. We paid the premiums. They kept the policy."

---

## Close

> "I'm not arguing risk shouldn't be rewarded. I'm arguing it should be rewarded to whoever
> actually carried it. Right now the public funds the research, insures the failure, and
> receives none of the upside. Fix the ledger and the billionaire disappears on his own."
